# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

## 1. How can one consciousness contain different letters?

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · KASHMIR SERIES P. 187

**इदं स्थानम् इदं करणम् अयं वर्ण इति कथङ्कारं विभागः?**

*Idaṃ sthānam idaṃ karaṇam ayaṃ varṇa iti kathaṅkāraṃ vibhāgaḥ?*

"How, then, can there be differentiation into 'this is a place of articulation,' 'this is an instrument,' and 'this is a phoneme'?"

But don't miss the preceding qualification:

**बोधैकघनतानिर्विशेषतायाम्**

*Bodhaikaghanatānirviśeṣatāyām.*

In the undifferentiated condition of consciousness as one compact fullness.

That's the actual difficulty.

If consciousness is ultimately one, whence its apparent plurality?

Let's dismantle the question.

### Bodha - बोध

Awareness, knowing, consciousness.

From √**बुध् - budh**, to awaken or understand.

Compare:

**बुद्ध - buddha**

Awakened.

**बोधन - bodhana**

Making known, awakening.

**बोध - bodha**

Knowing or awareness.

### Ekaghana - एकघन

**एक - eka**

One.

**घन - ghana**

Compact, dense, solid or homogeneous.

Thus:

**एकघन - ekaghana**

One compact fullness.

Now, don't imagine consciousness is literally a dense physical material.

The word expresses its non-fragmented reality.

Not a warehouse of little consciousness particles.

### Nirviśeṣatā - निर्विशेषता

**निर् - nir-**

Without.

**विशेष - viśeṣa**

Distinction, particular characteristic, differentiation.

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract state.

Thus:

**निर्विशेषता - nirviśeṣatā**

The condition of being without distinction.

And the locative singular:

**निर्विशेषतायाम् - nirviśeṣatāyām**.

Notice the ending **-āyām**.

A feminine locative singular pattern.

So the question is framed as:

*In the condition of undifferentiated fullness of consciousness...*

How can distinctions arise?

### Sthānam - स्थानम्

Place, position, station.

From √**स्था - sthā**, to stand or remain.

In Sanskrit phonetics, *sthāna* can designate a place of articulation: the throat, palate, teeth and so forth.

### Karaṇam - करणम्

Instrument or means.

From √**कृ - kṛ**, to do or make.

In phonetics, *karaṇa* can designate an articulatory instrument or organ.

Now these two are related but not identical.

The place of articulation is where a sound is produced.

The articulatory instrument is what participates in producing it.

For example, the tongue can function as an articulator, while the relevant contact region helps specify the consonant.

Traditional grammatical descriptions classify these somewhat differently from modern phonetics, but the distinction is intelligible.

### Varṇa - वर्ण

Letter, phoneme, sound-category.

In ordinary Sanskrit it can also mean colour or class.

Here it means a phonemic element.

### Kathaṅkāram - कथङ्कारम्

How? In what manner?

An emphatic interrogative expression.

And:

### Vibhāgaḥ - विभागः

Differentiation, division, distinction.

From:

**वि - vi**

Apart, distinctly.

**√भज् - bhaj**

To divide, distribute, share.

The noun:

**विभाग - vibhāga**

Division or differentiation.

Masculine nominative singular:

**विभागः - vibhāgaḥ**.

Now the question is clear.

If consciousness is one undivided fullness, how can there be a division between articulatory locations, instruments and phonemes?

### The dangerous nondual conclusion

Imagine somebody learning:

"All sounds are Śakti."

Then deciding:

"Excellent. Sanskrit phonetics is therefore unnecessary."

They pronounce **श - śa**, **ष - ṣa**, and **स - sa** identically.

Their teacher says:

"Those are three different consonants."

The student replies:

"At the supreme level, they are one."

The teacher replies:

"At the ordinary level, your pronunciation is shit."

Both statements can be perfectly compatible.

Because the claim of ultimate unity does not require the elimination of manifest differentiation.

And that's precisely the distinction Abhinavagupta is about to defend.


## 2. Svātantrya - The Absolute is free to manifest differences

Here's Abhinavagupta's answer.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · P. 187

**यदेवं स्वातन्त्र्यं तथाविधे स्वात्मनि... अवभासयति।**

*Yad evaṃ svātantryaṃ tathāvidhe svātmani ... avabhāsayati.*

That very sovereign freedom manifests differentiated appearances within its own Self.

And the examples he chooses are spectacularly ordinary.

**घटोऽयं - ghaṭo 'yam.**

This is a pot.

**सुखमिदं - sukham idam.**

This is happiness.

**ज्ञानमिदं - jñānam idam.**

This is knowledge.

**ज्ञाताहम् - jñātāham.**

I am the knower.

Four statements.

And each illuminates a different aspect of conscious manifestation.

### Ghaṭo 'yam - घटोऽयम्

Start with:

**घटः - ghaṭaḥ**

Pot, nominative singular.

**अयम् - ayam**

This, masculine nominative singular.

Now join them:

*ghaṭaḥ + ayam → ghaṭo 'yam.*

The visarga changes, and the initial *a* of *ayam* is represented as elided by the avagraha:

**ऽ**

So:

**घटोऽयम् - ghaṭo 'yam**.

Now the proposition:

"This is a pot."

A determinate object has appeared.

It possesses recognizable characteristics.

It is not being confused with an elephant, a tree or a philosophical objection.

### Sukham idam - सुखमिदम्

**सुख - sukha**

Happiness, pleasure, ease.

**इदम् - idam**

This.

Both are neuter.

So:

**सुखमिदम् - sukham idam**.

"This is happiness."

Now the object isn't a pot.

It's an experiential quality.

A feeling.

An immediately apprehended condition.

Yet it is still something about which awareness can make a determinate judgment.

### Jñānam idam - ज्ञानमिदम्

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge, cognition.

**इदम् - idam**

This.

"This is knowledge."

Now the manifested object of attention is cognition itself.

We're no longer simply identifying an external object or a pleasant feeling.

We're apprehending a cognitive occurrence.

And finally:

### Jñātāham - ज्ञाताहम्

**ज्ञाता - jñātā**

The knower.

Masculine nominative singular of the agent noun *jñātṛ*.

**अहम् - aham**

I.

Through the joined vowels:

*jñātā + aham → jñātāham.*

"I am the knower."

Now notice the progression.

A pot.

Happiness.

Knowledge.

The knower.

It is not a randomly assembled list.

The examples traverse distinguishable forms of manifestation: object, experience, cognition and subjectivity.

And yet all can appear within consciousness.

### The actual philosophical argument

Suppose the supreme consciousness is one and undivided.

Does it follow that it can only manifest one homogeneous object forever?

Abhinavagupta says no.

Its intrinsic freedom includes the capacity to manifest different forms.

And we already recognize those differences in experience.

The cognition of a pot is not the same determinate cognition as happiness.

Happiness is not identical in content to the reflective apprehension of knowledge.

And the first-person recognition "I am the knower" has a different structure again.

But these distinctions do not establish four independently ultimate conscious substances.

They are differentiated manifestations within the self-revealing reality.

**Nonduality concerns the ultimate ground, not the abolition of intelligible difference.**

This is exactly why *svātantrya* matters.

If the supreme reality were incapable of manifesting difference, it would not possess the universal expressive freedom Abhinavagupta attributes to it.

It would be something much closer to an inert homogeneous object.

And he's already rejected that possibility repeatedly.

### The metaphysical colour palette

Imagine a painter possessing a range of colours.

Red.

Blue.

Gold.

Black.

The painting contains real visual distinctions.

But the existence of several colours doesn't require several independent artists.

Now, the analogy is limited.

The human painter works with materials not created simply through personal awareness.

Bhairava, within Trika's metaphysics, is the ultimate ground of the entire manifestation.

But the structural point is useful.

**The capacity for diverse expression is compatible with unity of agency.**

And now Abhinavagupta makes the point with an almost dismissive rhetorical question.


## 3. How much effort would infinite variety require of the Absolute?

The next sentence is:

**को वा कियान् वा प्रयासः?**

*Ko vā kiyān vā prayāsaḥ?*

What effort, or how much effort, would be involved?

Now the question is wonderful.

Because the opponent seems to imagine that the supreme reality must struggle to produce every differentiated form.

"One letter? Fine."

"Another letter? More difficult."

"Five consonant classes? Extraordinary expenditure."

"Thirty-six tattvas? We're going to need a divine engineering contractor."

But Abhinavagupta has already established *svātantrya*.

Sovereign freedom.

The absolute power of manifestation.

So why assume that differentiating a pot from happiness places some independent limitation upon it?

### Prayas - प्रयास

Effort, exertion, endeavour.

Related to a verbal family involving striving or attempting.

**प्रयासः - prayāsaḥ**

Masculine nominative singular.

And:

**कियान् - kiyān**

How great, how much?

Masculine nominative singular.

The rhetorical question concerns the supposed difficulty of manifestation.

Now don't take this as an empirical claim about a creator performing physical work without energy expenditure.

It's an internal metaphysical argument.

Within the Trika conception of sovereign consciousness, differentiated manifestation doesn't require a second independent cause capable of overpowering its freedom.

The supreme reality is not constrained by a foreign production mechanism.

Thus the complexity of manifestation is not proof of independent ontological plurality.

### The editorial verses on p. 187

The Kashmir Series inserts a substantial square-bracketed discussion immediately after *jñātāham*. It contains verses arguing that if the supreme Lord were confined to a single limited form, his consciousness and lordship would resemble the restricted existence of an ordinary object.

Those verses illustrate the problem very effectively.

But they are **editorial additions in this transcription**, not part of Abhinavagupta's uninterrupted prose.

The unbracketed argument proceeds directly from his examples of manifestation to the rhetorical question about effort. The GRETIL transcription confirms that continuity. ; ; \]

This is important because the original argument is actually sharper than the expanded gloss.

The opponent asks how an undifferentiated conscious ground can manifest differences.

Abhinavagupta replies: through the very freedom that allows it to manifest a pot, pleasure, knowledge and the knower.

And what difficulty is there in that for universal freedom?

Now he takes the argument to its logical extreme.

Not merely fifty letters.

Not merely human speech.

**Every class of being.**

Including rocks.

Trees.

Animals.

Humans.

Gods.

And increasingly exalted mantra-beings.

The ontology is about to become enormous.


## 4. Rocks, trees, animals, humans, gods - One Parā, different manifestations

Now we cross onto Kashmir Series p. 188.

Abhinavagupta says that all classes of beings belong to the one supreme ground of Parā.

The Sanskrit enumerates:

**पाषाण - pāṣāṇa** - stone.

**तरु - taru** - tree.

**तिर्यङ् - tiryaṅ** - animal.

**मनुष्य - manuṣya** - human.

**देव - deva** - god.

**रुद्र - rudra** - Rudra-class divine being.

**केवलिन् - kevalin** - a technically defined class of subject.

**मन्त्र - mantra** - mantra-being.

Then the lords and great lords of those mantra-classes.

And the conclusion is that the one supreme Parā remains in the form of Parameśvara throughout that diversity.

The crucial phrase is:

**एकैव पराभट्टारिकाभूमिः।**

*Ekaiva parābhaṭṭārikābhūmiḥ.*

There is but one supreme ground of the venerable Parā Goddess.

Now let's examine that.

### Pāṣāṇa - पाषाण

Stone, rock.

The final consonant is retroflex:

**ण - ṇa**.

Not dental **न - na**.

### Taru - तरु

Tree.

A masculine *-u* stem.

Different declensional behaviour from *pāṣāṇa*.

### Tiryaṅ - तिर्यङ्

A creature belonging to the animal realm, literally connected with oblique or transverse movement in traditional classifications.

In Indian cosmological vocabulary, *tiryak* and related forms are commonly used for nonhuman animals.

Don't confuse the word's traditional classification with a modern zoological definition.

### Manuṣya - मनुष्य

Human being.

Notice the conjunct:

**ष्य = ष् + य**.

**मनुष्य - manuṣya**.

### Deva - देव

God, luminous or divine being.

From the √*div* family involving shining or playing.

But in the present context, the word belongs to a traditional cosmological classification.

### Kevalin - केवलिन्

One who is isolated, separate or alone in a technical sense.

The precise Śaiva classification matters.

In Śaiva cosmology, *kevalin* can refer to classes of subjects such as *pralayākala* or *vijñānākala*, depending upon the classification under discussion.

It does not automatically mean a fully liberated Jain *kevalin*.

Same Sanskrit word.

Different doctrinal use.

### Mantra, Mantreśvara, Mantramaheśvara

Remember from Part X that *mantra* is not always merely a recitable formula.

In this cosmological context, it can designate a class of conscious beings.

And:

**मन्त्रेश्वर - mantreśvara**

Lord of mantras.

**मन्त्रमहेश्वर - mantramaheśvara**

Great lord of mantras.

The distinctions express levels or classes of divine agency within the Śaiva system.

Now here's the main point.

**All these differentiated forms belong to the universal ground of Parā.**

But what does that mean?

### It doesn't mean a rock thinks like a human

We need to be very careful.

Abhinavagupta's metaphysical claim is not simply:

"A stone has an ordinary human mind."

Nor:

"Trees speak Sanskrit internally."

Nor:

"Every crow possesses the same cognitive capacities as a philosopher."

That would collapse the distinctions he has just defended.

The argument concerns the **ultimate ontological ground** of all manifest entities.

Within Trika, a rock, a tree, a human and a divine being can differ profoundly in their manifested capacities.

Yet their ultimate reality isn't considered independent of universal consciousness.

That's the nondual claim.

Not that all differentiated beings are functionally identical.

### The great spiritual bureaucracy

Imagine the universe as one enormous divine organization.

Rocks.

Trees.

Animals.

Humans.

Gods.

Rudras.

Mantras.

Mantra-lords.

Great mantra-lords.

A cosmic hierarchy stretching from a pebble to the highest operative divine being.

Now someone says:

"Surely each class must have a separate ultimate consciousness?"

Abhinavagupta replies:

"No. Their differentiation is manifested within one Parā."

The metaphor is limited because an ordinary organization contains separate people and an external administration.

The Trika claim is stronger.

The underlying reality is not merely managing the manifestations from outside.

**Their being is not ultimately independent of that reality.**

And this is why the same divine power can produce multiple phonemic expressions.

The differences are real as manifestations.

The ground is one.

Now Abhinavagupta gives a name to the power that encompasses such diverse expressions.

And it's one we've encountered repeatedly.

**Mantravīrya.**


## 5. मन्त्रवीर्य - The potency of all articulated and unarticulated sound

The conclusion on p. 188 identifies the divine reality as:

**मन्त्रवीर्यम् - mantravīryam.**

The potency or generative force of mantra.

Now that's an important word.

### Mantra - मन्त्र

Sacred formula, instrument of thought or contemplation, but also a technical category in Śaiva cosmology.

We shouldn't reduce every occurrence to the familiar folk derivation "man protects."

The term has a complex ritual, linguistic and theological history.

Here it is connected with the supreme expressive power.

### Vīrya - वीर्य

Strength, power, potency, vigour, generative force.

In reproductive contexts, it can also refer to semen.

That association matters within the broader Śiva-Śakti symbolism we've been following.

But the immediate sense here is **the effective potency of mantra**.

The power through which differentiated sound becomes manifest.

### Mantravīryam - मन्त्रवीर्यम्

Neuter nominative or accusative singular.

And the prose describes the divine power as having a sound-body characterized by:

**स्फुट - sphuṭa**

Distinct, clearly manifested.

**अस्फुट - asphuṭa**

Indistinct.

**व्यक्त - vyakta**

Manifest, articulated.

**अव्यक्त - avyakta**

Unmanifest, unarticulated or not distinctly expressed.

Now that's a crucial distinction.

The expressive power isn't confined to sound that has already become a clearly articulated human phoneme.

The whole field of differentiated and undifferentiated sound belongs to the divine power.

But the categories remain distinguishable.

An audible spoken syllable is not the same perceptual phenomenon as a vague rumble.

The argument is that both participate in the wider power of manifestation.

### Sphuṭa versus vyakta

Now learn the nuances.

**स्फुट - sphuṭa**

Clear, distinctly apprehensible.

**व्यक्त - vyakta**

Manifest, differentiated, expressed.

They overlap, but aren't necessarily identical in every context.

A sound can be audible without being distinctly identifiable as a particular word.

A rhythm may be recognizable without having a lexical meaning.

A roar may communicate danger without possessing the phonemic structure of Sanskrit prose.

And Abhinavagupta is about to argue that **the value of a sound cannot be restricted to clearly articulated letters**.

That's the next major movement.

The ontology of speech has to include more than the language of scholars.

And here's how he illustrates it.

Musical instruments.

Different registers.

And then animals.

Because the whole universe, apparently, has been recruited into Abhinavagupta's Sanskrit seminar.


## 6. The vīṇā, the vipañcī, the kacchapikā, and the drum

Abhinavagupta's next illustration names four kinds of instruments.

**वीणा - vīṇā**

**विपञ्ची - vipañcī**

**कच्छपिका - kacchapikā**

**मुरुज - muruja**

The first three belong to the family of stringed instruments. The *muruja* is a type of drum.

And his argument is that a recognizable sound or tonal identity can emerge from different physical sources.

The Sanskrit says:

**स एव स्वनोऽन्यतोऽन्यतो देशादप्युद्भवन्।**

*Sa eva svano 'nyato 'nyato deśād apy udbhavan.*

That very sound, although arising from one location or another...

The point is that the identity of the sound need not be absolutely tied to one physical instrument or one articulatory location. ; \]

Now let's examine the actual words.

### Vīṇā - वीणा

The familiar Indian string instrument, or historically a family of string instruments rather than one unchanging design.

The final vowel is long:

**णा - ṇā**.

And the nasal is retroflex.

### Vipañcī - विपञ्ची

A historical type of string instrument.

Notice:

**पञ्च - pañca**

Five.

It might be tempting to derive the full instrument name directly from the numeral and infer its number of strings.

But the historical construction of the *vipañcī* is a separate musicological question.

We shouldn't manufacture an instrument specification from a syllabic resemblance.

What is certain here is that Abhinavagupta uses it as one of several distinct sound-producing instruments.

### Kacchapikā - कच्छपिका

Another string instrument.

Now the phonemes are useful.

**क - ka**

**च्छ - ccha**

**पि - pi**

**का - kā**

The conjunct:

**च्छ = च् + छ**.

The name is related to *kacchapa*, tortoise, though its precise historical construction should not be reconstructed solely from the name.

### Muruja - मुरुज

A type of drum, more commonly also encountered in related forms such as *muraja* in Sanskrit musical literature.

The distinction in spelling matters because the consulted passage actually has **murujādiṣu**.

We shouldn't silently replace every occurrence with the familiar English name of another percussion instrument.

### Svana - स्वन

Sound, resonance, tone.

From √**स्वन् - svan**, to sound or resonate.

Now:

**स्वनः - svanaḥ**

Sound, masculine nominative singular.

### Anyatas - अन्यतः

From elsewhere, from another place or direction.

Repeated:

**अन्यतोऽन्यतः - anyato 'nyataḥ**

From one place and another.

And:

### Udbhavan - उद्भवन्

Arising, emerging.

From:

**उद् - ud**

Up, out.

**√भू - bhū**

To become or arise.

Present participial form:

**उद्भवन् - udbhavan**.

Now the illustration becomes clear.

The sound emerges from different instruments.

The instruments themselves are distinct.

The mode of sound production differs.

But the resulting sound can be apprehended through a common tonal or musical identity.

### The philosophical distinction

Abhinavagupta isn't saying a plucked string and a struck drum produce identical acoustic waveforms.

They don't.

Nor is he denying that their mechanisms differ.

That would defeat the example.

The argument is that **a recognizable sonic identity can be expressed through differentiated physical conditions**.

And this provides an analogy for the relationship between phonemic identity and variable places of manifestation.

### The cosmic orchestra

Imagine an orchestra.

One instrument sounds a note.

Another sounds a corresponding note.

They differ in timbre.

Their physical construction differs.

But the musical relation can still be recognized.

Now imagine a philosopher insisting:

"If the instruments differ, there must be a completely unrelated music for each instrument."

No.

Difference in physical means doesn't eliminate every possible common identity.

And that's the point.

**The manifest instrument and the underlying sonic category are distinguishable without becoming entirely unrelated.**

Now Abhinavagupta extends this argument.

Not just different instruments.

Different **registers**.

High.

Middle.

Low.

Because apparently one musical example was not sufficient to satisfy the Absolute.


## 7. Tāra, Madhya and Mandra - One sound, three vocal registers

The next words are:

**तारमध्यमन्द्रेष्वपि।**

*Tāramadhyamandreṣv api.*

Even in the high, middle and low registers.

Now here's a very useful Sanskrit triad.

### Tāra - तार

High, acute or upper register.

### Madhya - मध्य

Middle, intermediate.

### Mandra - मन्द्र

Low, deep or bass register.

And the combined expression:

**तारमध्यमन्द्रेषु - tāramadhyamandreṣu**

In the high, middle and low registers.

The final ending **-eṣu** is locative plural.

Now the important thing.

The same tonal identity can be apprehended despite differences in register.

But that doesn't mean pitch and register aren't real.

Abhinavagupta is not claiming low and high notes sound identical.

He's pointing to a kind of continuity or common musical identity across differentiated expression.

### Sthāyisvara - स्थायिस्वर

Now the key compound.

**स्थायिन् - sthāyin**

Abiding, stable, continuing.

Related to √**स्था**, to stand.

**स्वर - svara**

Tone, sound, vowel or musical note, depending upon context.

Thus:

**स्थायिस्वर - sthāyisvara**

An abiding or underlying tone.

And the text refers to its:

**ऐकात्म्य - aikātmya**

Unity, having one nature.

Now, in the present context, that means a common sonic identity can be apprehended across different registers.

The physical realizations differ.

But they needn't be treated as fundamentally unrelated sonic categories.

### The editorial verses

The Kashmir Series includes two verses from a *Varṇaśikṣā* in square brackets.

They describe sound rising through the navel, chest, throat and head, and connect different recitational registers with Vedic metres.

They're useful illustrations of traditional phonetic and musical classification.

But **they are editorially inserted**, not the uninterrupted continuation of Abhinavagupta's prose.

The main commentary makes the point much more briefly: the identity of a tone can be considered despite high, middle and low register.

Now here's why this matters.

If the same tonal category can be expressed through distinct registers, the difference between an inner phonemic power and its outward physical articulation needn't constitute a contradiction.

The one power can manifest under different conditions.

And this brings us to a more provocative example.

What if the sound doesn't come from a musical instrument?

What if it comes from a different kind of living being?

A bird.

Specifically, a crow or raven.

Now the poor animal is about to be drafted into the phonology department.


## 8. The crow's *ka*, *ṭa* and *ra* - Apparently, the bird knows his consonants

At Kashmir Series p. 189, Abhinavagupta states that the same phoneme can manifest through different places of production in different creatures.

He gives the example of:

**ध्वाङ्क्ष - dhvāṅkṣa**

A crow or raven-like bird.

And identifies sounds resembling:

**ककार - kakāra**

The *ka* sound.

**टकार - ṭakāra**

The *ṭa* sound.

**रेफ - repha**

The *r* sound.

Now that's spectacular.

We've spent sixteen installments studying the metaphysics of Sanskrit.

And Abhinavagupta now essentially says:

"Look at that crow. He's doing phonetics."

But let's understand what he actually means.

### Dhvāṅkṣa - ध्वाङ्क्ष

A crow, raven or corvid in traditional Sanskrit usage.

Now the spelling is valuable.

**ध्वा - dhvā**

Begins with:

**ध्व = ध् + व**.

Then:

**ङ्क्ष - ṅkṣa**

A complex cluster involving the velar nasal and the *kṣ* conjunct.

The word is:

**ध्वाङ्क्ष - dhvāṅkṣa**.

And the locative plural:

**ध्वाङ्क्षेषु - dhvāṅkṣeṣu**

Among crows.

### Kakāra - ककार

The *ka* sound.

**क - ka**

Plus the phonemic naming element:

**कार - kāra**.

Thus:

**ककार - kakāra**.

### Ṭakāra - टकार

The *ṭa* sound.

Notice:

**ट - ṭa**

Retroflex.

Not dental:

**त - ta**.

### Repha - रेफ

The *r* sound.

This is a technical designation familiar from grammatical and phonemic discussions.

We've already encountered it in the interpretation of the vocalic **ऋ - ṛ**.

Now Abhinavagupta describes the sounds of crows as arising through various bodily regions, including:

**उदर - udara**

Abdomen.

**पायु - pāyu**

Anus or anal region.

**कण्ठ - kaṇṭha**

Throat.

**तालु - tālu**

Palate.

Now that's a rather extraordinary list.

And one we shouldn't quietly sanitize.

The text really does invoke regions extending from the abdomen and lower body to the throat and palate.

### What exactly is being claimed?

Abhinavagupta is arguing that phonemic identities needn't be restricted to the precise human articulatory arrangements described by Sanskrit grammarians.

Other beings can produce sounds that he interprets through related sonic categories.

But here we need a crucial distinction.

**This is a traditional phonetic and metaphysical interpretation, not a modern zoological demonstration that crows articulate Sanskrit phonemes with human-equivalent vocal organs.**

Bird vocal anatomy differs substantially from human speech anatomy.

The comparison doesn't establish literal identity of articulatory mechanism.

And we shouldn't pretend it does.

The philosophical function of the example is clear enough without that exaggeration.

**A recognizable sound-character may be expressed through different physiological arrangements.**

Now, Abhinavagupta is pursuing a very expansive conception of *varṇa*.

The phonemic power isn't restricted to one human mouth.

And, at the level of his metaphysics, the same Śakti underlies the differentiated vocalizations of various beings.

### The crow's Sanskrit examination

Imagine a crow sitting outside a monastery.

It calls.

"Ka! Ka!"

A Sanskrit grammarian looks up.

"That bird has an unusual place of articulation."

The crow calls again.

"Ka!"

The grammarian begins writing a dissertation.

Abhinavagupta appears.

"Excellent. Now explain why the bird's sound is not outside the universal power of speech."

The grammarian puts down his pen.

The crow steals his lunch.

The crow has won.

### The deeper philosophical problem

If Mātṛkā is the universal power underlying phonemic manifestation, can a sound produced by another creature be completely outside her?

Abhinavagupta argues that it cannot.

But this creates an objection.

Because the bird's cry isn't clearly articulated Sanskrit.

It may be indistinct.

So can we genuinely treat it as belonging to Mātṛkā?

And now the commentator develops the distinction between **clearly articulated** and **indistinct** sound.

This is where the argument becomes particularly important.

Because it extends sacred phonemic power beyond ordinary human language.


## 9. Avyakta - An indistinct sound doesn't cease to be sound

The text states:

**अव्यक्तत्वेऽपि... शब्दत्वात्।**

*Avyaktatve 'pi ... śabdatvāt.*

Even though indistinct, because it is sound.

And then gives the more comprehensive reason:

**शब्दस्य च मातृकातिरेकिणोऽभावात्।**

*Śabdasya ca mātṛkātirekiṇo 'bhāvāt.*

Because there is no sound that ultimately lies outside Mātṛkā. ; \]

Now let's dismantle the Sanskrit.

### Vyakta - व्यक्त

Manifest, distinct, articulated, explicit.

Related to a verbal formation involving making something visible or manifest.

### Avyakta - अव्यक्त

Unmanifest, indistinct, unarticulated.

The negative prefix:

**अ - a-**.

Thus:

**अव्यक्त - avyakta**.

And the abstract noun:

**अव्यक्तत्व - avyaktatva**

The condition of being indistinct.

Locative singular:

**अव्यक्तत्वे - avyaktatve**.

Now:

**अपि - api**

Even, although.

Thus:

**अव्यक्तत्वेऽपि - avyaktatve 'pi**

Even in the condition of indistinctness.

### Śabdatva - शब्दत्व

The condition of being sound.

**शब्द - śabda**

Sound, word.

**-त्व - -tva**

Abstract condition.

Thus:

**शब्दत्व - śabdatva**

Soundhood.

Ablative:

**शब्दत्वात् - śabdatvāt**

Because of being sound.

Now the next phrase.

### Mātṛkā - मातृका

The Mother, or the divine phonemic matrix.

We've studied this extensively.

In the present context, Mātṛkā is not merely a printed list of Sanskrit letters.

She is the divine power through which phonemic manifestation becomes possible.

### Atirekin - अतिरेकिन्

Exceeding, going beyond, being outside.

From:

**अति - ati**

Beyond.

And a formation involving *atireka*, excess or going beyond.

Now:

**मातृकातिरेकिणः - mātṛkātirekiṇaḥ**

Of what lies beyond Mātṛkā.

And:

**अभावात् - abhāvāt**

Because of absence or nonexistence.

So the whole statement means:

There is no independently existing sound wholly outside the universal phonemic matrix.

### This is not the claim that every sound is a Sanskrit word

Here's the essential distinction.

A bird cry is not automatically a Sanskrit noun.

The crash of a drum is not necessarily a grammatically correct Sanskrit sentence.

And an ocean wave does not possess a hidden declensional ending simply because it makes a noise.

Abhinavagupta's claim concerns the **underlying expressive power of sound**, not the proposition that every acoustic event already has a conventional Sanskrit lexical analysis.

Within the Tantric account, Mātṛkā is the ground of phonemic manifestation.

And that ground can include sounds whose articulation is indistinct.

Now here's the philosophical issue.

The opponent might concede that the bird's cry is a sound.

But still object:

"Fine. Perhaps it's outside the alphabet. We simply don't include it because it has no linguistic use."

That would be an intelligible move.

If the alphabet is merely a practical inventory of conventional human phonemes, we could exclude certain noises.

But Abhinavagupta is making a stronger argument.

He says that the absence of ordinary linguistic articulation does **not** make a sound functionally meaningless.

And he gives a wonderful counterexample.

Drums.

The ocean.

Joy.

And suffering.


## 10. A drum can move you without saying a single word

Now comes one of the most unexpectedly modern-feeling arguments in the commentary.

Abhinavagupta says that even sounds whose phonemic forms are indistinct can produce:

**ह्लाद - hlāda**

Delight.

And:

**परिताप - paritāpa**

Distress, suffering or painful agitation.

The relevant expression is:

**ह्लादपरितापकारित्वम्।**

*Hlādaparitāpakāritvam.*

The capacity to produce delight or distress.

And the examples include sounds of drums and the sea.

Now this is a remarkably effective objection to the claim that indistinct sounds possess no meaningful function.

### Hlāda - ह्लाद

Delight, joy, pleasant excitement.

Related to the same root-family as:

**आह्लाद - āhlāda**

Delight.

Which we encountered in Somānanda's description of supreme conscious bliss.

Now, ordinary *hlāda* isn't automatically identical to supreme *ānanda*.

But the lexical relationship is useful.

### Paritāpa - परिताप

Distress, burning pain, affliction.

From:

**परि - pari**

Around, completely.

**ताप - tāpa**

Heat, suffering, pain.

Related to √**तप् - tap**, to heat, burn or undergo austerity.

Thus:

**परिताप - paritāpa**

Affliction or distress.

### Kāritva - कारित्व

The condition of causing or producing something.

Related to the causative verbal family of √**कृ**, to do or make.

Now:

**ह्लादपरितापकारित्व - hlādaparitāpakāritva**

The capacity to produce delight and distress.

A lovely enormous compound.

But it expresses a very simple observation.

**A sound can affect consciousness even when it isn't recognized as a clearly articulated word.**

### The music argument

Imagine hearing a drum.

It produces a rhythm.

There may be no lexical meaning.

No noun.

No verb.

No sentence.

Yet the sound can affect your experience.

It may be energizing.

Pleasurable.

Alarming.

Oppressive.

Now imagine hearing a great storm at sea.

The roar of the waves can evoke awe, fear or distress.

Again, the sound needn't possess conventional human syntax to be significant.

And that's Abhinavagupta's counterexample.

The opponent says:

"Indistinct sounds have no use."

Abhinavagupta replies:

"They can produce delight or distress. What other use were you demanding?"

The relevant rhetorical question is:

**कोऽन्योऽभिमत उपयोगः?**

*Ko 'nyo 'bhimata upayogaḥ?*

What other desired use is there?

Now that is a wonderful philosophical comeback.

Because the opponent's criterion for inclusion has become too narrow.

The sound's significance cannot be reduced to its suitability for ordinary lexical analysis.

### The contemporary analogy

Imagine listening to instrumental music.

A passage moves you deeply.

Yet no intelligible sentence has been spoken.

You don't conclude:

"Nothing meaningful occurred because the drummer failed to supply an accusative singular."

That would be absurd.

The experience can possess significance without depending upon conventional propositional language.

Now, Abhinavagupta goes further than this ordinary observation.

He incorporates such sounds within the universal power of Mātṛkā.

That's the specifically Tantric claim.

The experiential observation supports the rejection of absolute uselessness.

The theological interpretation explains the sounds through the divine power.

Keep those two levels distinct.

**Nonlexical sound can be meaningful in experience; Abhinavagupta understands its deeper ground through Mātṛkā.**

And now we reach something even more surprising.

In some Śaiva mantra teachings, the **indistinct sound** is not merely tolerated.

It can be regarded as particularly important.

Even more central than clearly articulated syllables.

This is where **Nāda** and **Nirodhinī** enter.


## 11. Ardhacandra, Nāda and Nirodhinī - The mantra becomes subtler than letters

The commentary now invokes teachings concerning:

**अर्धचन्द्र - ardhacandra**

Half-moon.

And related subtle phonemic powers associated with the ascent or penetration of mantra.

It says that, in relevant Śaiva scriptural contexts, the nature of mantra is often explained principally through **indistinct sound**, rather than only through a sequence of clearly articulated letters.

Now that's an important transition.

We've spent many installments studying distinct vowel and consonant forms.

And Abhinavagupta now says the metaphysical power of mantra cannot be exhausted by the outwardly audible alphabet.

### Ardhacandra - अर्धचन्द्र

**अर्ध - ardha**

Half.

**चन्द्र - candra**

Moon.

Thus:

**अर्धचन्द्र - ardhacandra**

Half-moon.

In Tantric phonemic diagrams, the term may designate a subtle sign or stage in the progression beyond ordinary articulated sound.

But don't assume every written crescent in every script automatically carries precisely the same function.

The immediate context is a Śaiva mantra doctrine.

### Mantravyāpti - मन्त्रव्याप्ति

**मन्त्र - mantra**

Mantra.

**व्याप्ति - vyāpti**

Pervasion, penetration, extension.

From *vi-√āp*, to pervade or obtain.

Thus:

**मन्त्रव्याप्ति - mantravyāpti**

The pervasion or penetration of mantra.

And:

### Sāra - सार

Essence, core, substance.

So:

**मन्त्रव्याप्तिसार - mantravyāptisāra**

The essential power of mantra-pervasion.

Now the philosophical idea.

The clearly articulated syllable is one level of manifestation.

The subtle *nāda* or inner sonic power is another.

And the tradition can attribute particular efficacy to the subtler level.

Again, this is a theological and contemplative classification.

It isn't an empirical claim that an inaudible sound has been measured as possessing greater physical acoustic energy than an audible one.

The doctrine concerns the levels of expressive consciousness.

### Nirodhinī - निरोधिनी

Now the text cites a striking fragment:

**निरोधिनीमनुप्राप्तः शब्दः शुमशुमायते।**

*Nirodhinīm anuprāptaḥ śabdaḥ śumaśumāyate.*

Having reached Nirodhinī, the sound produces something described as *śuma-śuma*.

Now that is extraordinary.

The sacred text is giving an **onomatopoeic description** of a subtle sound.

And the word is delightfully unusual.

### Nirodhinī - निरोधिनी

The restraining or arresting power, in the relevant phonemic sequence.

Related to:

**निरोध - nirodha**

Restraint, cessation, obstruction.

From:

**नि - ni**

Down, into.

**√रुध् - rudh**

To obstruct, restrain or arrest.

The feminine form:

**निरोधिनी - nirodhinī**

She who restrains.

The accusative singular:

**निरोधिनीम् - nirodhinīm**.

### Anuprāptaḥ - अनुप्राप्तः

Having reached, arrived at.

From:

**अनु - anu**

Following, along.

**प्र - pra**

Forth.

**√आप् - āp**

To obtain or reach.

The past participle:

**अनुप्राप्त - anuprāpta**

Reached.

Masculine nominative singular:

**अनुप्राप्तः - anuprāptaḥ**.

Agreeing with:

**शब्दः - śabdaḥ**

Sound.

### Śumaśumāyate - शुमशुमायते

Now here's the remarkable word.

The form mimics or describes a characteristic sound.

*Śuma-śuma.*

It is not an ordinary lexical noun requiring us to assign a definite physical referent.

Nor should we pretend to know the exact acoustic waveform from the written imitation.

The text is using sound symbolism to describe a subtle sonic occurrence.

And the important point is that this occurrence is included within the doctrine of mantra.

### The philosophical significance

Now think carefully.

The opponent wanted sound to be clearly articulated in ordinary phonemic form to be meaningful.

Abhinavagupta points to a scriptural tradition in which the most significant mantra-powers may be described through increasingly subtle, indistinct sonic states.

If the opponent's criterion were correct, much of the relevant Tantric doctrine would become unintelligible.

And Abhinavagupta refuses that.

The power of mantra is not limited to the grossly articulated sound.

Its ultimate nature belongs to the divine expressive consciousness.

Now the text invokes bells, metal resonances and other sounds discussed in teachings of *Nāda*.

And then it quotes a particularly extraordinary passage from the *Guhyayoginītantra*.

Because apparently horses, lions and camels are about to join the divine choir.


## 12. The Guhyayoginītantra - Horses, lions and camels become sacred sound examples

Now comes a quoted scriptural passage.

Its opening includes:

**हयो हेषति यद्वच्च**

*Hayo heṣati yadvac ca.*

Just as a horse neighs...

And:

**सिंहो गर्जति यद्वच्च**

*Siṃho garjati yadvac ca.*

Just as a lion roars...

And:

**उष्ट्रः सीत्कुरुते यथा**

*Uṣṭraḥ sītkurute yathā.*

Just as a camel makes its characteristic hissing or whistling sound.

Now, the passage also mentions another animal vocalization, transmitted with a difficult expression involving *dānta*. The parallel explanatory edition interprets it as a tamed ox bellowing. That reading is possible within its context, but I won't invent a definitive zoological identification from the form alone.

The interesting thing is that the quoted Tantra deliberately uses **nonhuman vocalizations** as examples of powerful sound.

And then associates such sounds with a ritual claim concerning the drawing of the life-powers of bound beings.

Now we should distinguish the textual claim from empirical fact.

The source claims that powerful Yoginīs can employ such sonic forms in a special ritual operation of attraction or drawing in.

That is a **traditional esoteric claim**.

It is not evidence that neighing, roaring or imitating animal sounds has been scientifically shown to extract another being's life force.

And it is not an instruction to experiment on animals.

Its function here is to support the broader Śaiva claim that sonic power need not be restricted to clearly articulated Sanskrit words. ; \]

Now let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Hayaḥ - हयः

Horse.

The connected form:

**हयो - hayo**

Arises from visarga sandhi before the following *h*.

### Heṣati - हेषति

Neighs.

An onomatopoeic verbal form associated with the horse's cry.

Now this is a useful distinction.

The word names a sound through an established Sanskrit verb.

It is not therefore the same as a horse producing a grammatically articulated Sanskrit sentence.

### Siṃhaḥ - सिंहः

Lion.

Notice the anusvāra:

**ं - ṃ**.

And:

### Garjati - गर्जति

Roars.

From √**गर्ज् - garj**, to roar or thunder.

Now compare:

**गर्जन - garjana**

Roaring, thunderous sound.

And:

**गर्जति - garjati**

He or it roars.

Same root-family.

### Uṣṭraḥ - उष्ट्रः

Camel.

Now the cluster:

**ष्ट्र = ष् + ट् + र**.

That's a rather impressive conjunct.

And:

### Sītkurute - सीत्कुरुते

Makes a hissing or whistling sound.

The form contains the imitative sound *sīt* and a verbal formation from √**कृ - kṛ**, to do.

Thus the sense is "makes a *sīt* sound."

Now, Abhinavagupta's choice of quotation is philosophically important.

The examples are not conventional Sanskrit words with ordinary lexical meanings.

They're vocalizations.

And the Tantra treats them as relevant to the power of sound.

### The animal orchestra

Imagine a horse neighing.

A lion roaring.

A camel hissing.

And Abhinavagupta saying:

"Excellent. All of you illustrate the impossibility of restricting sound to clearly articulated human language."

The horse looks confused.

The lion continues roaring.

The camel spits.

The Sanskrit philosopher is absolutely delighted.

But underneath the ridiculous image is a serious doctrinal point.

**The field of sonic manifestation is broader than conventional human phonemic articulation.**

And within Trika, that field belongs ultimately to Śakti.

Now Abhinavagupta immediately qualifies the ritual citation.

The animal-sound examples are merely an *upāya*, a means.

The real mantra is inward.

And that is where the argument becomes exceptionally subtle.


## 13. The inward Nāda - Why the sound isn't the whole mantra

Now we arrive at one of the most important philosophical statements in the passage.

Abhinavagupta distinguishes the outward ritual sound from the inward nature of mantra.

The key expression is:

**आन्तर एवासौ नादात्मा मन्त्रः।**

*Āntara evāsau nādātmā mantraḥ.*

That mantra, having the nature of Nāda, is inward.

Now, don't misread this as a general rejection of spoken mantras.

He's not saying external recitation is worthless.

Nor that one should stop chanting Sanskrit.

The distinction concerns **the ultimate nature of the expressive power that becomes articulated through mantra**.

### Āntara - आन्तर

Inner, internal.

Related to:

**अन्तर् - antar**

Within.

The adjective:

**आन्तर - āntara**

Internal.

Masculine nominative singular:

**आन्तरः - āntaraḥ**.

Through sandhi:

**आन्तर एव - āntara eva**.

The connected spelling is:

**आन्तर एव** or, in the continuous phrase, **आन्तर एवासौ**.

### Nādātman - नादात्मन्

Having Nāda as one's nature.

**नाद - nāda**

Sound, resonance, the subtle sonic principle.

**आत्मन् - ātman**

Self, nature.

Thus:

**नादात्मन् - nādātman**

Having the nature of Nāda.

The masculine nominative singular:

**नादात्मा - nādātmā**.

Now the phrase:

**नादात्मा मन्त्रः - nādātmā mantraḥ**

The mantra whose nature is Nāda.

### The distinction between *nāda* and a physical sound wave

In ordinary usage, *nāda* can denote sound or resonance.

In Tantric metaphysics, it can designate a subtle principle within the manifestation of speech.

The latter is the relevant use here.

We should not simply reduce it to a specific frequency measurable by a microphone.

Nor should we pretend that all audible sound is therefore identical in ritual power.

The classification concerns the relationship between manifested sonic forms and the underlying divine power.

### An extremely important double negative

Now comes a subtle grammatical point worth retaining.

The Sanskrit says that this conception of mantra cannot **fail** to apply even to mantras consisting of clearly manifested letters.

The wording contains:

**न न सञ्चारयितुं शक्यते।**

*Na na sañcārayituṃ śakyate.*

It cannot not be extended or applied.

Now this is a double negative.

And it matters.

The point is **not** that inward Nāda cannot have any relation to articulated mantras.

Quite the opposite.

The inward principle must also be acknowledged as underlying the clearly articulated mantra.

Let's learn the construction.

### Sañcārayitum - सञ्चारयितुम्

To cause to move, communicate, extend or apply.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Together, completely.

**√चर् - car**

To move, proceed.

The causative:

**चारयति - cārayati**

Causes to move.

And the prefixed form:

**सञ्चारयति - sañcārayati**

Transmits, causes to move, extends.

The infinitive:

**सञ्चारयितुम् - sañcārayitum**.

Now:

### Śakyate - शक्यते

It is possible, can be done.

Related to √**शक् - śak**, to be able.

The passive or impersonal verbal construction:

**शक्यते - śakyate**

Can be done.

With:

**न न - na na**

It cannot fail to be done.

Thus the argument extends the inward nature of mantra to outwardly articulated forms as well.

### The significance

Imagine reciting a mantra.

You hear the syllables.

You recognize the phonemes.

You may understand their meaning or their traditional ritual association.

But the capacity through which that recitation is apprehended and made meaningful isn't exhausted by the external acoustic event.

Abhinavagupta's doctrine grounds the articulated form in a subtler expressive consciousness.

And this relationship is not limited to deliberately sacred formulae.

It's the same structure that has governed the entire passage.

The manifest sound is differentiated.

The underlying expressive power is one.

### The metaphor of a distant pot

Abhinavagupta then supplies an almost hilariously ordinary analogy.

A pot remains a pot even when it is far away.

The same reality can be recognized despite a difference in manifest conditions.

The Sanskrit says:

**विदूरगतोऽपि घटो घट एव।**

*Vidūragato 'pi ghaṭo ghaṭa eva.*

Even a pot that has gone far away remains a pot.

Now the pot has returned again.

It simply refuses to leave the commentary.

### Vidūragata - विदूरगत

**वि - vi**

Apart, away.

**दूर - dūra**

Far, distant.

**गत - gata**

Gone.

From √**गम् - gam**, to go.

Thus:

**विदूरगत - vidūragata**

Gone far away.

And:

**घट एव - ghaṭa eva**

A pot indeed.

Now here's the analogy.

A change in distance need not destroy the object's identity.

Similarly, an indistinct or otherwise transformed acoustic manifestation needn't be excluded automatically from the broader sound-principle.

The metaphor is about continuity through variable conditions.

Not a proof that distance has no effect on acoustics.

### The final conclusion

The inward nature of Nāda and the outward manifestation of sound aren't independent unrelated substances.

And the significance of a sound isn't confined to ordinary human lexical articulation.

That is the principle Abhinavagupta now uses to interpret one of the most astonishing claims in Patañjali's *Yogasūtras*.

**Knowledge of the vocalizations of all beings.**

Now we're going to move from Kashmir Śaivism directly into the *Vibhūtipāda* of classical Yoga.

And the connection is much more sophisticated than simply saying that meditation lets you talk to animals.


## 14. Patañjali's Yogasūtra III.17 - How could a yogin understand the sounds of all creatures?

At Kashmir Series pp. 190-191, Abhinavagupta invokes **Śeṣamuni**, the venerable serpent-associated sage traditionally identified with Patañjali.

And he refers to the teaching of:

**सर्वभूतरुतज्ञान - sarvabhūtarutajñāna**

Knowledge of the sounds of all beings.

Now this is a striking connection.

Because the same Patañjali whose grammatical tradition we encountered in the vowel discussion is now relevant through the *Yogasūtras*.

But it's a different textual context.

We're not simply discussing *e* and *o* pronunciation.

We're discussing the relationship between **sound, meaning and cognition**.

The original *Yogasūtra* 3.17 reads:

**शब्दार्थप्रत्ययानामितरेतराध्यासात् संकरस्तत्प्रविभागसंयमात् सर्वभूतरुतज्ञानम्॥**

*Śabdārthapratyayānām itaretarādhyāsāt saṃkaras tatpravibhāgasaṃyamāt sarvabhūtarutajñānam.*

It teaches that sound, meaning and cognition are ordinarily superimposed or confused with one another, and that through *saṃyama* on their distinction, knowledge of the sounds of all beings is said to arise. ; (https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutras-with-commentaries/d/doc1216724.html); \]

Now let's unpack it.

### Śabda - शब्द

Sound, word.

A phonetic or linguistic expression.

### Artha - अर्थ

Meaning, object, referent, purpose.

In this context, the referent or meaning associated with the sound.

### Pratyaya - प्रत्यय

Cognition, conception, mental representation.

In different grammatical and philosophical contexts, the word has other technical meanings too.

Here it concerns the cognitive apprehension associated with the word and its meaning.

Now the compound:

**शब्दार्थप्रत्यय - śabdārthapratyaya**

Sound, meaning and cognition.

And the genitive plural:

**शब्दार्थप्रत्ययानाम् - śabdārthapratyayānām**.

Of sound, meaning and cognition.

### Itaretara - इतरेतर

One another, mutually.

From the repeated pronoun-family of *itara*, other.

### Adhyāsa - अध्यास

Superimposition, attribution, projection of one thing upon another.

From:

**अधि - adhi**

Upon.

**√अस् - as**

In the relevant verbal formation of placing or throwing upon.

Thus:

**अध्यास - adhyāsa**

Superimposition.

The ablative:

**अध्यासात् - adhyāsāt**

Because of superimposition.

### Saṃkara - संकर

Intermixture, confusion, blending.

From a formation involving bringing or mixing together.

Now the proposition.

Sound, meaning and cognition are ordinarily experienced in a closely integrated way.

Consider hearing the word:

**गो - go**.

Cow.

You hear the sound.

You apprehend the meaning.

And you form a cognition involving the relevant object.

The three may appear together in ordinary experience.

Yet Patañjali distinguishes them analytically.

The sound isn't simply identical to the physical cow.

The object isn't identical to your mental cognition.

And the cognition isn't merely the audible syllable.

Now comes the yogic operation.

### Pravibhāga - प्रविभाग

Detailed differentiation, separation.

From:

**प्र - pra**

Forth.

**वि - vi**

Distinctly.

**भाग - bhāga**

Part, division.

Thus:

**प्रविभाग - pravibhāga**

Careful differentiation.

### Saṃyama - संयम

The integrated application of concentration, meditation and meditative absorption in Patañjali's technical scheme.

Now:

**संयमात् - saṃyamāt**

From or through *saṃyama*.

And:

### Sarvabhūtarutajñāna - सर्वभूतरुतज्ञान

**सर्व - sarva**

All.

**भूत - bhūta**

Living being, creature, existent.

**रुत - ruta**

Cry, call, vocalization.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

Thus:

**सर्वभूतरुतज्ञान - sarvabhūtarutajñāna**

Knowledge of the vocalizations of all beings.

### Why Abhinavagupta invokes it

Now here's the fascinating question.

How could distinguishing sound, meaning and cognition produce understanding of bird calls?

Patañjali's Yoga tradition presents this as an extraordinary attainment or *siddhi*.

Abhinavagupta's concern is the philosophical possibility of meaning in sounds not clearly articulated as ordinary human speech.

If such sounds were wholly outside the field of meaningful sound, how could the claimed yogic knowledge of them be intelligible?

His expansive account of Mātṛkā provides a metaphysical framework.

The bird's sound belongs to the manifestation of Śakti.

Its relation to meaning is not necessarily exhausted by conventional human language.

And therefore the yogic claim can be interpreted within that broader account.

### The important qualification

This is a traditional doctrinal explanation of a claimed extraordinary capacity.

It is **not independently verified evidence that practising *saṃyama* gives somebody universal, infallible translation of every animal call**.

Nor does recognizing the philosophical argument establish that Abhinavagupta had conducted a modern scientific study of animal communication.

The value of the passage lies in understanding how his metaphysics relates **sound, meaning and cognition** across different forms of manifestation.

And that is a genuinely sophisticated question.

### The crow returns one more time

Imagine a crow calling.

A person hears a noise.

An ornithologist investigates its behavioural significance.

A Sanskrit grammarian examines possible phonetic similarities.

A yogin, according to the traditional *siddhi* claim, may apprehend a deeper meaning through *saṃyama*.

And Abhinavagupta asks what makes all these forms of apprehension possible within the universal expressive power.

The perspectives differ.

The empirical claims differ.

But his philosophical system insists that meaningful manifestation is not ultimately outside consciousness.

That's the relationship.

And now he extends the argument to sounds produced by drums.

Because apparently the entire percussion section is now involved in language theory.


## 15. The drum can announce victory or defeat

The commentary continues with another example.

The sound of a **भेरी - bherī**, a large drum.

And the fact that particular drum signals can indicate:

**जय - jaya**

Victory.

**अजय - ajaya**

Defeat or absence of victory.

Now this is an interesting case.

Because the drum sound may not contain a conventional spoken word.

Yet it can function as a sign.

For example, a particular rhythm or signal could announce a military event.

The sound has a meaning through established practice.

And Abhinavagupta uses this to challenge the claim that only clearly articulated lexical words can be meaningful.

### Bherī - भेरी

A large drum or kettledrum.

The vowel:

**ए - e**

And the final long:

**ई - ī**.

### Jayā-jaya - जयाजय

The contrast between victory and nonvictory or defeat.

Now:

**जय - jaya**

Victory.

From √**जि - ji**, to conquer.

And:

**अजय - ajaya**

Nonvictory, defeat.

The prefixed negative *a-*.

When joined:

*jaya + ajaya → jayājaya.*

Because:

**a + a → ā**.

Now:

### Sūcakatā - सूचकता

The condition of indicating or signalling.

From:

**सूचक - sūcaka**

Indicating, signifying.

Related to √**सूच् - sūc**, to indicate.

And:

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract state.

So:

**जयाजयसूचकता - jayājayasūcakatā**

The condition of indicating victory or defeat.

Now this is a useful distinction.

A sound can signify through a conventional code.

The code might be different from ordinary spoken language.

Yet the sound still has a communicative function.

### Why this example differs from the ocean

Now here's a subtle point.

The ocean roar can produce delight or fear.

That gives it experiential significance.

The drum signal can indicate victory or defeat.

That gives it a more specific communicative significance.

Those are not identical kinds of meaning.

Abhinavagupta is gathering several examples to undermine the overly narrow assumption that indistinct sound cannot possess any function.

But we should preserve the distinctions.

A pleasurable sound.

A threatening sound.

A conventional signal.

A sacred mantra.

An animal call.

All may have different roles.

And the universal ontology of sound doesn't erase those roles.

### The point

**Meaning is not exhausted by the ordinary lexical articulation of Sanskrit speech.**

That is why Abhinavagupta can discuss drums and bird cries within the larger account of Mātṛkā.

And now he brings the argument back to traditional phonetics.

Specifically, disagreements over the places where certain sounds are produced.

The *h* sound.

Visarga.

The chest.

And the roots of the teeth.

Which is particularly funny after all the cosmic claims.

We've gone from the universal ground of consciousness back to somebody trying to work out where to put their tongue.

But this is not a digression.

It's the next stage of the same argument.


## 16. The Śikṣā traditions - Even grammarians disagree about articulatory location

Now Abhinavagupta invokes the *Śikṣā* tradition, the traditional science of phonetics and correct pronunciation.

The Sanskrit contains two short technical assertions:

**हविसर्जनीयावुरस्यावेकेषाम्।**

*Havisarjanīyāv urasyāv ekeṣām.*

According to some, *ha* and Visarga are associated with a chest articulation.

And:

**रदनमूलमेकेषाम्।**

*Radanamūlam ekeṣām.*

According to others, the root of the teeth.

Now this is important because it shows that traditional phonetic classifications themselves can vary.

And Abhinavagupta uses that variability to support his broader account of sound identity.

### Visarjanīya - विसर्जनीय

The visarga sound.

From the *visarga* family we've studied.

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission.

**विसर्जनीय - visarjanīya**

A grammatical designation of the Visarga sign or sound.

Now the dual form:

**हविसर्जनीयौ - havisarjanīyau**

*Ha* and Visarga.

### Uras - उरस्

Chest.

Now:

**उरस्य - urasya**

Pertaining to the chest.

The dual:

**उरस्यौ - urasyau**.

So:

**हविसर्जनीयावुरस्यौ - havisarjanīyāv urasyau**

*Ha* and Visarga are chest-associated.

And:

**एकेषाम् - ekeṣām**

Of some, according to certain authorities.

Now the other phrase.

### Radana - रदन

Tooth.

### Mūla - मूल

Root, base.

So:

**रदनमूल - radanamūla**

Root of the teeth.

Now the meaning is clear.

Different phonetic traditions may classify the articulation differently.

But the sound isn't thereby annihilated.

### The philosophical argument

Suppose one tradition associates a sound with the chest.

Another with a different articulatory region.

Does that mean one group is discussing a completely different metaphysical entity?

Not necessarily.

The sound may be recognized through common phonemic identity despite differences in description or production.

Now, ordinary phonetics still matters.

We should investigate actual articulatory differences.

But the existence of such differences doesn't establish independent ultimate consciousnesses behind each sound.

And that's Abhinavagupta's concern.

### A crucial editorial distinction

The Kashmir Series adds two verses inside square brackets to explain the phonetic statements.

They give more specific classifications of *ha*, Visarga and their associated articulatory regions.

Those are editorially supplied illustrations, not additional uninterrupted prose from Abhinavagupta.

The main text itself invokes the Śikṣā statements and draws its conclusion. ; \]

Now here's the next question.

If phonetic variations can be recognized within the same broader sound-principle, what happens when grammarians count them as separate letters?

The alphabet gets bigger.

A lot bigger.

And we arrive at the next extraordinary number.

**Sixty-four.**


## 17. Sixty-four letters - The alphabet starts multiplying

Now we reach Kashmir Series p. 192.

And Abhinavagupta explains how some phonemic enumerations arrive at **sixty-four** letters.

Now the grammar students are in trouble.

They've only just memorized the regular consonantal series.

Then learned the vowels.

Then the special signs.

Then Mātṛkā.

And Abhinavagupta announces:

"Actually, in another classification, there are sixty-four."

Fantastic.

The alphabet has acquired fourteen additional employees.

But the important point is that these are **distinctions of pronunciation and classification**, not necessarily fourteen completely unrelated basic phonemes.

The passage gives the additional categories in order.

### First: Jihvāmūlīya and Upadhmānīya

The Sanskrit begins:

**विसर्जनीयाज्जिह्वामूलीयोपध्मानीयौ।**

*Visarjanīyāj jihvāmūlīyopadhmānīyau.*

From Visarga, the Jihvāmūlīya and Upadhmānīya forms.

Now these are genuine Sanskrit phonetic terms.

And they're worth learning.

**जिह्वा - jihvā**

Tongue.

**मूल - mūla**

Root.

Thus:

**जिह्वामूल - jihvāmūla**

Root of the tongue.

And:

**जिह्वामूलीय - jihvāmūlīya**

Pertaining to the tongue-root.

In traditional Sanskrit phonetics, *Jihvāmūlīya* is a special realization of Visarga before certain velar consonants, particularly *k* and *kh*.

Now the second:

**उपध्मानीय - upadhmānīya**

A special labial realization of Visarga, traditionally associated with its occurrence before *p* and *ph*.

The word belongs to the family of *upadhmā*, blowing or breathing toward something.

Now the philosophical point.

Two special articulatory realizations can be distinguished from the more general Visarga category.

The phonetic distinction has a concrete basis.

But Abhinavagupta doesn't infer that they must therefore be independent ultimate sound-principles outside Mātṛkā.

They're differentiations within the expressive field.

### Second: The five Yamas

The commentary then introduces:

**अनुनासिकेभ्यः पञ्च यमान्।**

*Anunāsikebhyaḥ pañca yamān.*

Five *Yama* sounds associated with the nasal consonants.

Now:

**अनुनासिक - anunāsika**

Nasal, produced with nasal resonance.

Related to:

**नासिका - nāsikā**

Nose.

And:

**पञ्च - pañca**

Five.

Then:

**यम - yama**

Here a technical phonetic category, not the deity of death.

Now, don't confuse these with the ordinary five main nasal consonants themselves.

The classification concerns special related phonetic realizations in traditional phonology.

The editor adds a short bracketed inventory to illustrate the five.

That inventory is explanatory, not a new sentence of Abhinavagupta's main prose.

### Third: Seven lighter articulatory variants

Next the text names:

**ड - ḍa**

**ढ - ḍha**

**य - ya**

**र - ra**

**ल - la**

**व - va**

**क्ष - kṣa**

And describes lighter or less forceful realizations of those sounds as additional distinguishable categories.

The technical expression is:

**लघुप्रयत्नतरान् - laghuprayatnatarān.**

Let's dismantle it.

**लघु - laghu**

Light, small, slight.

**प्रयत्न - prayatna**

Effort, articulatory exertion.

**-तर - -tara**

Comparative formation.

Thus:

**लघुप्रयत्नतर - laghuprayatnatara**

Involving a comparatively lighter effort of articulation.

Now don't interpret this simply as "pronounce the letters quietly."

Articulatory effort and loudness are not the same thing.

The distinction concerns traditional phonetic descriptions of how the sound is produced.

And Abhinavagupta points out that such differences can be counted as additional categories.

### The arithmetic of sixty-four

The scheme can be understood as:

| Component | Count |
|--------|--------|
| Base fifty-letter Tantric inventory | 50 |
| Jihvāmūlīya and Upadhmānīya | 2 |
| Five Yama sounds | 5 |
| Seven lighter articulatory variants | 7 |
| **Total** | **64** |

Now here's the important qualification.

**This does not mean ordinary Classical Sanskrit necessarily has sixty-four independently contrastive phonemes in every linguistic analysis.**

The count belongs to a traditional expanded phonemic scheme.

Different systems count certain sounds and variants separately.

And the ritual significance of a sixty-fourfold Mātṛkā is not identical with an ordinary modern phonological inventory.

Abhinavagupta's concern is precisely how variation and classification can be accommodated within the universal power of speech.

Now notice the philosophical structure.

The base sound.

Its variant.

Its place of articulation.

Its degree of effort.

Its special realization.

All can be distinguished.

But the distinctions do not establish independent ultimate realities.

**The alphabet becomes numerically larger without dividing Bhairava into additional substances.**

That's the central point. ; \]


## 18. ऋ and र - The difference between a vowel and a consonant survives nonduality

Now Abhinavagupta makes a particularly useful comparison.

He invokes:

**ऋवर्ण - ṛvarṇa**

The vocalic *ṛ*.

And:

**रशब्द - raśabda**

The consonantal *r* sound.

And says their relationship illustrates a distinction like that between vowels and consonants generally.

This is wonderful because we've been learning this exact difference since Part XIII.

### The vowel ṛ

**ऋ - ṛ**

A vocalic rhotic.

It occupies a position in the traditional vowel series.

For example:

**कृ - kṛ**

The consonant *k* with the vocalic *ṛ* sign.

Now:

### The consonant r

**र - ra**

The consonantal rhotic.

For example:

**क्र - kra**

A conjunct beginning with *k* followed by *r*.

And:

**क्रि - kri**

That consonantal cluster with the vowel *i*.

These are not the same as **कृ - kṛ**.

Now the distinction is important.

The sounds can be related through their rhotic character.

But that doesn't eliminate their different phonological roles.

One is a vowel.

The other a consonant.

### Svara - स्वर

Vowel, or tone in other contexts.

### Vyañjana - व्यञ्जन

Consonant.

The word belongs to the *vy-añj* family, involving expression or making manifest.

And:

**स्वरव्यञ्जन - svaravyañjana**

Vowels and consonants.

Now the philosophical point.

**Relationship is not the same as absolute identity of function.**

The vocalic *ṛ* and consonantal *r* can belong to a related sound-family.

But they're not interchangeable in Sanskrit words.

And the fact that both are ultimately understood through Mātṛkā doesn't eliminate their conventional linguistic distinction.

This is exactly the balance Abhinavagupta has been defending.

Unity of ground.

Difference of manifestation.

Not a forced choice between the two.

### The dreaded nondual spelling error

Imagine a student writing:

**कृति - kṛti**

An act, work or creation.

But replacing the vocalic *ṛ* with a consonantal *r* plus *i*.

The result has a different phonological structure.

The student explains:

"But all sounds are one consciousness."

And the teacher replies:

"Then the supreme consciousness can surely manifest the correct vowel."

Excellent.

We've resolved the apparent contradiction.

And now the commentary turns toward the sixty-fourfold Mother.

The sacred alphabet arranged as eight groups of eight.


## 19. Aṣṭāṣṭaka - Eight groups of eight, one Mātṛkā

The divine Mother in her phonemic form.

Pervaded.

This world, this universe.

The verse doesn't establish that the physical universe contains exactly sixty-four kinds of matter.

### The Goddess's eight-by-eight spreadsheet

Imagine the Absolute beginning with one self-revealing consciousness.

Then articulating them as sounds.

Then classifying those sounds into eight octads.

**The sixty-fourfold arrangement is a representation of the powers; it is not sixty-four independent consciousnesses.**

And that is why the verse identifies the Mātṛkā-circle with the pervasion of the entire universe.

### Abhinavagupta's reference to another exposition

## 21. The next boundary - Fullness matters more than endlessly counting letters

At Kashmir Series p. 193, Abhinavagupta begins:

**इह तु तत्प्रक्रियानभिनिवेशः पूर्णतैकसारत्वात्।**

*Iha tu tatprakriyānabhiniveśaḥ pūrṇataikāsāratvāt.*

Here, however, there is no insistence upon that particular procedural classification, because fullness is the sole essential principle.

This is an excellent natural boundary.

The discussion of sound-variation and expanded alphabets has reached its culmination.

Now the commentary explicitly changes emphasis.

From detailed phonemic enumeration.

To the relationship between **conventional language and the nonconventional ground of consciousness**.

And that is going to be a spectacular next installment.

Because Abhinavagupta will ask how ordinary words acquire meaning.

He'll consider the relation between convention and prior understanding.

He'll examine the word *go*, cow.

And he'll argue that even conventional language depends upon a more fundamental conscious reality that isn't itself merely the result of a convention.

This is a remarkable continuation.

The first part of the argument asked:

**How can one consciousness manifest different sounds?**

The next will ask:

**How can those sounds come to mean anything?**

And the distinction is profound.

A sound's existence is one problem.

Its intelligibility as a sign is another.

The relation between sound, meaning and awareness is where Abhinavagupta now turns.

We will resume at that exact line in Part XVIII. ; \]


## 22. The complete philosophical movement of Part XVII

Let's gather the entire argument in its original order.

| Stage | Abhinavagupta's point |
|--------|--------|
| The objection | How can undivided consciousness manifest separate letters and articulatory instruments? |
| The examples | Pot, happiness, knowledge and knower demonstrate different forms of conscious presentation |
| Svātantrya | Sovereign freedom is the ground of differentiated manifestation |
| Classes of beings | Stones, trees, animals, humans and divine beings are not ultimately outside Parā |
| Mantravīrya | Distinct and indistinct sound belong to the universal expressive power |
| Different instruments | Recognizable sonic identities can arise from different physical sources |
| High, middle and low registers | Musical identity can be apprehended across variable realizations |
| Crows and other creatures | Sonic expression is not restricted to the human articulatory system |
| Indistinct sound | A sound need not be a clearly articulated word to have significance |
| Drums and the sea | Nonlexical sounds can produce pleasure or distress |
| Subtle Nāda | The inward nature of mantra underlies outward articulation |
| Guhyayoginītantra | Animal vocalizations are used as examples in esoteric sound teachings |
| Patañjali's Yogasūtra III.17 | Sound, meaning and cognition can be analytically distinguished |
| Drum signals | Nonlexical sounds can communicate conventional meanings |
| Śikṣā phonetics | Traditions recognize different places and modes of articulation |
| Sixty-four letters | Special phonetic distinctions can enlarge the Mātṛkā inventory |
| Ṛ and r | Related sounds can retain different phonological functions |
| Eight groups of eight | The Trikaratnakula identifies the sixty-fourfold Mātṛkā with the Kula-circle |

Now the philosophical problem is no longer mysterious.

The one consciousness can manifest many differentiated forms through its own freedom.

And those forms can retain their distinct functions.

The pot isn't the same as happiness.

A human voice isn't the same as a lion's roar.

A drum isn't the same as an ocean wave.

A short vowel isn't the same as a consonant.

A clearly articulated mantra isn't acoustically identical to an indistinct resonance.

**Nonduality does not require the erasure of these differences.**

It requires that their ultimate reality not be established as independently separate from universal consciousness.

That is the point of *sarvasarvātmakatva*.

And it's why Abhinavagupta can accommodate the whole diversity of sound within Mātṛkā.

The differentiated manifestations matter.

But the ground remains one.

## 24. The final insight - Śiva doesn't speak only Sanskrit

This is the insight I want to retain from Part XVII.

Abhinavagupta has established that the divine power of expression is not restricted to one physical configuration.

It can become manifest through human speech.

Through musical instruments.

Through different registers.

Through indistinct sounds.

Through the vocalizations of animals.

And through the subtle *Nāda* described by the Tantric scriptures.

Now we must distinguish the levels.

The conventional phonology of Sanskrit remains meaningful.

A crow's call is not a grammatically correct Sanskrit sentence merely because both involve sound.

A musical note does not possess the same semantic structure as the word *ghaṭa*, pot.

And the metaphysical classification of subtle sound is not identical with a modern scientific account of acoustics.

But none of those differences requires the sounds to exist independently of the universal expressive power.

And that is the philosophical point.

**The same consciousness can manifest genuinely different sounds without becoming fundamentally divided by those differences.**

This is why the entire passage returns to Mātṛkā.

The Mother is not merely fifty written glyphs.

Within the Tantric system, she is the conscious power underlying the manifestation of sound.

And her differentiated expressions can be classified in various ways.

Fifty.

Sixty-four.

Eight groups of eight.

Different recitational variants.

Different degrees of articulation.

But the multiplication of sound-classes doesn't multiply the ultimate conscious ground.

Now the really beautiful thing is what happens next.

Having established the universality of Mātṛkā, Abhinavagupta refuses to become trapped in endlessly enumerating her variations.

Because the essential point is **pūrṇatā**, fullness.

And he turns to the deeper question.

How does sound become meaningful?

What is the relation between the word *go*, cow, and the actual object?

Why can a child acquire a word through convention?

And how can convention itself be established unless consciousness already possesses a more fundamental capacity for meaningful apprehension?

That is where Part XVIII begins.

The alphabet has become universal.

Now language itself is going on trial.

And Abhinavagupta is going to prosecute the idea that words acquire meaning from an entirely self-sufficient external convention.

The poor grammarians haven't a chance.
