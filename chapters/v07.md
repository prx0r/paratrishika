# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART VII - Mālinī and the Great Alphabet Dispute

*Abhinavagupta discovers that the sacred scriptures have assigned the letters of the alphabet to different principles of reality, realizes this could wreck an entire system of Tantric cosmology, and proceeds to defend the Absolute by starting an argument with everyone.*

Continuing the commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series edition, pp. 120 onward


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

We've reached the point where Sanskrit itself has become an enormous metaphysical contraption.

The vowels express the powers of consciousness.

The consonants correspond to the *tattvas*, the principles of manifestation.

The first vowel, **अ - a**, symbolizes Anuttara.

The entire alphabet unfolds as an expression of supreme Speech, **Parā Vāk**.

And the final conjunct **क्ष - kṣa** brings us to the completion of the manifested series.

Beautiful.

An astonishingly elegant system.

Everything has a place.

The universe is organized.

The Goddess is satisfied.

Surely Abhinavagupta can now continue explaining the relationship between vowels, lunar phases and divine powers?

Unfortunately, there's a problem.

A very large problem.

**The other Tantric scriptures don't arrange the letters in the same order.**

Oh, for fuck's sake.

We've constructed this magnificent cosmic alphabet, and suddenly the Mālinīvijayottaratantra is assigning different letters to the tattvas.

The Svacchandatantra has its own arrangement.

The Siddhātantra presents another relevant system.

And even within the Mālinī tradition, the alphabet may be rearranged completely.

At first glance, it looks as though the divine powers have failed to agree upon their own filing system.

Which is particularly embarrassing when your metaphysics claims the sounds disclose the fundamental structure of reality.

But Abhinavagupta isn't going to dismiss the problem.

He's going to make it explicit, investigate the rival systems, and then ask a much deeper question:

**If Śiva possesses absolute freedom, why should his manifestations be confined to one compulsory sequence?**

This is where the commentary becomes genuinely formidable.

And quite funny.

Because Abhinavagupta is about to ridicule two opposite positions.

First, that the differing arrangements are arbitrary conventions with no deeper reality.

Second, that divine freedom makes philosophical reasoning entirely unnecessary.

His answer will be far more subtle.

And, as usual, the Sanskrit grammar will do a considerable amount of work.


## 1. The first problem - There are thirty-four differentiated principles

We resume at the end of Abhinavagupta's explanation of the four *dhāraṇās* and five higher Brahma-principles.

He has just accounted for the *vijñānākala* class of subjects by invoking **Mahāmāyā**, and explained why different scriptures sometimes include that level within adjacent principles rather than listing it separately.

Remember the basic difficulty.

The familiar thirty-six-*tattva* system is not identical with every classificatory arrangement used in the earlier Śaiva Tantras.

Some principles can be included within broader categories.

Others may be explicitly distinguished.

For instance, the temporal and restrictive principles **Kāla** and **Niyati** are not separately enumerated in the particular arrangement used here.

Abhinavagupta has therefore reached a set of thirty-four differentiated principles, considered in relation to their supreme source.

Now comes his statement:

ATTESTED SANSKRIT - KASHMIR SERIES, P. 120

**एवमेतानि चतुस्त्रिंशत्तत्त्वानि प्रक्रियात्मना स्थितान्यकारमेवादिरूपतया भजन्ते।**

*Evam etāni catustriṃśat tattvāni prakriyātmanā sthitāny akāram evādirūpatayā bhajante.*

A close rendering:

Thus these thirty-four principles, existing as an ordered exposition, have the letter *a* as their primordial form or beginning.

Now that's a superb sentence for learning Sanskrit.

### Catuśtriṃśat - चतुस्त्रिंशत्

Thirty-four.

Break it down:

**चतुर् - catur**

Four.

**त्रिंशत् - triṃśat**

Thirty.

The combined numeral is:

**चतुस्त्रिंशत् - catustriṃśat**

Thirty-four.

Notice that Sanskrit often constructs compound numbers in the order *unit + ten*.

And this word contains the conjunct:

**स्त्र = स् + त् + र**

When you see:

**त्रिंशत् - triṃśat**

You should recognize thirty.

And when you see:

**चतुस्त्रिंशत्**

You've got thirty-four.

Now the next phrase.

### Prakriyātmanā - प्रक्रियात्मना

This one is particularly useful.

**प्रक्रिया - prakriyā**

Procedure, ordered exposition, process, system of analysis.

**आत्मन् - ātman**

Self, nature, essence.

The compound:

**प्रक्रियात्मन् - prakriyātman**

Having the nature of a procedure or systematic arrangement.

The ending:

**-आत्मना - -ātmanā**

Instrumental singular of the compound's *-ātman* element.

Thus *prakriyātmanā* means something like "in the character of a systematic exposition."

Now why is this important?

Because Abhinavagupta has already distinguished the ultimate reality from its different modes of exposition.

An ordered tattva system is a way of understanding manifestation.

It is not necessarily a chronological account in which the supreme reality literally manufactures one phoneme every few seconds.

The procedural order has a function.

And that function must be understood.

### Ādirūpatayā - आदिरूपतया

Now another beautiful compound.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning, origin.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form, nature.

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract state or condition.

Then the instrumental ending:

**-या - -yā**

So:

**आदिरूपतया - ādirūpatayā**

In the capacity or condition of having the primordial form.

The thirty-four principles have *a* as their beginning or source.

And here's the important metaphysical distinction:

They depend upon Anuttara.

But Anuttara doesn't depend upon their particular order of manifestation.

That is the seed of the whole argument.

Because now Abhinavagupta asks the obvious question.


## 2. कोऽयं क्रमः - What the bloody hell is this order?

Here's the central challenge, in his own Sanskrit:

**कोऽयं सृष्टिसंहारज्ञप्तिस्थित्यवतारक्रमाणां मध्यात् क्रमः।**

*Ko 'yaṃ sṛṣṭisaṃhārajñaptisthityavatārakramāṇāṃ madhyāt kramaḥ?*

What sort of sequence is this, among the sequences of creation, reabsorption, cognition, maintenance and manifestation?

The commentary raises this because the present arrangement starts with the supreme Śiva principle represented by *a*, proceeds through the gross elements, and eventually reaches Śakti.

But the other scriptural arrangements often work from **क्ष - kṣa**, associated with Earth, backwards toward the vowel series and supreme consciousness.

Now the problem isn't merely:

"Why is one alphabet backwards?"

It's:

**What metaphysical operation is the alphabet supposed to represent?**

Creation?

Withdrawal?

Cognition?

Maintenance?

The emergence of manifestation?

These are different ways of organizing the relationship between the unmanifest and manifest.

Let's learn the Sanskrit.

| Word | IAST | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **सृष्टि** | *sṛṣṭi* | Creation, emission |
| **संहार** | *saṃhāra* | Withdrawal, reabsorption |
| **ज्ञप्ति** | *jñapti* | Cognition, notification |
| **स्थिति** | *sthiti* | Maintenance, abiding |
| **अवतार** | *avatāra* | Descent, manifestation |
| **क्रम** | *krama* | Sequence, order |

### The grammar of ko 'yam

**कः - kaḥ**

Who? Which? What?

Here the interrogative is masculine nominative singular because it modifies *kramaḥ*, which is masculine.

Then:

**अयम् - ayam**

This.

So:

**कः + अयम् → कोऽयम्**

*kaḥ + ayam → ko 'yam*

This is the same visarga sandhi you've already learned.

A masculine *-aḥ* form before initial *a* often becomes **-o 'a-**.

The avagraha **ऽ** marks the elided initial *a*.

Thus:

**कोऽयम् - ko 'yam**

What is this?

And in the full sentence:

**कोऽयं क्रमः - ko 'yaṃ kramaḥ**

What is this sequence?

Now Abhinavagupta is asking something genuinely demanding.

If the phonemes are inherently linked to the tattvas, why is there more than one arrangement?

Could there be multiple legitimate forms of manifestation?

Or does one system have to be wrong?

And if they are all legitimate, what makes them legitimate?

Is the relationship conventional?

Scripturally prescribed?

Established by divine will?

Or grounded in something deeper?

Those are the questions we're going to follow.


## 3. The first rival system - From क्ष backwards to अ

Abhinavagupta first names the Mālinīvijayottara, Siddhātantra and Svacchandatantra.

Their arrangement, he observes, works in the opposite direction from the system he has just explained.

Instead of beginning with **अ - a** and moving through the differentiated principles, they associate **क्ष - kṣa** with Earth and move toward the supreme vowel-series.

**The Parātrīśikā arrangement**

# अ

Anuttara

# क … म

Manifestation

# क्ष

Śakti

**The contrasting withdrawal-oriented arrangement**

# क्ष

Earth

# …

Higher principles

# स्वर

Vowel-series

These show the organizing direction, not every individual correspondence. The opposing traditions also group phonemes into mantra-units rather than always assigning one letter to one tattva.

Now here's the important thing.

**The latter arrangement isn't merely the former chart read backwards.**

The Sanskrit passage describes a more complicated grouping.

Earth is associated with *kṣa*.

Other sets of tattvas receive groups of consonants.

The highest Śiva principle is represented through the sixteen vowels as a unity.

And Śakti is included in that supreme principle because, as the commentary explicitly argues, power cannot ultimately be separated from the possessor of power.

So these are genuinely different systems of organization.

Not simply two scribes holding the same manuscript upside down.

### The first quoted arrangement

Abhinavagupta cites a scriptural formulation describing the *kṣa*-based distribution of phonemes through the principles.

The important phrase is:

**क्षमादितः - kṣamāditaḥ**

Beginning with *kṣa*.

Let's dismantle it.

**क्ष - kṣa**

The phoneme formed from क् + ष.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning, origin.

**-तः - -taḥ**

An adverbial or ablatival formation, here indicating the starting point.

The continuous expression conveys beginning from the *kṣa* sound.

Now notice something absolutely vital.

The Sanskrit **क्ष** isn't a simple independent consonant letter in the same way as क or प.

In conventional Devanāgarī writing, it's a conjunct:

**क् + ष = क्ष**

But in the sacred alphabet of the Tantric system, *kṣa* is treated as a terminal phonemic unit.

That distinction makes it possible to use the entire series from *a* to *kṣa* as a symbol of total manifestation.

It's a conventional linguistic structure receiving a metaphysical interpretation.

### Why reverse the order?

In a withdrawal-oriented ritual, the practitioner may proceed from gross manifestation toward increasingly subtle principles.

Earth.

Water.

Fire.

Air.

Space.

The faculties.

The limited subject.

The higher powers.

Ultimately, supreme consciousness.

This is a coherent structure of **saṃhāra**, reabsorption.

And in initiation, especially *adhvaśuddhi*, purification of the pathways or orders of manifestation, such an arrangement can have a defined ritual role.

### Adhvaśuddhi - अध्वशुद्धि

Break it down:

**अध्वन् - adhvan**

Path, course, way.

**शुद्धि - śuddhi**

Purification.

From √*śudh*, to become pure.

Together:

**अध्वशुद्धि - adhvaśuddhi**

Purification of the path or ordered levels of manifestation.

In Śaiva initiation, different *adhvans* can represent the structures of reality-phonemes, words, mantras, principles and worlds-within which bondage is to be purified.

The ritual isn't merely a phonetic recital.

It participates in a whole cosmological system.

And if you are proceeding from gross manifestation toward its underlying source, a reverse ordering makes sense.

Imagine dismantling a house.

You start with the finished structure.

Remove the furnishings.

Then the walls.

Then the foundations.

You don't begin by demolishing the abstract concept of architecture.

Likewise, a withdrawal-oriented system can begin with the grossest tattva.

But Abhinavagupta's problem remains.

If these correspondences have a genuine spiritual significance, how can different arrangements all claim validity?

Hold that question.

Because the next system makes the problem even worse.


## 4. Mālinī arrives - The Goddess rearranges the alphabet

Now comes the system you've specifically been waiting for.

**मालिनी - Mālinī**

A name associated with a garland, a wreath, or one adorned with a garland.

And within Trika, Mālinī is a supreme form of Śakti expressed through a specially ordered arrangement of phonemes.

The ordinary Sanskrit is worth learning.

### Mālā - माला

**माला - mālā**

Garland, wreath, string or necklace.

### Mālinī - मालिनी

**मालिनी - mālinī**

A feminine form associated with possession of a garland.

The masculine form *mālin* means garlanded or possessing a garland, and the feminine is *mālinī*.

Notice the long vowels.

मा - mā.

लि - li.

नी - nī.

The final **-ī** is represented by the right-side vowel sign **ी**.

So:

**मा + लि + नी = मालिनी**

Now the lovely thing about this name is that the image of a garland suits the phonemic system.

Individual sounds are like elements strung into a single expressive power.

But Mālinī is not merely the ordinary alphabet arranged in a decorative circle.

Her distinctive feature is that her letter-order differs from the usual sequence.

### Bhinnayoni - भिन्नयोनि

Abhinavagupta calls attention to Mālinī as:

**भिन्नयोनि - bhinnayoni**

Let's understand the term.

**भिन्न - bhinna**

Separated, divided, broken apart, differentiated.

From √*bhid*, to split or divide.

**योनि - yoni**

Womb, source, origin, generative matrix.

Thus:

*bhinnayoni*

Having a differentiated or differently configured generative matrix.

This term requires care.

It does not mean the Goddess has a defective womb.

Nor is it merely an anatomical assertion.

It concerns the organization of the phonemic powers through which manifestation is symbolically generated.

The adjective *bhinna* also describes separation and differentiation more generally.

And its root is beautifully visible in another word:

**भेद - bheda**

Difference, division.

From the same root-family √*bhid*.

Now compare:

**भेद - bheda**

Difference.

**अभेद - abheda**

Non-difference.

You've encountered that distinction constantly in Trika.

And Mālinī's alternative ordering expresses the differentiated arrangement of the powers without establishing their ultimate separation from Śiva.

### The unusual alphabet

An explanatory annotation preserved with the Kashmir Series transcription supplies a Mālinī sequence beginning:

**न ऋ ॠ ऌ ॡ थ च ध ई …**

*na ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ tha ca dha ī …*

Notice the first phoneme.

**न - na**

Not **अ - a**.

And immediately after it come vocalic vowels.

Then a mixture of consonants and other vowels.

This is plainly not the ordinary Sanskrit alphabetic order.

The sequence in the source's accompanying annotation also contains a few orthographic uncertainties, so I'm not going to reconstruct its entire fifty-plus-member inventory from a potentially corrupted transcription and pretend every character is secure. The main commentary establishes the crucial point: Mālinī employs a different ordering from conventional Mātṛkā.

Now here's the extraordinary implication.

The usual alphabet arranges letters partly according to phonetic characteristics.

Vowels.

Velars.

Palatals.

Retroflexes.

Dentals.

Labials.

Semivowels.

Sibilants.

And the remaining sounds.

Mālinī does not preserve that conventional sequence.

She rearranges the phonemes into a distinct Tantric configuration.

The ordering is doctrinally and ritually significant.

But it also creates a formidable exegetical problem.

How can *ka* express one tattva in one system, while another sound expresses that same tattva in Mālinī?

And if both are divine, why the disagreement?

The problem is not going away.

It's expanding.

Like Śakti herself.

### Mālinī versus Mātṛkā

Now distinguish the two carefully.

**मातृका - Mātṛkā**

The alphabetic Mother-power, associated here with the conventional or undivided source-order of the phonemes.

Described in the explanatory tradition as *abhinnayoni*, the undivided matrix.

**मालिनी - Mālinī**

The Goddess expressed through a different ordering of those phonemic powers.

Called *bhinnayoni*, the differentiated matrix.

The passage contrasts the Mālinī arrangement with the familiar Mātṛkā arrangement.

The accompanying annotations also explain Mālinī-mantra as expressing **Śakti**, and Mātṛkā-mantra as expressing **Śaktimat**, the possessor of power.

This is an interpretive gloss supplied with the edition, so we should not confuse the exact wording of the editorial annotation with Abhinavagupta's own uninterrupted Sanskrit prose.

The doctrinal contrast itself is deeply appropriate.

Śakti is the power of differentiation.

Śaktimat is the reality possessing that power.

But the two aren't independent substances.

### Sanskrit laboratory: Abhinnayoni

**अभिन्नयोनि - abhinnayoni**

Now compare it with *bhinnayoni*.

**भिन्न - bhinna**

Divided, differentiated.

**अभिन्न - abhinna**

Undivided, not separated.

Add:

**योनि - yoni**

Matrix, source.

So:

**abhinnayoni** \= having an undivided source or matrix.

Now be careful about the pronunciation.

**अभिन्न - abhinna**

The consonant after भि is doubled:

**न्न = न् + न**

And *bhinna* is not pronounced *bhina* with a single n.

The doubled consonant is part of the phonological form.

### The great theological distinction

In the symbolic system, Mātṛkā represents the familiar sequence and Mālinī an alternative ordering that manifests the power of differentiation.

But the two don't establish separate ultimate realities.

They express distinct relationships among the same divine phonemic powers.

And this is where the metaphysics of Śiva and Śakti becomes necessary.

**The possibility of multiple arrangements is itself a manifestation of freedom.**

That's the direction Abhinavagupta is heading.

But he isn't going to settle for that assertion alone.

Not yet.

First, the other traditions must be brought into the courtroom.


## 5. Mālinī's second mapping - Earth has moved to फ

Abhinavagupta next gives the beginning of another scriptural arrangement.

In this scheme, Earth is associated not with **क्ष**, as in the previously cited order, but with **फ**.

Yes.

The Earth principle has moved.

Apparently the cosmic alphabet has undergone a departmental restructuring.

The relevant statement begins with the assignment:

**फे धरातत्त्वम् - phe dharātattvam**

The Earth principle at *pha*.

Let's learn that.

### Phe - फे

Start with:

**फ - pha**

An aspirated labial consonant.

Now add the vowel **ए - e**.

The dependent vowel sign is **े**.

So:

**फे - phe**

This is the locative singular form of a phoneme-name represented as *pha*.

In a phrase such as *phe dharātattvam*, it indicates the letter *pha* as the location of the correspondence.

It's not the meaningless syllable *phe* introduced from nowhere.

The vowel comes from the case ending.

### Dharātattva - धरातत्त्व

**धरा - dharā**

Earth, the supporting one.

Related to √*dhṛ*, to hold or support.

**तत्त्व - tattva**

Principle of reality.

Together:

**धरातत्त्व - dharātattva**

The Earth principle.

Now the contrast becomes explicit.

| Arrangement | Earth corresponds to |
|--------|--------|
| The regular alphabetic scheme of the Parātrīśikā | क - *ka* |
| The reverse arrangement cited from other Śaiva scriptures | क्ष - *kṣa* |
| The Mālinī-based arrangement | फ - *pha* |

One Earth principle.

Three phonemic positions.

All within religious systems claiming sacred authority.

Well.

This is awkward.

Imagine three astrologers giving Mars three different planetary rulerships, and every one insisting the arrangement is cosmologically fundamental.

Or three ritual specialists arguing whether the Earth tattva lives in the first consonant, the last consonant, or somewhere else entirely.

At some point someone has to ask:

**Are these correspondences real, or are we just making it up?**

And Abhinavagupta knows the question cannot be avoided.

That is precisely why he brings all these systems together.

He's not accidentally exposing a contradiction.

He's deliberately forcing the problem into view.




## 6. The three Vidyās - Parā, Parāparā and Aparā enter the dispute

Now Abhinavagupta introduces another classification.

The **Vidyātraya**.

The threefold sacred knowledge or mantra-power.

**विद्यात्रय - vidyātraya**

Let's break it down.

**विद्या - vidyā**

Knowledge, sacred wisdom, or a female deity embodied in a mantra.

**त्रय - traya**

A triad, set of three.

So:

**vidyā + traya → vidyātraya**

The three Vidyās.

And of course, within Trika, the principal three are:

**परा - Parā**

**परापरा - Parāparā**

**अपरा - Aparā**

Now here's the important connection.

The three aren't simply three devotional goddesses placed alongside one another.

They express three modalities of the supreme Śakti, particularly the relationship between unity and differentiation.

But in the present passage, they also govern different systems of **ritual pervasion and purification**.

The text has shifted from an alphabetic mapping to a different kind of ontological organization.

### Parā - Supreme Will

The first of the triad is Parā.

In the accompanying scriptural explanation, she is related to **icchāśakti**, the power of will.

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will, desire, intention.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

Thus:

**इच्छाशक्ति - icchāśakti**

The power of will.

Now, don't reduce this to the ordinary psychological experience of wanting something.

Your desire for tea is not, as such, the unlimited sovereign will of Bhairava.

The divine *icchā* is the primordial freedom through which manifestation becomes possible.

The commentary's associated explanation describes the first outward stirring of the manifesting power.

The world is not yet fully articulated as a collection of separate objects.

There is the initial freedom to manifest.

Parā is identified with that supreme power.

### Parāparā - Knowledge and the integration of unity and difference

Now:

**परापरा - parāparā**

This name itself is philosophically beautiful.

*Parā* means supreme.

*Aparā* means lower or not supreme, in a relative classification.

Their combination names the intermediate power, where unity and difference are simultaneously relevant.

In the interpretive material associated with this passage, Parāparā is especially related to **jñānaśakti**, the power of knowledge.

And knowledge is precisely where the distinction between subject and object becomes meaningful.

The thing known appears.

But it is still apprehended within awareness.

There is differentiation.

Yet there is also a conscious unity through which the differentiation is known.

This is the domain of Parāparā.

### Aparā - The power of action

Finally:

**अपरा - aparā**

The differentiated or lower power.

The interpretive passage connects her particularly with **kriyāśakti**, the power of action.

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Activity, doing, action.

From √*kṛ*, to make or do.

In the fully manifested universe, things appear as differentiated subjects and objects.

Actions take place.

Causes and effects are distinguished.

The world of practical relations becomes articulated.

This corresponds to the more differentiated operation of Śakti.

### The three powers together

**परा**

Parā

Supreme will; the freedom to manifest without fully articulated differentiation.

**परापरा**

Parāparā

Knowledge; unity and difference apprehended together.

**अपरा**

Aparā

Action; differentiated manifestation.

This is a schematic philosophical association, not a claim that each goddess can exercise only one divine power.

All three remain manifestations of the one consciousness.

And the text will soon make that distinction especially important.

### But why introduce these here?

Because the different Vidyās can govern different ritual applications of the tattvas.

Under Parā, one arrangement is appropriate.

Under Parāparā, another.

Under Aparā, yet another.

The correspondence between a phoneme and a principle therefore cannot be understood in isolation from the **specific power and operation** being invoked.

This is already suggesting a possible solution to the problem.

But it is not yet the ultimate solution.

Because if the power changes the mapping, what determines which mapping is valid in a given context?

That question still requires an answer.


## 7. The ritual systems - Three goddesses, three different maps

Abhinavagupta next cites the Mālinīvijayottara's teachings concerning the three Vidyās.

And he doesn't merely mention their names.

He introduces their different ranges of ritual operation.

### Aparā's sphere

In the cited tradition, Aparā is employed in operations involving the lower or differentiated orders of manifestation.

The commentary mentions the mantra forms:

**फट् - phaṭ**

**हुं - huṃ**

**ह्रीं - hrīṃ**

Now these are genuine mantra syllables discussed in the passage.

But their ritual effects belong to traditional Tantric claims. Listing them here does not establish that they produce supernatural results, and this isn't a practical instruction to perform initiation or destructive rites.

We're studying their doctrinal classification.

The important point is that different mantra forms are used to represent different ranges of ontological manifestation.

The text discusses such ranges through **aṇḍas**, cosmic enclosures or spheres.

### Aṇḍa - अण्ड

**अण्ड - aṇḍa**

Egg, sphere, cosmic enclosure.

Notice the retroflex consonant.

**ण् + ड = ण्ड**

And that is different from the dental sequence *nda*.

The word can be used for the cosmic "eggs" or domains within the Tantric cosmology.

Here the familiar groupings include:

- The gross, material domain.
- The Prakṛti-associated domain.
- The Māyā-associated domain.
- The Śakti-associated domain.

These are not astronomical planets.

They're ontological and ritual classifications.

A mantra assigned to a given enclosure expresses its relation to a particular range of manifestation.

### The powers of coverage

The technical Sanskrit word is:

**व्याप्ति - vyāpti**

Pervasion, inclusion, extension or scope.

From *vi-* and √*āp*, to reach, obtain or pervade, through the established nominal formation.

Abhinavagupta discusses the *vyāpti* of the divine powers.

That is, how far each power's operation extends across the cosmological order.

Now compare a few conceptual uses.

**एकव्याप्ति - ekavyāpti**

Single or unitary pervasion.

**पराव्याप्ति - parāvyāpti**

Supreme pervasion.

The vocabulary concerns what is included within a particular divine or ritual power.

The same general metaphysical reality can therefore be approached through different ranges of inclusion.

### Parāparā's arrangement

The middle Goddess has her own organization of the higher and lower principles.

The scriptural citations relate particular mantra units to the pure and impure orders, using grouped syllables rather than the same one-letter-per-tattva mapping.

And here is the important distinction.

A mantra consisting of three phonemes can represent a larger ontological range.

It isn't necessary to assign one individual letter to one individual principle in every case.

A set of sounds may function as a unified mantra associated with an entire level.

Thus the numerical correspondence changes.

Not necessarily because the metaphysics has become incoherent, but because the ritual organization follows a different principle of grouping.

### Parā's supreme pervasion

Finally, the highest Goddess's system includes the use of a phonemic or mantra-unit representing the threefold order, another representing a fourth, and **visarga** as the expression of what transcends all.

Here's the Sanskrit word you already know:

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission, sending forth, release.

Ordinarily, the visarga sign is:

**ः**

Two vertically arranged dots.

But within Trika's phonemic theology, *visarga* also signifies the supreme power of emission.

The scriptural arrangement uses it to represent the domain beyond the lower enclosures.

Now here's the conceptual point.

An entire range of tattvas can be symbolically pervaded by a single sound or mantra.

The sound functions within a specific theological and ritual structure.

So the mapping is not simply a table of forty-odd independent objects.

It's an expression of the relationship between divine powers and the orders of manifestation.

But the arrangements still differ.

The opponent can still ask:

"If all three systems are valid, what makes them valid?"

And Abhinavagupta knows the question is serious.

### Why the ritual context matters

Imagine a city.

You can divide it into districts.

Transport zones.

Postal areas.

Administrative wards.

Police jurisdictions.

None of these classifications is necessarily identical.

Yet all may be legitimate for their intended purposes.

Now, that's a useful analogy for multiple Tantric mappings.

But it does **not** fully solve Abhinavagupta's problem.

Because his systems aren't merely practical descriptions invented by a local council.

They make claims about divine powers, initiation and the real structure of manifestation.

If their differences are purely arbitrary, how can they possess the profound ontological significance attributed to them?

That's precisely where Abhinavagupta's next objection begins.




## 8. The other mantra systems - And the problem gets worse

As if three divine systems weren't enough, Abhinavagupta now mentions further arrangements associated with:

**मातृकासद्भाव - Mātṛkāsadbhāva**

**रतिशेखर - Ratiśekhara**

**कुलेश्वर - Kuleśvara**

And other mantra-deities and Tantric scriptures.

Now, don't read these merely as examples of ordinary everyday words.

In this passage, they refer to particular Tantric mantra systems or authoritative traditions.

Their mappings differ from one another.

And differ from the Parātrīśikā's system.

And differ from the Mālinī order.

And sometimes seem almost completely inverted.

This is not an isolated manuscript discrepancy.

Abhinavagupta is recognizing an entire family of alternative sacred arrangements.

Now picture the scene.

Scholar A: "The Earth tattva corresponds to *ka*."

Scholar B: "No. It corresponds to *kṣa*."

Scholar C: "Actually, in this system it corresponds to *pha*."

Scholar D: "The whole group is represented by a mantra."

Scholar E: "The arrangement depends upon the Goddess and her domain of pervasion."

And the poor student is sitting there with seventeen Sanskrit charts wondering whether the entire tradition has accidentally been assembled by a committee of intoxicated librarians.

But Abhinavagupta isn't laughing it off.

He explicitly describes the problem as potentially disastrous for the student of the Āgamas.

That's important.

**He's not pretending the contradictions are merely superficial.**

He's asking how an authentic tradition can contain different structures while retaining a coherent account of divine reality.

And he rejects the easiest answer.


## 9. सङ्केत - Saṅketa: Could the whole system just be conventional?

The obvious solution would be:

"Different traditions use different conventions."

Problem solved.

One school assigns Earth to *ka*.

Another assigns it to *pha*.

Why not?

After all, languages themselves use different sounds for the same object.

English says "water."

Hindi says *pānī*.

Sanskrit says *jala*.

Different signs.

Similar referent.

So why can't the Tantric schools arbitrarily assign different letters to the same tattvas?

Abhinavagupta's response is a sharp objection.

He refuses to accept that the entire sacred relationship between letters and powers is **merely conventional**.

### Saṅketa - सङ्केत

**सङ्केत - saṅketa**

Convention, agreed signification, indication, signal.

This is a wonderful word to learn because it appears in Indian philosophy of language.

It concerns the relationship established between a sign and what it signifies.

Now, in ordinary communication, convention is important.

If you and I agree that the word "mango" refers to a particular fruit, we can communicate.

If a different community uses a different word, that's perfectly understandable.

But Abhinavagupta is concerned with the claim that sacred sounds possess a real relation to the cosmic order.

If every correspondence were entirely arbitrary, then the doctrine would lose the distinctive metaphysical significance claimed for its sounds.

### Sāṅketika - साङ्केतिक

Conventional.

Belonging to an agreed designation.

A derivative of *saṅketa*.

The long initial ā is part of the derivative formation.

Now compare:

**सङ्केत - saṅketa**

Convention.

**साङ्केतिक - sāṅketika**

Conventional, depending on convention.

And:

**पारमार्थिक - pāramārthika**

Pertaining to ultimate reality.

Related to *paramārtha*, supreme or ultimate meaning.

Here is the philosophical tension.

Ordinary words can depend on conventions.

But the supreme manifestation of divine speech cannot simply be reduced to a set of arbitrary human agreements.

Otherwise the fundamental doctrine of sacred phonemic power would be undermined.

### The extraordinary regional-language example

Abhinavagupta gives an example from regional vocabulary.

He discusses how a similar word may refer to food in one region and to a thief in another.

He mentions southern usage, Sindh, and Kashmir.

Now this is fascinating for two reasons.

First, it shows that Abhinavagupta understood the variability of linguistic convention.

Second, he uses that variability to distinguish ordinary linguistic designation from the deeper claims of Tantric phonemic cosmology.

He knows perfectly well that languages change.

He knows that words can have different meanings in different places.

He's not some naïve mystic who thinks every sound always possesses one fixed universal meaning in ordinary human speech.

But he argues that **the sacred organization of phonemic powers requires more than arbitrary convention if it is to perform the role attributed to it**.

The distinction is essential.

We should not replace it with the simplistic claim:

"Sanskrit has the one true natural name for every object."

That's not what the present argument establishes.

Instead, Abhinavagupta is exploring how different symbolic and ritual arrangements can possess validity through the deeper power of consciousness.

And that leads straight into the problem of divine will.


## 10. Abhinavagupta refuses the easy answer

Now the master gets properly combative.

The argument has reached a dangerous place.

If the phoneme-tattva correspondences differ from scripture to scripture, we could say they're simply conventions.

But Abhinavagupta asks whether a wholly arbitrary convention can explain the ritual and metaphysical role attributed to these correspondences.

He explicitly rejects that reduction.

**न च साङ्केतिकमिदम्।**

*Na ca sāṅketikam idam.*

And this isn't merely conventional.

Now, to understand the force of the objection, remember what *adhvaśuddhi* is supposed to accomplish within the tradition.

The practitioner, through initiation and ritual purification, is brought into a transformed relationship with the levels of manifestation.

The phonemic correspondences are not merely labels in a textbook.

They participate in the ritual means by which bondage is interpreted and addressed.

If they're entirely arbitrary, what establishes their distinctive significance?

### Śodhya and Śodhaka

Two important words.

**शोध्य - śodhya**

That which is to be purified.

**शोधक - śodhaka**

That which purifies, the purifying instrument or agent.

Both belong to the root-family of:

**शुध् - √śudh**

To become pure, clean or purified.

The causative:

**शोधयति - śodhayati**

He or she purifies.

Now look at the difference.

The *-ya* formation in *śodhya* expresses something requiring or suitable for the action.

The *-aka* formation in *śodhaka* expresses an agent or instrument associated with doing it.

So the ritual distinction is:

That which undergoes purification.

And the means through which purification is performed.

Now imagine two ritual systems assigning completely different mantras to the same structure of reality.

If neither correspondence has any basis beyond arbitrary personal preference, what gives either system a particular purifying relation to that structure?

That is the difficulty.

Abhinavagupta isn't establishing empirical proof that mantra rituals produce their traditional supernatural effects.

He's arguing from the internal premises of Śaiva ritual theory.

And within those premises, arbitrary convention seems insufficient.

But don't misunderstand him.

He doesn't deny that conventional signs exist.

Quite the opposite.

He asks what makes convention itself possible.

Which is a much more interesting question.


## 11. Even convention exists through divine freedom

Now comes the first major move toward resolution.

Abhinavagupta does not regard conventional designation as an independently existing force somehow operating outside supreme consciousness.

Conventions themselves are manifestations of conscious power.

The possibility that one community associates a sound with one meaning and another community associates the same sound with something else is not ultimately disconnected from Śiva's freedom.

In Trika terms:

**Convention too must have its reality within consciousness.**

That means the distinction between natural and conventional signification cannot establish a second ultimate principle outside Anuttara.

However, Abhinavagupta insists that this doesn't allow us to disregard the question of why a particular arrangement is effective or meaningful.

Otherwise we have explained nothing.

### The Sanskrit: Saṅketa versus Svātantrya

**सङ्केत**

saṅketa

A convention or established signifying relationship.

**स्वातन्त्र्य**

svātantrya

The sovereign freedom of universal consciousness.

The philosophical problem is the relation between them.

Ordinary conventions are contingent.

They may differ among languages, regions and communities.

But in Abhinavagupta's ontology, no convention becomes real by escaping consciousness.

Its occurrence depends upon the expressive freedom of consciousness.

Yet it still has a **particular structure**.

And that structure matters.

The fact that consciousness can manifest many different things doesn't mean that every differentiated manifestation must function identically.

Think about the letters:

**क - ka**

**ख - kha**

Both are velar consonants.

But ordinary Sanskrit phonology distinguishes their articulation.

*Ka* is unaspirated.

*Kha* is aspirated.

Their common place of articulation doesn't eliminate their difference.

Similarly, the universal ground of manifestation doesn't make every symbolic or ritual relation interchangeable at the practical level.

Different systems may possess different functions.

The deeper question is how those differences arise within divine freedom.

### An analogy from music

Imagine a composer.

The same set of notes can be arranged into different melodies.

The notes don't have to change their physical identities for their musical relations to change.

But you also cannot assume every arrangement produces the same musical effect.

Harmony, rhythm, structure and context matter.

Now Abhinavagupta's sacred phonemic claims are not identical to modern music theory.

But the analogy helps us understand the distinction.

**Freedom of arrangement is not the same as absence of structure.**

And this is the point he is moving toward.

The phonemic system can admit multiple legitimate arrangements without reducing them to meaningless accidents.

But the ultimate reason is not that the scripts are merely conventional.

It is that conscious manifestation itself is free.

And here someone raises an objection.

One so unbelievably lazy that Abhinavagupta practically comes out swinging.


## 12. “It's all Śiva's will, bro” - Abhinavagupta absolutely loses patience

Now imagine a philosopher saying:

"Why are you investigating these differences?"

"It's all divine will."

"Śiva can manifest anything."

"End of discussion."

Abhinavagupta is having absolutely none of it.

He anticipates the argument that divine will is complete and cannot properly be made subject to rational investigation.

Very well, he responds.

If that's the position, why bother studying the scriptures?

Why memorize them?

Why recite them?

Why explain them?

Why contemplate them?

Why maintain a guru lineage in which the meanings are carefully transmitted?

If divine will makes inquiry pointless, we might as well abandon the whole enterprise and remain silent.

The argument is devastating because it uses the opponent's premise against them.

### The key Sanskrit terms

**विचार - vicāra**

Examination, investigation, reflection, philosophical inquiry.

Related to *vi-* and √*car*, to move, proceed or examine.

And:

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness, examination, consideration.

From *vi-* and √*mṛś*, to touch, examine or apprehend.

Now be careful.

Ordinary *vicāra* is not simply identical with supreme *vimarśa*.

But in the context of philosophical practice, reflective examination can participate in the process through which misrecognition is removed.

Then:

**व्याख्यान - vyākhyāna**

Explanation, exposition, commentary.

From *vy-ā-√khyā*, to explain or make known.

And:

**अध्ययन - adhyayana**

Study, especially recitation or learning of scripture.

The commentary specifically discusses the activities of retaining, reading, explaining and investigating texts.

Now picture Abhinavagupta confronting someone who has spent years attending initiations but refuses to examine the actual meaning of a passage.

"Why do these scriptures disagree?"

"Divine will."

"What is the relation between the letters and the tattvas?"

"Divine will."

"How does one distinguish a valid interpretation from an arbitrary invention?"

"Divine will."

"Why have you been studying Sanskrit for fifteen years, then?"

Silence.

The man has philosophically cornered himself.

### The greatest objection

Abhinavagupta is not denying divine freedom.

That would contradict his entire metaphysics.

He is denying that **appealing to divine freedom excuses abandoning the meaningful examination of its manifestations**.

This is an essential distinction.

Suppose someone claims:

"Because consciousness is free, no causal or conceptual analysis has any value."

But the very analysis is also an expression of consciousness.

So why arbitrarily exclude it?

And if divine freedom manifests distinct systems of teaching, it may also manifest the reasoning through which their relationships are understood.

**The freedom of Śiva includes the possibility of inquiry.**

That's the crucial move.

Not merely freedom to produce different sacred alphabets.

Freedom to reveal their coherence.

And Abhinavagupta explicitly ties this to **anugraha**, divine grace.


## 13. Grace doesn't mean lying down and refusing to think

This is a particularly wonderful part of the commentary.

Abhinavagupta considers the idea that liberation depends upon Śiva's grace.

Very good.

But how does that grace become manifest?

It may manifest precisely as the capacity for increasingly subtle reflection and understanding.

The Sanskrit:

**अनुग्रह - anugraha**

Grace, favour, benevolent support.

The word is traditionally analysed through:

**anu-** - following, along with, toward.

And a formation related to √**grah**, to grasp, receive or take hold.

The complete word means grace or favourable action.

Now remember the three *upāyas*.

Āṇava.

Śākta.

Śāmbhava.

And the teaching of *anupāya*, the absence of a deliberate means in the highest immediacy.

None of this requires the practitioner to treat every reflective activity as a metaphysical mistake.

Different capacities and different manifestations of grace can make different means appropriate.

Abhinavagupta's point is that a refined power of inquiry can itself arise through grace.

### The Sanskrit: Dhī

**धी - dhī**

Intelligence, understanding, thought, insight.

And:

**धीषण - dhīṣaṇa**

Intelligence, discernment, wise understanding.

These words appear in discussions of the capacity to comprehend subtle matters.

Abhinavagupta describes an increasingly refined capacity for *vimarśa*, reflective understanding.

The student is not supposed to remain permanently satisfied with crude interpretations simply because Śiva is omnipotent.

The capacity to investigate more precisely belongs to the manifestation of consciousness.

And if that capacity is available, refusing to use it can itself become an obstacle.

### The lazy yogin

Abhinavagupta makes the criticism almost comically concrete.

A person might lie comfortably, stretch out, eat, and avoid the effort of subtle understanding.

He refuses to present that as the necessary implication of supreme grace.

Now, there are legitimate contemplative traditions emphasizing effortless repose.

But his objection concerns **the misuse of divine freedom as an excuse for intellectual inertia**.

Not every person reclining after lunch has attained Śāmbhavopāya.

Sometimes a man has simply eaten too much.

The distinction is worth preserving.

### Why reasoning matters

This leads to a deeper philosophical point.

Reasoning is not outside consciousness.

Interpretation is not outside consciousness.

The apparent contradictions among scriptures are not independent substances threatening the Absolute.

They are differentiated appearances within the very reality being studied.

Therefore they can be examined.

The inquiry can reveal how seemingly contradictory structures belong to different manifestations of one conscious freedom.

Abhinavagupta is effectively saying:

**Don't invoke the highest truth in order to avoid understanding the lower distinctions through which that truth is manifest.**

And this is precisely why he is willing to undertake the enormous commentary.

He isn't interested in merely repeating that everything is Śiva.

He wants to show, with philosophical precision, how the differentiated systems can be understood in relation to that reality.

Now, finally, he begins the explicit resolution.

; \]


## 14. The answer - There is no ultimate sequence

Now we arrive at the central statement.

The question was:

Why does one text arrange Śiva, Earth and Śakti in one order, while others reverse or rearrange them?

Abhinavagupta's reply is startling.

At the supreme level, there is no sequence in the ordinary sense.

The ordering belongs to the manifestation and its explanation.

The fundamental divine freedom is **akrama**, nonsequential.

### Krama - क्रम

Sequence, order, succession.

From √*kram*, to step or proceed.

### Akrama - अक्रम

Without sequence.

The prefix **a-** negates the following noun or adjective.

So:

**a + krama → akrama**.

Now here's a common mistake.

People hear "nonsequential" and assume it means a state in which nothing happens.

But Abhinavagupta has already established the opposite.

The supreme consciousness possesses **kriyāśakti**, the power of activity.

What it lacks is dependence upon an independently existing temporal mechanism.

Its activity is not reducible to an external chain of causes that existed before consciousness.

That's the distinction.

### Svātantrya is not bound by the chart

The divine freedom contains unlimited potential variety.

The Sanskrit concept:

**वैचित्र्य - vaicitrya**

Variety, diversity, multiplicity, the condition of being variegated.

Related to:

**चित्र - citra**

Variegated, wonderful, distinct, many-coloured.

And:

**विचित्र - vicitra**

Varied, extraordinary, diverse.

So *vaicitrya* indicates diversity or manifoldness.

The commentary speaks of an immeasurable diversity contained within supreme freedom.

Now put that together with *akrama*.

The Absolute is not one object forced to march through a pre-existing sequence.

Rather, sequential relations themselves are manifestations of its freedom.

The sequence from Earth to Śiva is possible.

The sequence from Śiva to Earth is possible.

Different groupings of powers are possible.

And none establishes an ultimate order outside consciousness to which consciousness must submit.

That's the ontological resolution.

But notice that it is not simply an assertion that every arbitrary mapping is equally appropriate for every purpose.

At the level of manifested systems, distinctions remain.

And those distinctions can be meaningful.

**The absence of an ultimate compulsory sequence does not eliminate the structured relationships appearing within particular sequences.**

This is the crucial philosophical distinction.

And the way Abhinavagupta develops it leads directly back to the Trika triad.


## 15. The three Śaktis stand above the hierarchy they manifest

Now Abhinavagupta introduces the three Goddess powers again.

**अपरा - Aparā**

**परापरा - Parāparā**

**परा - Parā**

But look at how they're being used here.

They are not simply three items inserted near the top of the thirty-six tattvas.

Their divine reality transcends the particular hierarchy they manifest.

The commentary expressly treats the threefold power as exceeding even high principles such as Sadāśiva and Anāśritaśiva in the specific sense that those principles can themselves be represented within the ritual manifestation of the divine.

This requires a very important distinction.

**The divine power symbolized by the Goddess is not reducible to an item in the diagram that her own freedom makes possible.**

You can diagram a hierarchy of tattvas.

You can represent those tattvas through mantras.

You can organize them through ritual visualization.

But the universal freedom enabling the entire manifestation isn't simply another item trapped inside its own diagram.

Now there's a rather delightful analogy here.

Imagine a painter drawing a mountain.

At the summit of the painted mountain, he draws the highest palace.

Then someone says:

"The painter must live inside the palace because it's the highest object in the painting."

No, mate.

You've confused the contents of the painting with the power that produced them.

The same mistake occurs when a supreme divine principle is imagined as merely one more location within a system of representations.

### The Śakti-triad

Again, the three powers can be interpreted through will, knowledge and action.

But now we need to understand them through their relationship to manifestation.

**Parā** expresses the undivided supreme power.

**Parāparā** expresses a condition in which unity and differentiation are simultaneously manifest.

**Aparā** expresses the differentiated forms of experience.

Now the critical point:

These are not three completely separate things.

Nor are they three temporal stages that must literally unfold in a fixed external sequence.

They're distinguishable modalities of the one divine freedom.

Thus different arrangements can place them in different relationships without requiring the destruction of the underlying nonduality.

### Why the ritual seat matters

Abhinavagupta illustrates this through the symbolism of the divine seat or *āsana*.

**आसन - āsana**

Seat, sitting-place, posture.

Related to √*ās*, to sit or dwell.

Now this is not merely the yoga posture meaning of *āsana*.

In Tantric ritual visualization, an *āsana* can be an elaborate symbolic structure supporting the deity.

Different principles are arranged as parts of that structure.

And the Goddess is conceived as its presiding power.

The hierarchy becomes a seat.

The seat supports the deity.

But the deity isn't reduced to the seat.

Now that's a lovely way of expressing the distinction.

The manifested structure can be arranged in a particular order for a ritual purpose.

Yet the divine reality manifesting through that structure is not confined by it.

This is why Abhinavagupta invokes such examples in the present argument.

**A ritual hierarchy can be meaningful without becoming an ultimate restriction upon consciousness.**

And now he develops one of the more extraordinary examples: a divine seat involving the *mahāpreta*, the great corpse.


## 16. Mahāpreta - The great corpse that supports the Goddess

Now this is an astonishing bit of Tantric symbolism.

Abhinavagupta cites a description of a divine seat involving a form of Īśvara or Sadāśiva as **mahāpreta**.

Let's dismantle that.

**महाप्रेत - mahāpreta**

- **mahā** - great.
- **preta** - departed, dead, a deceased being or corpse, depending on context.

In this ritual symbolism, the *mahāpreta* forms part of the seat supporting the higher divine powers.

Now, don't imagine Abhinavagupta is telling you to acquire an actual corpse and use it as furniture.

He's discussing the construction of a ritual and contemplative representation.

And the symbolism is metaphysically loaded.

A particular divine level may be represented as the inert support relative to the higher expressive Śakti.

But its designation as a corpse doesn't mean that absolute consciousness itself has become genuinely unconscious.

It's a **functional representation within the ritual hierarchy**.

### A textual variant

The transmitted passage contains a variation concerning whether the great corpse is characterized as *acetana*, unconscious, or *sacetana*, conscious.

The Devanāgarī transcription underlying the Kashmir Series includes the *acetana* reading here, while another online transcription gives *sacetana*.

I'm distinguishing them because this isn't a trivial spelling difference. It affects the literal description.

The important interpretive point remains the ritual positioning of the divine figure as the seat or support relative to higher Śakti. ; \]

### What the seat contains

The ritual structure extends through a series of supports and powers.

It includes the imagery of a base, a stalk, a knot, a lotus, a deity, and a trident-like arrangement rising above the seat.

The Sanskrit vocabulary is worth learning.

**मूल - mūla**

Root, foundation.

**कन्द - kanda**

Bulb, rootstock or base.

**दण्ड - daṇḍa**

Staff, stalk, rod.

**ग्रन्थि - granthi**

Knot.

**पद्म - padma**

Lotus.

**शूल - śūla**

Spear, point, trident component.

You can see why the imagery is so evocative.

The entire tattva-order becomes an elaborate ritual body.

Its higher structures culminate in divine powers.

The Goddess is not merely placed within the same category as the objects forming the seat.

She presides over the whole arrangement.

And that is precisely why this example appears in the argument about contradictory tattva sequences.

The same principles can function as parts of a ritual structure without becoming independently ultimate realities.

### The trident

Abhinavagupta describes the rising trident through subtle powers, including Śakti, Vyāpinī and Samanā in the particular ritual construction.

The trident is not simply a decorative weapon.

It symbolizes the ascent beyond progressively differentiated levels.

And the associated teaching describes the condition of the practitioner entering Khecarī through the transcendence of the limiting sequence.

Now compare this with what we learned in Part III.

Khecarī isn't merely a flying goddess.

She represents a supreme power of consciousness.

And *khecarīsamatā* concerns identity with that divine power.

The trident symbolism therefore reconnects the ritual structure with the original question of the Parātrīśikā.

**How does the limited experiencer attain the divine condition?**

The ultimate answer isn't by physically climbing a cosmological object.

It's through recognition of the reality represented by the hierarchy.

The hierarchy is an instrument of teaching and contemplation.

The divine freedom it expresses is not imprisoned within the instrument.

And that brings us to the great question of the levels of speech.


## 17. Somānanda versus the grammarians - Which level of speech is truly supreme?

Now Abhinavagupta cites Somānanda again.

And this is a particularly interesting intellectual disagreement.

The grammarian-philosophical traditions had developed sophisticated accounts of the levels of speech.

Including:

**पश्यन्ती - Paśyantī**

The visionary or seeing level.

But Somānanda challenges the idea that Paśyantī, considered as the highest level within a particular linguistic scheme, fully captures the supreme reality recognized by Trika.

The distinction is crucial.

A level may be the highest **within one system** without being identical with the unsurpassable in every possible sense.

Remember the opening sixteen interpretations of Anuttara?

The Absolute isn't simply the top item in a ladder.

And the same warning applies here.

Paśyantī may be extraordinarily subtle.

Yet Abhinavagupta distinguishes the supreme Parā from the differentiated powers of speech that she grounds.

### Paśyantī - पश्यन्ती

From √*paś*, to see.

A feminine present participial formation.

She who sees.

And in the theory of speech, a level at which word and meaning are not yet fully articulated as separate entities.

### Parā - परा

Supreme, transcendent.

Here the undivided power of speech, grounded in universal consciousness.

Now the difference.

Paśyantī can be described through the emergence of a subtle articulation.

Parā is the undivided ground in which even that emergence is possible.

This is not a dispute about which Sanskrit word sounds more impressive.

It's a dispute about the relation between differentiated and undifferentiated awareness.

And the moment you understand that, the entire argument about different alphabetic sequences becomes considerably clearer.

**No individual differentiated sequence can exhaust the ground of all possible sequences.**

Even a very subtle sequence.

Even a sacred sequence.

Even one received from an authoritative scripture.

The supreme Śakti remains beyond its limitation.


## 18. अहम् इदम् - “I am this”: The great reunion of subject and object

And now Abhinavagupta brings us to one of the most important formulations in the Pratyabhijñā tradition.

The relationship between:

**अहम् - aham**

I.

And:

**इदम् - idam**

This.

You've encountered these terms throughout our readings.

Now they're about to do the heavy lifting.

### Ordinary experience

You look at a tree.

You say:

"I see that tree."

There is a subject.

And an object.

The subject is apprehended as here.

The tree as there.

Two distinguishable poles.

Now, in ordinary life, that difference is useful.

You don't want to confuse yourself with the tree and walk into it.

But the metaphysical question is whether the distinction establishes two independently existing ultimate realities.

Abhinavagupta says no.

And he explains the higher states through different relationships between *aham* and *idam*.

### Aham-idam - अहमिदम्

I am this.

A formulation in which the apparent object is apprehended within the unity of conscious subjectivity.

Now be precise.

This does not mean that the individual psychological ego is literally the physical tree.

It's about universal consciousness, not personal self-inflation.

The *aham* at issue is the supreme subjectivity.

And the *idam* is the manifested universe.

The relation expresses their underlying identity.

### Idam-aham - इदमहम्

This am I.

Another formulation, emphasizing the differentiated object within the unity of awareness.

The higher Śaiva states can differ according to whether the *I* or *this* aspect predominates.

In the standard Pratyabhijñā presentation, Sadāśiva is associated with a predominance of I-consciousness and a less articulated objective pole, while Īśvara is associated with a more pronounced manifestation of the "this."

But the two are not independent consciousnesses.

They are distinguishable modes of supreme self-revelation.

### Sāmānādhikaraṇya - सामानाधिकरण्य

Now here's the word I really want you to learn.

**सामानाधिकरण्य - sāmānādhikaraṇya**

Co-reference, common locus, or the condition in which different expressions refer to the same underlying referent.

This is a technical grammatical and philosophical concept.

Break it into:

**समान - samāna**

Same, equal, common.

**अधिकरण - adhikaraṇa**

Locus, substrate, basis.

And the abstract derivative:

**सामानाधिकरण्य - sāmānādhikaraṇya**

The condition of having a common locus or referent.

Now imagine saying:

"This person is the teacher."

Two descriptions.

One referent.

The person.

And the teacher.

The descriptions differ, but they can refer to the same individual.

In Abhinavagupta's metaphysical application, *aham* and *idam* can be understood as apprehended within the same conscious ground.

Their apparent distinction does not require two ultimate substances.

And this is the crucial point.

The distinction can manifest without being ultimately independent.

### The problem with Māyā

At the level of limited subjectivity, the knower and known are apprehended as separately situated.

The "I" belongs to the subject.

The "this" belongs to the object.

And the individual experiences their distinction as ultimate.

But in the higher modes of awareness, the apparent object is apprehended within the same universal consciousness as the subject.

The commentary's treatment of Śuddhavidyā, Īśvara and Sadāśiva involves precisely this transformation of the subject-object relationship.

Now look how the argument about different alphabets has unfolded.

We started with different arrangements of phonemes.

Then different ritual systems.

Then the relationship between conventional signs and ultimate reality.

Then divine freedom.

Then the levels of speech.

And finally, the relationship between "I" and "this."

That wasn't an accidental detour.

The issue is the same throughout.

**How can differentiation be real as manifestation without establishing ultimate separation?**

That is the question underlying the whole doctrine.

; \]


## 19. The water and flame analogy - Difference within non-difference

Now Abhinavagupta gives a memorable comparison.

The realities appear inseparably, like water within water or flame within flame.

Think of pouring water into water.

You may distinguish the two portions before they meet.

But once combined, you cannot identify an independently separated region of water merely by insisting that one portion used to belong to a different container.

Similarly, one flame can be brought into contact with another.

Their luminous reality is not divided into independently sealed substances by the conceptual fact that we described them separately.

Now, the analogy is limited.

Water and flame are physical phenomena governed by ordinary causal processes.

Abhinavagupta is discussing universal consciousness.

But the image helps illustrate the relationship between differentiation and non-difference.

The manifested forms aren't merely unrelated objects standing outside consciousness.

Their reality is inseparable from the underlying luminosity.

### Prakāśa and Vimarśa return

**प्रकाश - prakāśa**

Luminosity, manifestation.

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness, self-apprehension.

The distinction between these is analytically useful.

But the supreme consciousness cannot be reduced to a light that illuminates objects while remaining entirely unaware of itself.

It is self-revealing.

Its power of manifestation and its reflexive awareness are inseparable.

That is why Abhinavagupta insists that the supreme reality isn't merely an undifferentiated blank.

It is capable of manifesting all possible difference.

Without ceasing to be itself.

### The great danger of the mirror analogy

Now here's something especially interesting.

Trika frequently uses the image of the universe reflected within a mirror.

The mirror remains itself.

Different forms appear within it.

And their appearance doesn't require the mirror to become each object in the ordinary physical sense.

But Abhinavagupta's argument here is even stronger.

He doesn't want the universe to be understood merely as a collection of reflections standing apart from the conscious ground.

He insists upon their inseparable reality within consciousness.

The world isn't an independent image pasted onto an unrelated substance.

It is the manifestation of Śiva's own freedom.

And that brings us back to the earlier question.

Why can the alphabetic mappings differ?

Because the differentiated structures of manifestation are not independently fixed conditions imposed upon consciousness.

They are expressions of its freedom.

And those expressions may be organized differently according to their mode and purpose.

**Different orderings can disclose one reality because their validity is grounded in the very freedom through which difference manifests.**

That is the heart of Abhinavagupta's solution.

But there is one more astonishing implication.


## 20. Every tattva contains the entire cosmos

Now the argument moves toward something even more radical.

If every manifested principle is ultimately grounded in the same universal consciousness, can we really say that one principle exists in absolute isolation from the others?

Abhinavagupta argues against such independence.

The structure of manifestation is relational and inclusive.

And he begins developing the insight that every tattva, properly understood, participates in the totality.

This doesn't mean Earth and Water are empirically indistinguishable.

Nor that the conventional classification is meaningless.

It means that no principle ultimately exists independently of the conscious ground and the relations necessary for its manifestation.

### The example of Earth

**पृथिवी - pṛthivī**

Earth.

In the tattva scheme, the gross element associated with solidity and the five sensory qualities.

Now, Abhinavagupta asks whether Earth could manifest without the subtler conditions upon which its reality depends.

The gross element presupposes subtle qualities.

Those qualities are related to the senses.

The senses depend upon more fundamental structures of experience.

The individual subject depends upon the limiting powers.

And those powers ultimately depend upon universal consciousness.

So Earth isn't an independently self-sufficient substance floating around outside the entire metaphysical order.

Its manifested nature includes a relationship to the principles that ground it.

### The Sanskrit: Sarvasarvātmaka

**सर्वसर्वात्मक - sarvasarvātmaka**

An exceptionally important Trika expression.

Break it down:

**सर्व - sarva**

All, everything.

Repeated:

**सर्वसर्व - sarvasarva**

All in all, the entirety in each and every aspect.

And:

**आत्मक - ātmaka**

Having the nature of, consisting of, essentially characterized by.

So *sarvasarvātmaka* expresses the all-pervading, all-inclusive nature of manifestation.

Now, this is not simply a statement that every physical thing literally contains miniature copies of all other physical things.

The doctrine concerns the metaphysical inseparability of manifested principles within the universal reality.

The world is not a pile of independently existing ontological bricks.

Each principle has its particular character.

Yet its existence depends upon the broader order and ultimately upon consciousness.

### The first and the last

Now here's a lovely insight.

One could approach the totality from the highest principle downward.

Or begin with the most differentiated principle and trace its dependence upward.

Either direction can disclose the same underlying reality.

And Abhinavagupta connects this with the structure of cognition itself.

The apprehension of a complete object doesn't necessarily proceed by first perceiving every isolated component in strict sequence.

Often the whole scene is apprehended, and its parts are subsequently distinguished.

Which leads to one of his most beautiful practical analogies.


## 21. The landscape - A surprisingly direct example of nonconceptual awareness

Abhinavagupta asks us to consider an expansive field of vision.

A landscape.

A forest.

A mountain.

Or an open region in which the view is not narrowly obstructed.

Imagine standing on a hill.

There's a vast landscape before you.

Trees.

Sky.

A distant river.

The shapes of mountains.

And perhaps a settlement far away.

Now at first, the scene may be apprehended as one integrated visual field.

Then you begin to distinguish:

"That's a tree."

"That's a building."

"There's the river."

"That mountain looks far away."

The differentiated cognitions arise.

But they don't necessarily create the original whole from nothing.

They articulate what was already manifest in awareness.

### Avikalpa-saṃvid

**अविकल्पसंविद् - avikalpasaṃvid**

Nonconceptually determined awareness.

We've already studied *vikalpa*, conceptual construction.

Now:

**a- + vikalpa → avikalpa**

Without conceptual differentiation.

And:

**saṃvid**

Consciousness, awareness.

So the compound describes awareness that is not yet structured through explicit conceptual determinations.

But remember the distinction.

This is not automatically an unconscious blank.

Nor does the absence of verbal labels prove that somebody has attained final liberation.

The issue is the relation between the whole field of awareness and its later differentiated apprehensions.

### A particularly beautiful passage

Abhinavagupta gives an example from a wide visual field and connects it with a teaching on placing the gaze in an open area without distracting nearby objects.

The aim is to illustrate an apprehension of the visual field not initially broken into isolated conceptual objects.

He uses this to help explain **how the last or complete manifestation can be apprehended as a whole before its constituent distinctions are explicitly identified**.

Now see why this matters to the alphabetic dispute.

An ordered system can be explained one principle at a time.

But the conscious reality underlying the entire system isn't necessarily produced at the end of that explanation.

Just as the complete scene doesn't arise only after you've named every tree.

The whole can be apprehended first.

Then the distinctions articulated.

This is an especially important connection with *pratibhā*, intuitive illumination.

The flash of apprehension isn't simply a linear sum of prior conceptual operations.

The power of awareness includes the capacity to manifest an integrated whole.

### The ridiculous intellectual trap

Now imagine somebody standing before a forest.

They carefully identify:

Tree one.

Tree two.

Tree three.

Rock one.

Rock two.

River.

Sky.

Mountain.

And then declare:

"I have finally assembled the forest."

My brother.

The forest was there before you finished making the list.

Likewise, the differentiated tattva-system does not manufacture the totality of consciousness.

It articulates relations within manifestation.

The universal ground is not dependent upon your successful completion of the metaphysical inventory.

That's why Abhinavagupta can defend alternative classifications without surrendering the unity of the doctrine.

### The limits of the analogy

Now this mustn't be oversimplified.

An integrated visual experience is still a conditioned experience.

A forest is not identical to universal consciousness as an ordinary empirical object.

And nonconceptual seeing isn't automatically recognition of Śiva.

The example serves a narrower philosophical purpose.

It shows how unity of apprehension can coexist with the possibility of differentiated conceptual knowledge.

Abhinavagupta uses that structure to illuminate the relationship between Parā Vāk and the differentiated manifestations of the alphabet.

**The order in which distinctions are recognized need not be the order in which their common ground comes into existence.**

And with that, the contradiction between the various arrangements becomes considerably less threatening.

## 22. The conclusion - Śiva has not signed a contract with the alphabet

Now return to the original problem.

The Parātrīśikā places the phonemes in one order.

The Mālinīvijayottara provides others.

The Svacchandatantra uses alternative mappings.

Mālinī rearranges the phonemes through a different generative matrix.

And the three Goddess powers employ distinct ritual applications.

How can these coexist?

Abhinavagupta's answer requires several distinctions.

First, the supreme consciousness is not subject to a compulsory temporal or phonemic sequence independent of itself.

Second, the manifested universe can be organized and apprehended through different procedures.

Third, those procedures can possess real doctrinal and ritual significance within their particular systems, rather than being dismissed as arbitrary nonsense.

Fourth, their reality ultimately depends upon the sovereign freedom of Śiva.

And fifth, recognizing divine freedom does not make serious grammatical, philosophical and textual investigation unnecessary.

In fact, the capacity for such investigation can itself be understood as a manifestation of grace.

Now that's a genuinely sophisticated solution.

He doesn't simply declare that every manuscript is equally correct about everything.

Nor does he insist that the universe possesses one rigid cosmic alphabet that no tradition is permitted to rearrange.

He grounds the diversity of the systems in the universal creative freedom of consciousness.

And the theological consequence is enormous.

**No individual pattern of manifestation can exhaust Anuttara.**

The supreme reality is not the prisoner of its own expressions.

### Why this isn't relativism

It's worth distinguishing Abhinavagupta's position from the modern claim that everything is merely a matter of perspective.

He does not say that the tattva systems are empty inventions with no objective spiritual significance.

His framework maintains that the sacred arrangements are connected with real divine powers.

But their relations can differ because those powers manifest in multiple legitimate ways.

The conclusion depends upon the metaphysical premises of Trika.

It isn't an empirical demonstration that every traditional ritual claim is correct.

Nor is it a license to invent arbitrary Sanskrit arrangements and call them authentic Mālinī.

Textual transmission, ritual context, grammatical constraints and lineage authority remain relevant.

**The freedom of the Absolute does not remove the need to distinguish a real scriptural teaching from somebody making things up.**

Abhinavagupta would be the first to tear an incoherent interpretation to pieces.

We've already seen him do precisely that.


## 23. The philosophical synthesis - Recognition isn't the final item in a sequence

Here's the insight I particularly want to retain from this installment.

The ordinary mind imagines realization as a series.

First I learn Sanskrit.

Then I study the tattvas.

Then I master mantra.

Then I purify the body.

Then I understand the subtle powers.

And at the very end, perhaps, I obtain Śiva.

But Abhinavagupta's analysis repeatedly challenges the assumption that the ultimate reality is a product of such a sequence.

The sequence may be useful.

The learning may be necessary for particular students.

The practice may transform the conditions of recognition.

Yet the reality being recognized is not created by those processes.

And the very ability to proceed through them depends upon consciousness.

Now compare this with the first verses of the text.

The Goddess asked how Anuttara gives Kaulika perfection immediately.

Bhairava said the method is established in the space of his Heart.

And now the phonemic cosmology unfolds the relationship between that Heart and the entire differentiated universe.

Different arrangements of sound become intelligible because they are not independent of the freedom of consciousness.

The whole argument is coming together.

**Anuttara is not only the first item before manifestation; it is the reality through which every possible manifestation is present.**

And this is why the *a* matters so much.

Not because the written character अ possesses a mysterious power independent of consciousness.

But because the tradition uses it as the symbol of the reality that is both the ground and the unsurpassable fullness of manifestation.

### The important Sanskrit distinction

**क्रम - krama**

Sequence.

**अक्रम - akrama**

Nonsequence.

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Freedom.

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness.

**सर्वात्मकत्व - sarvātmakatva**

The condition of being all or having the nature of the totality.

Now put them together.

The supreme consciousness possesses freedom.

That freedom manifests sequences.

Those sequences are apprehended through awareness.

But the reality of awareness is not constituted by being one more event within the sequence.

And the totality of manifestation remains grounded in the same universal consciousness.

The alphabet is a particularly elaborate way of expressing that relationship.

And the disagreement between sacred alphabets becomes an opportunity to demonstrate it.

Quite an extraordinary reversal.

What initially looked like a devastating contradiction becomes the occasion for a deeper account of nondual freedom.

### Three writing exercises

**1. The negative prefix**

## भिन्न → अभिन्न

bhinna → abhinna

The initial **अ** negates the adjective. Notice the conjunct **न्न = न् + न**.


**2. The locative form of pha**

## फ → फे

pha → phe

The vowel sign **े** represents *e*. In the cited expression it marks the locative of a phoneme-name.


**3. The three grammatical persons**

## सः - त्वम् - अहम्

saḥ - tvam - aham

He - You - I. Remember how Abhinavagupta relates them to Nara, Śakti and Śiva.

One particularly worthwhile compound is:

**सर्वसर्वात्मकत्वम् - sarvasarvātmakatvam**

Break it into:

*Sarva + sarva + ātmaka + tva + am.*

Everything.

In everything.

The condition of having the nature of the totality.

And with the final **-am**, the form can function as a neuter singular noun.

The word is a miniature statement of the entire metaphysical doctrine we've been studying.

**The universe is not ultimately a sequence that consciousness must obey. Sequence is one of the ways consciousness manifests its freedom.**

That is the philosophical heart of Part VII.

Not that every order is meaningless.

Not that sacred sounds can be arbitrarily rearranged without consequence.

The man really does refuse to lose.
