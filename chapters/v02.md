# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART II - The Sixteen Meanings of Anuttara

*Abhinavagupta explains one Sanskrit word sixteen times, dismantles the spiritual hierarchy, interrogates the entire business of liberation, and eventually concludes that the Absolute cannot be improved by anything whatsoever.*

Continuing the commentary on Parātrīśikā 1 · Kashmir Series edition, pp. 19-32


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

The Goddess had asked Bhairava:

**अनुत्तरं कथं देव सद्यः कौलिकसिद्धिदम्।**

*Anuttaraṃ kathaṃ deva sadyaḥ kaulikasiddhidam.*

How, O Lord, does Anuttara immediately grant Kaulika perfection?

Straightforward question, really.

How does recognition of the Supreme liberate you?

And we've reached the point where Abhinavagupta finally begins explaining the actual word *Anuttara*.

Now, remember, we're dealing with a philosopher who can take a perfectly ordinary Sanskrit syllable and unfold an entire cosmology from it.

He's not about to say, "Anuttara means unsurpassable, next question."

Oh, no.

That would be philosophically irresponsible.

Instead, **he develops sixteen interpretations**, gradually dismantling what it means to be higher, lower, bound, liberated, speaking, knowing, meditating, initiating, breathing, and even acting in time.

The number sixteen is significant in Trika's symbolism of fullness, particularly its associations with the sixteen vowel-powers and the complete unfolding of Śakti. This does not mean each of the sixteen explanations corresponds one-to-one to a particular vowel.

A textual detail before we begin: Abhinavagupta's Sanskrit develops these meanings continuously rather than setting them out as sixteen numbered textbook definitions. I'll use sixteen numbered interpretive divisions, cross-checked against the original passage, while keeping his sequence. We'll distinguish his ordinary grammatical explanations from his deliberate Tantric reinterpretations of syllables. ; ; \]

And we're going to learn the Sanskrit properly.

Not "this mystical word vaguely means consciousness."

I mean the consonants, the vowel signs, the actual case endings, the compound formations, and why a little bloody dot or missing vowel can completely change how you read something.

Right.

Into the abyss.

## First, dismantle the word

# अनुत्तर

anuttara

# अन्

*an-*

Negative prefix

# उत्तर

*uttara*

Higher, further, answer

Ordinary grammatical analysis: an- + uttara → anuttara, unsurpassable

The Devanāgarī is worth examining.

**अ + नु + त् + त + र**

But don't mistake that sequence of written syllabic units for the word's grammatical division.

The correct grammatical division is:

**अन् + उत्तर**

The negative prefix *an-* ends with a consonant. *Uttara* begins with the vowel *u*.

Their combination produces *anuttara*.

Now, the two consecutive t's are already part of *uttara*.

They're not introduced by the negative prefix.

Look:

- **नु - nu**: न with the short-*u* vowel sign ु below it.
- **त् - t**: त with the virāma ्, suppressing its inherent *a*.
- **त - ta**: त with its inherent *a* retained.
- **र - ra**: र with its inherent *a*.

Together **त्तर** gives *ttara*.

This is the distinction you discovered when learning how the inherent *a* works: consonant letters contain an *a* unless another vowel replaces it or the consonant is joined without it.

Now comes the absolutely delicious complication.

Abhinavagupta will eventually stop treating *anuttara* solely as **an + uttara**.

He starts exploiting the possibilities of its constituent sounds.

*An*, *a*, *nut*, *tara*.

And he draws on different Sanskrit verbal roots and traditional interpretive possibilities to give those sounds additional meanings.

This is called **nirvacana**, explanatory etymological interpretation.

And it's important to understand that this is not necessarily historical linguistic derivation.

He isn't claiming that all the meanings could be recovered from a modern dictionary's account of how the word originated.

He is reading the sacred word as an expression of consciousness, allowing its phonetic structure to disclose different philosophical relations.

In other words, the Sanskrit word has become a small metaphysical machine.

Let's turn the bloody thing on.


## Interpretation 1 - Nothing is higher than it

अनुत्तर AS THE UNSURPASSABLE

The commentary begins:

**न विद्यते उत्तरमधिकं यतः।**

*Na vidyate uttaram adhikaṃ yataḥ.*

There is nothing further or superior to it.

The literal breakdown:

| Sanskrit | Function | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *na* | Negative particle | Not |
| *vidyate* | Present, third-person singular | Exists, is found |
| *uttaram* | Neuter singular | Higher, further |
| *adhikam* | Neuter singular | Greater, additional |
| *yataḥ* | Relative form | Than which, from which |

Notice *vidyate*.

Its root is √*vid*, associated here with existing or being found. Don't mechanically translate every appearance of √*vid* as "to know"; Sanskrit has different verbal formations and meanings.

Now the philosophy.

Abhinavagupta invokes the thirty-six *tattvas*.

You've already encountered these in the Tantrasāra, so we can go straight to their significance.

The tattvas are the graded principles through which the universal reality is understood as manifesting differentiated experience, from Śiva and Śakti down through Māyā, mind, senses, and the gross elements.

Now ask: what accounts for the reality of those tattvas?

Within Trika, none possesses ultimate independence from consciousness.

The tattvas can appear, function, and be differentiated because their reality is grounded in Bhairava's self-manifestation.

But supreme consciousness doesn't need a thirty-seventh metaphysical substance to explain why *it* exists.

Otherwise we'd have a delightful philosophical disaster.

Imagine:

"Śiva is the ultimate reality."

"Wonderful. What makes Śiva real?"

"Ultra-Śiva."

"And what makes Ultra-Śiva real?"

"Premium Śiva."

Congratulations, you've invented an infinite subscription-based ontology.

Abhinavagupta is having absolutely none of it.

Anuttara is not one more item requiring a higher foundation.

**It is that which does not depend upon a higher reality for its existence or manifestation.**

And this is where the word *adhika* matters.

It means more, additional, superior.

Nothing can be added to Parā Saṃvid in order to make it fully itself.

It's already complete.

Now be precise: this does not mean it lacks the power of creation, nor that it cannot manifest something apparently new.

It means manifestation does not supply consciousness with a perfection it previously lacked.

Śakti's creativity is an expression of fullness, not a desperate attempt to repair a metaphysical deficiency.

And then Abhinavagupta adds a marvellous description of the nature of supreme consciousness:

**अनर्गलानपेक्षप्रथाचमत्कार**

*Anargalānapekṣaprathācamatkāra.*

Let's pull this magnificent compound apart.

- **anargala** - unrestricted, unbarred.
- **anapekṣa** - not dependent on something else.
- **prathā** - manifestation, expansion, appearing.
- **camatkāra** - wonder, astonishment, the self-delighting immediacy of awareness.

*Anapekṣa* itself contains the negative prefix *an-* and *apekṣā*, expectation or dependence.

And *prathā* is related to √*prath*, to spread, become manifest.

The complete expression characterizes supreme consciousness as freely manifesting, independent and suffused with its own self-revelatory wonder.

Not a dead absolute.

Not an unconscious cosmic lump.

Not a blank mathematical zero.

A living, self-revealing fullness.

**First meaning: Anuttara is unsurpassable because there is nothing beyond or additional to supreme consciousness.**


## Interpretation 2 - There is no answer beyond it

उत्तर AS AN ANSWER

Now Abhinavagupta pivots.

*Uttara* can mean something higher.

But it also means an **answer**, a reply.

Suddenly the word changes character.

The Goddess asked a question, and Bhairava is going to answer.

Yet Abhinavagupta points out that the consciousness giving rise to the question is already the same reality that makes the answer possible.

The disciple's question arises out of the immense ocean of consciousness.

The guru's answer also arises within that same consciousness.

What fundamentally distinct answer could exist outside it?

Picture an ocean producing a wave that asks:

"Excuse me, where can I find the water?"

And another wave replies:

"Well, according to scripture, you'll need to travel approximately three nautical miles beyond the reef."

My brother.

YOU ARE WET.

Of course, this analogy has limits. Ordinary waves do not possess the reflexive awareness Abhinavagupta attributes to consciousness.

But the philosophical point stands.

When the object of inquiry is the very consciousness enabling the inquiry, the question and answer are not ultimately separate from that reality.

### Sanskrit laboratory: प्रश्न and प्रतिवचन

**प्रश्न - praśna**: question.

Associated with *pra-* and √*prach*, to ask.

**प्रतिवचन - prativacana**: reply, answer.

- *prati* - back, in return, in response.
- *vacana* - speaking, utterance, statement.
- √*vac* - to speak.

Look at the Devanāgarī:

**प्र = प् + र**

Here *r* follows *p*.

But in **प्रश्न**, the final cluster **श्न** represents:

श् + न

The inherent vowel after श is suppressed.

You're learning to recognize not just vocabulary but the actual architecture of written Sanskrit.

Now here's the deeper problem.

A question presupposes something not yet known.

An answer brings about knowledge.

But universal consciousness is not constituted by the same ignorance that characterizes a limited questioner.

Nevertheless, it possesses the freedom to manifest the drama of inquiry and resolution.

The guru-disciple relationship is real as a manifestation.

It is not ultimately a relationship between two independently existing consciousnesses.

**Second meaning: Anuttara is that beyond which there is no further answer, because it is already the reality of both questioning and knowing.**


## Interpretation 3 - Liberation is not an escape journey

उत्तर AS CROSSING OVER

Now we get into the genuinely cheeky stuff.

Abhinavagupta draws on another semantic possibility.

**उत्तरणम् - uttaraṇam**

Crossing over.

The Sanskrit root here is √*tṝ*, to cross.

And this is the same root-family you encounter in words associated with passage, crossing, and deliverance.

Traditional religious language is full of this imagery.

You are trapped on the miserable shore of saṃsāra.

There's a river of ignorance.

Over there, on the other shore, is liberation.

So you must cross.

Very reasonable.

Abhinavagupta says:

Hang on, mate.

**Where exactly are you planning to go?**

He is criticizing an account of liberation conceived as the limited subject passing through successive ontological stages until it acquires Śiva's nature.

His example runs through a sequence of identifications:

**शरीर - śarīra**

The body

**प्राण - prāṇa**

Vital life

**बुद्धि - buddhi**

Intellect

**स्पन्दन - spandana**

Vibration, pulsation

**शून्य - śūnya**

The void

**शिवत्व - śivatva**

The state of Śiva

Now, the ascent through increasingly subtle levels can have genuine significance within Tantric practice.

But the critique concerns the assumption that the Absolute is a destination produced by the journey.

If Śiva is the underlying reality of every stage, the passage from body to prāṇa, from prāṇa to intellect, and from intellect to void cannot manufacture the divine consciousness that was otherwise missing.

This is an essential distinction between **a means of recognition** and **the production of reality itself**.

Consider your meditation.

Suppose you become increasingly absorbed, eventually reaching a state in which ordinary thoughts disappear.

You might say:

"Fantastic. I've reached the stage beyond thoughts. Now I must be closer to Śiva."

Abhinavagupta would ask whether the elimination of particular mental contents is itself sufficient to establish recognition of consciousness's universal nature.

It isn't.

The absence of thought is not automatically the recognition of the absolute.

An unconscious person is not necessarily a liberated sage.

A rock isn't enlightened merely because it lacks discursive thinking.

The reality of Śiva cannot depend on the production of one particular temporary mental state.

The *crossing* is thus reinterpreted.

Liberation is not the limited self travelling far enough to encounter something fundamentally external to its being.

It is the removal of the error by which consciousness takes itself to be merely that limited self.

And here's the wonderfully cruel implication.

You can spend years climbing spiritual ladders while secretly reinforcing the belief that your true nature is somewhere else.

Abhinavagupta isn't saying practices are useless.

He's saying **do not confuse the ladder with the reality it helps you recognize.**

Third meaning: Anuttara is beyond liberation conceived as crossing from one ontologically separate state into another.


## Interpretation 4 - You cannot climb into omnipresence

अनुत्तर BEYOND THE ASCENT THROUGH CAKRAS

Now Abhinavagupta turns to the yogic ascent itself.

And this is particularly interesting because Trika has an extraordinarily elaborate understanding of the subtle body.

We're talking nāḍīs, prāṇa, cakras, ascending awareness, the central channel, the cranial aperture, and increasingly subtle states of consciousness.

Abhinavagupta knows this material.

He's not some armchair philosopher announcing that yogic practice is nonsense because he's never managed to sit cross-legged for more than four minutes.

He is deeply acquainted with the tradition.

And he asks a devastating question.

**If consciousness is universal, what does it mean to travel upwards to reach it?**

He mentions a progression through subtle locations.

| Sanskrit | Literal meaning |
|--------|--------|
| **नाभि - nābhi** | Navel |
| **हृद् - hṛd** | Heart |
| **कण्ठ - kaṇṭha** | Throat |
| **तालु - tālu** | Palate |
| **ब्राह्म - brāhma** | Brahmic or cranial locus |
| **भैरवबिल - bhairavabila** | Aperture of Bhairava |

That last compound is magnificent.

**Bhairava + bila**

*Bila* means a hole, cavity, or opening.

**भैरवबिल - bhairavabila**

The opening associated with Bhairava.

Look at the written compound.

भै = भ with the vowel **ै**, representing *ai*.

र = *ra*.

व = *va*.

बि = ब with **ि**, representing short *i*.

ल = *la*.

That little *i* sign appears visually before its consonant, although you pronounce the consonant first.

That's another of the script's delightful tricks.

Now imagine a yogin who has learned to move awareness through all these locations.

He experiences tremendous changes in perception, bodily sensation and attention.

He finally approaches what he believes to be the highest centre.

And Abhinavagupta asks:

Where was consciousness during the journey?

At the navel?

Consciousness.

At the heart?

Consciousness.

At the throat?

Consciousness.

And when the process culminates?

Still consciousness.

The subtle locations and experiences can be important manifestations and instruments of practice. But they do not constitute spatial containers for an otherwise absent Absolute.

**The ascent may change the practitioner's realization. It does not change the spatial location of universal consciousness.**

In Trika, *āṇavopāya* often employs precisely such supports. The criticism is not that the means are illegitimate, but that no conditioned process can be the ultimate ontological foundation of Anuttara.

You cannot literally climb into that which has no outside.

Fourth meaning: Anuttara is not attained by upward movement through a succession of subtle bodily locations.


## Interpretation 5 - There is no ultimate bondage-and-liberation pair

बन्ध AND मोक्ष

Now Abhinavagupta pulls another semantic trick.

*Uttara* may be associated with that from which one crosses, and the crossing itself.

So he considers the paired notions of bondage and liberation.

**बन्ध - bandha**

Bondage, binding, confinement.

From √*bandh*, to bind.

**मोक्ष - mokṣa**

Release, liberation.

Related to √*muc*, to release or let go.

Now look at the problem.

Liberation ordinarily presupposes bondage.

You are bound.

Then something changes.

You become free.

But if this is an ultimate description of reality, what happened to Śiva before liberation?

Was supreme consciousness previously imprisoned?

Did the Absolute accidentally lose its cosmic keys?

Was Mahābhairava locked in the basement, begging the metaphysical landlord to let him out?

No.

The distinction belongs to the condition of the limited experiencer.

Bondage is not a second, independently existing reality with enough power to fundamentally alter Śiva.

And liberation is not the production of a second divine substance.

### The critical Trika distinction

At the level of the limited subject, bondage matters.

Misrecognition matters.

Practice matters.

Suffering matters.

Grace and recognition matter.

Abhinavagupta is not denying the lived significance of these distinctions.

But from the standpoint of Anuttara, the universal consciousness has not passed from a defective state to a perfected state.

It is always itself.

So the paradox is:

**The bound individual requires liberation, but the consciousness constituting the reality of that individual was never itself ultimately bound.**

And this is where Trika becomes rather different from a simplistic claim that everything is already perfect and therefore nothing needs doing.

The very freedom of Śiva includes the capacity to manifest limitation and the corresponding recognition of its real nature.

Fifth meaning: Anuttara lies beyond the ultimate opposition between bondage and liberation.

; \]


## Interpretation 6 - No description captures it

उत्तर AS A DETERMINING UTTERANCE

Abhinavagupta now turns against language itself.

*Uttara* can involve a statement, an utterance, something said.

But every ordinary description distinguishes its object from what it is not.

Suppose I say:

"Śiva is infinite."

Now we've used a predicate.

Infinite.

What does it mean?

Not finite.

Good.

Then I say:

"Śiva is consciousness."

Now we've introduced another category.

And the philosophical danger is that we start treating consciousness as one property belonging to one identifiable object among other objects.

Abhinavagupta recognizes the predicament.

### The Sanskrit: इदृश and तादृश

**ईदृश - īdṛśa**

Of this kind, such as this.

**तादृश - tādṛśa**

Of that kind, such as that.

Notice the common second element, *-dṛśa*, related to √*dṛś*, to see.

These words are demonstrative descriptions of a particular kind.

They allow us to distinguish one thing from another.

And ordinarily that's useful.

"This mango is ripe."

"That mango is rotten."

Excellent. Language has saved your lunch.

But what happens when you attempt to describe the reality that is the condition of every description and every distinction?

There is no external class of realities against which the Absolute can be exhaustively defined.

You can use concepts to point towards it.

You cannot turn the universal ground of conceptualization into a fully bounded conceptual object.

**Sixth meaning: Anuttara is not subject to the delimitation imposed by ordinary descriptive statements.**


## Interpretation 7 - Even calling it “this” is too much

इदम् AND THE STRUCTURE OF OBJECTIFICATION

Now he becomes even more ruthless.

It's not just elaborate descriptions that create the problem.

Even the word:

**इदम् - idam**

"This."

You point at something and say:

"This is the reality."

And Abhinavagupta says:

Well, what exactly did you just point at?

Because the structure of *idam* already presupposes something presented as an object.

A *this* is distinguished from some other possible *that*.

And once you're operating through this distinction, you're already within the realm of determined cognition.

### The Sanskrit: विकल्प

**विकल्प - vikalpa**

Break it down:

- **vi-** - differentiation, separation, particularization.
- √**kḷp** - to arrange, form, be suitable, devise.
- **vikalpa** - conceptual construction, determination, alternative or distinction.

The word has multiple usages in Sanskrit. In Trika epistemology, it frequently concerns determinate cognition structured through conceptual distinctions.

Let's use a practical example.

You're sitting quietly.

A sensation appears.

Before you formulate a detailed judgement, there is already awareness of the sensation.

Then the mind declares:

"This is discomfort."

Then:

"This discomfort is preventing my meditation."

Then:

"I am the sort of person whose meditation is constantly interrupted."

Well, congratulations.

We've constructed an entire autobiography out of a sore hip.

The movement from immediate manifestation into conceptual identification is important.

But Abhinavagupta is not saying that all conceptual activity is inherently evil.

He's saying that **Anuttara cannot be restricted to the conceptual object produced by such activity**.

The undetermined basis of manifestation is not absent when conceptual thought occurs.

It is that through which even conceptual determinations can appear.

### Sanskrit laboratory: अविकल्पित

**अविकल्पित - avikalpita**

- *a-* - not.
- *vikalpita* - constructed, determined, conceived.

Thus, not conceptually determined.

Here the negative prefix is *a-* because *vikalpita* begins with a consonant.

Compare:

*an + uttara → anuttara*

*a + vikalpita → avikalpita*

The choice between **a-** and **an-** is not random.

It reflects the phonological form of the following element.

And notice *-ita*, a common participial ending.

It can indicate something that has undergone or possesses the state described by the verbal formation.

Now, here's the deeper philosophical move.

Anuttara is not just a mysterious object beyond thought.

It is what enables both conceptual and nonconceptual experiences to manifest.

That is why merely replacing ordinary thoughts with an imagined blank state doesn't automatically solve the problem.

You might have eliminated the descriptions.

But have you recognized what makes their presence or absence possible?

Seventh meaning: Anuttara is not an object delimited even by the elementary conceptual designation *idam*, "this."




## Interpretation 8 - The spiritual hierarchy is not the Absolute

आणव, शाक्त, शांभव AND THE HIERARCHY OF STATES

Now Abhinavagupta goes after something rather close to home.

The hierarchy of spiritual means and levels of consciousness.

You've studied the three major *upāyas* in the Tantrasāra.

**आणव**

āṇava

**Āṇavopāya** - employs supports involving body, breath, mental representations and other limited instruments.

**शाक्त**

śākta

**Śāktopāya** - works principally through the power of awareness, thought and insight.

**शांभव**

śāmbhava

**Śāmbhavopāya** - involves immediate awareness or will, without dependence on elaborate conceptual procedures.

These are genuinely differentiated means in the tradition.

But if Anuttara were simply the highest member of this series, it would still be defined through comparison.

Abhinavagupta also lists gradations of the states of consciousness.

**जाग्रत् - jāgrat**: waking.

**स्वप्न - svapna**: dreaming.

**सुप्त - supta**: sleeping.

**तुर्य - turya**: the fourth.

**तुर्यातीत - turyātīta**: beyond the fourth.

And here we need to be especially careful.

He does not simply deny that the tradition distinguishes these states.

He points out that their structure as higher and lower belongs to an articulated system of relations.

Anuttara cannot be exhaustively identified with one rung within that system.

Imagine someone saying:

"I have transcended waking consciousness and entered the fourth state."

Very good.

"And after that I attained the state beyond the fourth."

Excellent.

"And now I'm trying to attain the one beyond the state beyond the fourth."

My dear yogic accountant.

At some point we're going to need to stop adding floors to the building.

The Absolute is not merely the last available floor.

### Sanskrit laboratory: उत्तरॊत्तर

**उत्तरोत्तर - uttarottara**

Higher and higher, successive, progressively superior.

From:

*uttara + uttara → uttarottara*

Why the *o*?

Because Sanskrit vowel sandhi gives:

**a + u → o**

The final *a* of the first *uttara* combines with the initial *u* of the second.

And notice:

**उत्तर - uttara**

**उत्तरोत्तर - uttarottara**

Now you can see the sandhi rather than memorizing an inexplicable spelling.

Philosophically, the doubling captures the very process under examination.

Higher.

Then higher again.

Then higher again.

Anuttara is not the endless continuation of *uttarottara*.

It is the reality in which the whole comparative ordering is possible.

Eighth meaning: Anuttara transcends hierarchies of spiritual means, ontological levels and states of consciousness, without rendering their practical distinctions meaningless.


## Interpretation 9 - No person is metaphysically higher or lower

THE TRANSCENDENCE OF SOCIAL RANK

This one is striking.

Abhinavagupta turns from spiritual gradation to social distinctions.

He includes the divisions of caste or social rank among the hierarchies that do not characterize Anuttara.

The Sanskrit expression involves:

**ऊनाधिकत्व - ūnādhikatva**

A state of inferiority and superiority, of less and more.

Let's dissect it.

- **ūna** - deficient, less, incomplete.
- **adhika** - greater, additional, superior.
- **-tva** - the abstract state or quality of being something.

That suffix **-tva** is everywhere in Sanskrit philosophy.

You've already seen it in:

**शिवत्व - śivatva**

The state or condition of being Śiva.

And:

**कर्तृत्व - kartṛtva**

Agency, the state of being a doer.

So *ūnādhikatva* means a condition involving lesser and greater status.

And Abhinavagupta argues that Anuttara cannot be delimited by these distinctions.

The commentary specifically invokes traditional social divisions, including Brahmin, Kṣatriya, Vaiśya, Śūdra and those considered outside or below the major ranked categories.

This is a metaphysical claim concerning the supreme reality, not proof that the historical Trika community abolished caste or lived without social hierarchy.

Those are different questions.

But the philosophical implication is significant.

If the reality of all individuals is universal consciousness, an individual's ultimate nature cannot be superior or inferior because of birth.

The differences belong to manifested social and conventional classifications.

Not to Anuttara as such.

Imagine declaring:

"My consciousness is spiritually superior because my ancestors had a better ceremonial job description."

Abhinavagupta's metaphysics gives that claim no ultimate footing.

**Ninth meaning: Anuttara is beyond the metaphysical ranking of persons as higher or lower.**

; \]


## Interpretation 10 - Even the hierarchy of speech is transcended

पश्यन्ती AND THE POWERS OF LANGUAGE

Remember Part I?

We introduced four levels of speech.

Now the commentary returns to the differentiated powers of language.

But this time Abhinavagupta is looking at them through the meaning of *uttara* as a particular level or power.

The Sanskrit mentions:

**पश्यन्ती - paśyantī**

This is a beautiful word to learn.

Its root is √**paś**, to see.

*Paśyantī* is a feminine present participial form.

Literally, **she who sees** or **the seeing one**.

In the philosophy of speech, Paśyantī is a subtle level in which the separation between signifier and signified has not yet become fully articulated.

Then comes:

**मध्यमा - madhyamā**

Middle, intermediate.

From *madhya*, middle, with a feminine form.

And:

**वैखरी - vaikharī**

Articulated speech.

In the traditional fourfold account, these differentiated powers are grounded in **Parā Vāk**, supreme speech.

Now here's the problem.

Once we've divided speech into four levels, it's tempting to say:

Parā is higher.

Paśyantī is lower.

Madhyamā is lower still.

Vaikharī is the bottom.

And we've successfully invented a metaphysical elevator.

But the very distinction between these powers depends on the manifestation of consciousness.

At the ultimate level, they are not independent substances competing for spiritual seniority.

Anuttara is not simply one more segmented speech-level set over against the others.

The supreme freedom of speech manifests all of them.

Think about what happens when you say:

**अहम् - aham**

"I."

The sound emerges as articulated speech.

You hear it.

You identify the word.

You recognize its meaning.

But its ultimate significance in Trika isn't merely that your mouth successfully produced two syllables.

The tradition asks what self-awareness the utterance expresses, and how the entire differentiated act of speaking arises from consciousness.

The articulated word and the supreme awareness it may disclose are not metaphysically alien to each other.

**Tenth meaning: Anuttara transcends the differentiated ordering of the powers of speech.**


## Interpretation 11 - Beyond the Aghorā powers

अघोरा AND DIVINE MODALITIES

Now the text introduces the divine powers known through the *Aghorā* group.

First, the Sanskrit.

**घोर - ghora**

Terrible, frightening, formidable.

**अघोर - aghora**

Not terrible, non-frightening.

Add the feminine ending and we have:

**अघोरा - aghorā**

The feminine form.

Notice how the little prefix changes the meaning.

*Ghora.*

Frightening.

*Aghora.*

Not frightening.

Of course, Tantric theological symbolism is more complicated than this basic dictionary contrast.

The powers associated with these categories participate in the different ways consciousness reveals, conceals and engages with manifestation.

A power may be experienced as liberating or binding according to its operation in relation to the individual.

Abhinavagupta is not here providing a new catalogue of invented goddesses.

He's examining the very idea of differentiated powers as separately classified modes.

At the level of Anuttara, such distinctions cannot establish independent realities outside universal consciousness.

That does not mean the powers are unreal.

It means their differentiation does not compromise their underlying nonduality.

A helpful analogy:

White light can be analysed through different colours.

The colours are genuinely distinguishable in the appropriate conditions.

But if you imagine each colour as a fundamentally independent source of light, you've misunderstood their relationship to the spectrum.

Similarly, the divine powers can be differentiated without becoming ultimately separate from their luminous ground.

**Eleventh meaning: Anuttara is not circumscribed by the differentiated categories of powers such as Aghorā.**


## Interpretation 12 - Beyond Parā, Parāparā and Aparā as separate categories

THE THREE GREAT GODDESS POWERS

And now we arrive at something unmistakably Trika.

The three divine powers:

**परा**

Parā

Supreme power, associated with undivided awareness.

**परापरा**

Parāparā

The power integrating unity and differentiation.

**अपरा**

Aparā

The power manifesting differentiation.

### The word Parāparā

**परापरा - parāparā**

This is especially interesting because the name joins contrasting terms:

*parā + aparā → parāparā*

The first is supreme or transcendent.

The second is lower or non-supreme in a relative classification.

Their combination signifies a mode involving both unity and difference.

This is one of the great beauties of Trika symbolism.

The triad isn't simply a list of unrelated goddesses.

It articulates distinct ways universal consciousness manifests and apprehends itself.

And Abhinavagupta's interpretation insists that Anuttara is not exhausted by these differentiated expressions.

Here's the essential distinction:

**Parā, as a specific member of the triad, can be distinguished from Parāparā and Aparā. Anuttara, as the ultimate reality, cannot be reduced to a competing member of that triad.**

Otherwise we'd end up with the absurd picture of supreme consciousness competing with its own manifestations.

Imagine a theatre director producing three characters and then announcing:

"The first character is more fundamentally real than the entire theatre, the script, the performers, and the production itself."

No, mate.

The first character may represent the highest level within the drama, but its dramatic distinction doesn't stand outside the reality that enables the whole performance.

Twelfth meaning: Anuttara transcends the differentiated classification of the three Goddess powers, while being their inseparable reality.




## Interpretation 13 - You cannot inject consciousness into consciousness

THE GREAT INITIATION PROBLEM

Now Abhinavagupta becomes properly dangerous.

Because we're entering the matter of **dīkṣā**, initiation.

And initiation was not some casual optional flourish within the Tantric traditions.

It was extraordinarily important.

Teachers transmitted mantras, ritual qualifications, teachings, and forms of spiritual authorization.

So here's the question.

If the guru initiates a disciple, what is actually transmitted?

And can that transmission literally manufacture a consciousness that was previously absent?

Abhinavagupta offers another interpretive division of the word *anuttara*.

This time through:

**नुद् - nud**

A verbal root meaning to push, impel, or drive.

And:

**तरण - taraṇa**

Crossing, passage.

He examines the interpretation of spiritual crossing as something brought about by an impulse or transmission from the teacher to the student.

### Sanskrit laboratory: नुद् and प्रेरण

**नुद् - nud**

Look at the final consonant.

द्

This is द without its inherent *a*.

The virāma matters: the verbal root ends in *d*, not *da*.

**प्रेरण - preraṇa**

Impelling, sending forth, stimulation.

Related to √*īr* and the formation *prer*, to impel.

Now the theological problem.

In initiation, the guru's consciousness is described as stimulating or awakening the disciple's consciousness.

But is consciousness really a physical object?

Can you scoop out some enlightened awareness with a ceremonial ladle and pour it into another person's head?

"Hold still, mate. I'm transferring approximately 250 millilitres of Bhairava."

Obviously not.

Consciousness, in Abhinavagupta's metaphysics, is self-luminous and all-pervasive.

It is not situated in one container and absent from another.

So when a ritual describes the transmission or joining of consciousness, we need to interpret it appropriately.

The guru can be instrumental in bringing about recognition, dispelling obscurations and establishing the disciple in the teaching.

But the guru cannot create the supreme consciousness itself.

This is a crucial distinction.

### Why ritual still matters

Abhinavagupta is not simply rejecting initiation.

Indeed, his other writings devote considerable attention to ritual, initiation, mantra and the role of the guru.

The problem is taking a ritual sequence to be a literal spatial transport of all-pervasive awareness.

If an initiation succeeds, it is not because Śiva was formerly absent from the disciple and has now been imported.

It is because the conditions of recognition have changed.

**Thirteenth meaning: Anuttara does not depend on an externally imposed act of initiation that literally transports consciousness from one place to another.**

And yes, this is an extraordinary claim from within a tradition that also has highly developed initiation rituals.

Abhinavagupta is not unaware of the tension.

He is philosophically interpreting what those rites accomplish.

; \]


## Interpretation 14 - Beyond the little breathing individual

अन्, अणु AND THE LIFE OF CONSCIOUSNESS

Now the word gets even more interesting.

Because Abhinavagupta examines **an** in connection with breathing and life.

The Sanskrit root:

**अन् - √an**

To breathe, live.

And through an interpretive derivation involving the zero-form suffix *kvip*, he associates *an* with the breathing individual.

This is Tantric explanatory etymology, not permission to assume every word containing *an* historically derives from breathing.

He then brings in:

**अणु - aṇu**

The minute or limited individual.

Now notice something critical in the writing.

# न

*na*

Dental n

# ण

*ṇa*

Retroflex ṇ

These are different consonants.

In **अणु - aṇu**, the consonant is retroflex ṇ, produced with the tongue curled back.

In **अनु - anu**, it's dental n.

The words are not interchangeable merely because their Romanized spellings look similar without diacritics.

So please keep the little dot under **ṇ** when writing IAST.

The dot is doing a real job.

### What is the limited individual?

In Trika, *aṇu* is not simply a tiny physical object.

It is consciousness appearing under the condition of limited subjectivity.

The universal "I" is contracted into the apparent identity of a finite experiencer.

This gives us one of the central problems of the tradition.

How does the unlimited manifest itself as the limited?

How does the universal subject appear as a being whose knowledge and agency are constrained?

Now Abhinavagupta connects this with breathing and life.

He invokes the difference between merely being identified with bodily or vital processes and the deeper reality of knowing and acting.

And the commentary brings in the profound idea that life, in its fundamental sense, is connected with **jñāna and kriyā**.

Knowledge and action.

Let's break those down.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowing, knowledge.

From √*jñā*, to know.

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action, activity.

From √*kṛ*, to do or make.

Notice the initial conjunct in ज्ञान:

**ज् + ञ = ज्ञ**

This is not a single simple consonant letter.

It is a conjunct of *j* and *ñ*.

In Sanskrit recitation, its traditional pronunciation differs from many modern Hindi pronunciations.

And **क्रि** in *kriyā* combines:

क् + र + ि

The vowel belongs to the consonant cluster as a syllable, even though the written short-*i* sign appears visually to the left.

Now the philosophical brilliance.

A finite living being knows.

It acts.

Its knowledge and action are restricted.

But the very possibility of knowing and acting is grounded, according to Trika, in the universal divine powers of knowledge and action.

So when we ask what fundamentally makes a living experiencer possible, we eventually reach consciousness itself.

Not the body as an independently self-luminous substance.

Not breath as an entirely self-sufficient ultimate principle.

Not the psychological ego as the original source of awareness.

And even a subtle condition of emptiness, the *śūnya-pramātṛ* or void-experiencer, does not establish an independent reality beyond Bhairava.

### What is śūnya-pramātṛ?

**शून्यप्रमातृ - śūnyapramātṛ**

- **śūnya** - empty, void.
- **pramātṛ** - knower, cognizing subject.

In the technical Trika account, this refers to a mode of subjectivity in which differentiated contents may be absent, while a subtle limitation remains.

This is why Abhinavagupta refuses to equate blankness with absolute liberation.

A state of mental emptiness may eliminate recognizable objects without fully revealing universal self-awareness.

A practitioner can experience a great silence and conclude that the absence of contents is the final truth.

But Trika asks what makes even that silence manifest.

**Fourteenth meaning: Anuttara is not limited to the finite breathing subject or the void-experiencer; their reality depends on the universal power of consciousness.**




## Interpretation 15 - The letter अ is the beginning of the whole cosmic business

THE PHONEMIC INTERPRETATION OF ANUTTARA

Right.

This is where Abhinavagupta stops being merely a philosopher and becomes an absolutely terrifying Sanskrit magician.

Because now he considers the opening:

**अ - a**

Not merely as part of a negative prefix.

But as the first vowel, a symbolic expression of supreme consciousness.

And this is particularly relevant to what you've been learning about Devanāgarī.

You recently noticed that Sanskrit consonant letters already contain the inherent vowel *a*.

That observation is exactly the kind of thing that makes the phonemic metaphysics of Trika so fascinating.

But there are two separate levels here.

**Grammatically**, the inherent vowel is a feature of the abugida writing system and its representation of Sanskrit syllables.

**Metaphysically**, Trika interprets *a* as the primordial manifestation of the supreme reality in the system of sacred phonemes.

The philosophical interpretation doesn't cause the orthographic rule.

But the rule provides an especially evocative way to contemplate the symbolism.

### Why अ?

In the Tantric phonemic scheme, *a* can represent Anuttara.

It is the first vowel.

The primary opening.

The sound from which the later phonemic differentiations are symbolically unfolded.

And Abhinavagupta develops a relationship between this first opening, the powers of manifestation, and the final completeness expressed as universal *aham*.

Remember:

**अहम् - aham**

"I."

Now, in the traditional alphabetic symbolism, this word has enormous significance.

The first phoneme:

**अ - a**

And the final consonantal element:

**ह - ha**

followed by the nasal completion represented in *aham*.

In Trika, the word *aham* can symbolize the inclusion of the entire expressive universe within universal I-consciousness.

Not because the English concept of "I" is extraordinarily important.

But because the Sanskrit phonemic structure is being used to express the identity of universal awareness and its manifestations.

The supreme "I" is not the ego's private self-description.

It's the universal subjectivity within which all objects appear.

### The Sanskrit: अहंभाव

**अहंभाव - ahaṃbhāva**

- **aham / ahaṃ** - I.
- **bhāva** - being, state, condition, manifestation.

Thus I-consciousness or the state of being "I."

And the commentary describes a movement involving the first and final stages of the complete I-consciousness, universal manifestation, and the power of emission.

The term **kalā** appears here.

**कला - kalā**

A portion, digit, aspect, or operative power.

In these traditions, the word often has a highly technical significance referring to an aspect or potency of divine manifestation.

Here it concerns the power of consciousness in its relationship to complete I-awareness.

### Now the strange phonemic redivision

Abhinavagupta explores the sounds of *anuttara* in a new way.

The opening **a** is associated with the primordial consciousness.

The element **nut** is interpreted in relation to the power of emission, *visarga*.

And **tara** is associated with crossing, floating or surpassing.

This is not ordinary dictionary analysis of *anuttara*.

It is an esoteric interpretation of the phonemic sequence.

The term *visarga* is especially important.

**विसर्ग - visarga**

- *vi-* - outward, apart, distinctly, depending on context.
- √*sṛj* - to send forth, release, emit.

The noun refers to emission or release.

In the writing system, the sign **ः** is also called visarga.

And you already know its visual appearance.

Two dots.

One above the other.

Now the Tantric significance.

Visarga becomes a symbol for consciousness's power to emit manifestations without ceasing to be itself.

The universe is not something the Absolute produces out of a foreign material outside its own nature.

It is consciousness manifesting its own powers.

**The emitted reality never becomes metaphysically independent of its source.**

Think of an artist imagining an entire fictional world.

The characters have relationships.

There are conflicts and locations.

Events unfold.

Yet the whole imagined world depends on the creative consciousness through which it is present.

That analogy is limited, because Trika treats the world as real divine manifestation rather than merely a human fantasy.

But it conveys something essential.

Manifestation does not require an entirely separate ultimate substance.

Now here's the really beautiful part.

The sound **अ** is not just being treated as the beginning of a spoken sequence.

It is taken as an indication of the original fullness of conscious manifestation.

The divine freedom of Śakti allows that fullness to appear as the entire differentiated cosmos.

But the original awareness does not get chopped into pieces as though somebody had attacked the Absolute with a cosmic breadknife.

**Fifteenth meaning: Anuttara is interpreted through its phonemes as the primacy of consciousness and its unrestricted power of emission.**

The metaphysics is no longer merely attached to the meaning of the word.

It is being read through the very structure of sound.




## Interpretation 16 - Even space and time cannot contain its action

अक्रमक्रिया - ACTION WITHOUT SUCCESSION

And finally.

The sixteenth.

The culmination.

Abhinavagupta takes us beyond even the assumption that activity must proceed through a sequence of movements.

He examines another possibility involving *anut*, the absence of impelling or movement, and compares it with space, before arguing for the incomparable freedom of consciousness.

And he brings us to one of the most profound ideas in his entire philosophy:

**अक्रमक्रिया - akramakriyā**

Nonsequential activity.

Let's actually understand that, because it's an absolutely magnificent philosophical problem.

### Step one: What is krama?

**क्रम - krama**

Sequence, succession, order.

Related to √*kram*, to step or proceed.

Think about walking.

You take one step.

Then another.

Then another.

A sequence.

Or think about making tea.

You boil the water.

Pour it over the leaves.

Wait.

Drink.

The acts occur in a temporal order.

Ordinary action seems to depend on *krama*.

### Step two: What is akrama?

**अक्रम - akrama**

Without succession, nonsequential.

The negative prefix **a-** modifies *krama*.

Then:

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action.

So:

**अक्रमक्रिया - akramakriyā**

Nonsequential action.

Now that sounds like a contradiction.

How can there be action without succession?

Surely action requires something to happen.

And happening requires time.

And time requires before and after.

But Abhinavagupta is distinguishing two levels of activity.

The activity of differentiated manifestation, which appears temporally ordered.

And the fundamental divine activity of consciousness, which does not depend on an externally imposed temporal framework.

### The Sanskrit: विमर्श

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness, self-apprehension, the active self-recognition of consciousness.

The word is associated with *vi-* and √*mṛś*, to touch, consider, examine or apprehend.

In Trika, *vimarśa* is one of the absolutely central concepts.

And it's important not to reduce it to ordinary reflective thinking.

It isn't simply the little mental voice saying:

"Ah yes, I seem to be conscious."

That would already be a differentiated mental event.

*Vimarśa* is the intrinsic self-revelatory power of consciousness.

Consciousness does not need another independent observer to make it aware of itself.

It is self-manifesting.

And its freedom to disclose, differentiate and reintegrate its own manifestations is inseparable from that awareness.

### The philosophical problem

Suppose a thought appears.

Then another thought appears.

Then you remember the first thought.

Each event is temporally distinct.

But what establishes that there has been a succession?

The relation between the events must itself be apprehended.

The sequence appears *to* consciousness.

And in Trika, the power of temporal differentiation is itself a manifestation of supreme freedom.

Abhinavagupta is not asserting the empirical claim that ordinary neural activity takes place without time.

He is making a metaphysical claim about the relation between universal consciousness and the temporal succession of its appearances.

The ultimate reality is not one more event waiting behind the earlier events.

It is the ground in which their manifestation and ordering are possible.

Think of a film.

A character on screen experiences a sequence of events.

Yesterday.

Today.

Tomorrow.

But the entire structure of the film is present as a completed work.

Now extend that analogy cautiously.

Trika isn't claiming the universe is literally a prerecorded movie.

It is using the distinction between succession within manifestation and the freedom of the manifesting consciousness to challenge the assumption that all activity must be subordinate to time.

And this is where **svātantrya** returns.

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Autonomy, independence, sovereign freedom.

The power we've already spent considerable time learning in Trika.

Consciousness does not depend upon a temporal mechanism external to itself in order to manifest.

Rather, temporality is one of the ways it manifests.

So the ultimate activity of Śiva is not merely an enormous causal process taking place inside a bigger universe.

It is the very freedom through which causal processes, temporal sequences and apparent spatial distinctions arise.

And here's the distinction that will save you from misunderstanding the entire doctrine.

**Nonsequential does not mean inactive.**

Nor does it mean every ordinary event occurs simultaneously in some straightforward physical sense.

It means that the supreme freedom of consciousness cannot be reduced to the temporally differentiated actions that it manifests.

Now recall the Goddess's first question.

How does Anuttara immediately bestow perfection?

**सद्यः - sadyaḥ**

Immediately.

We are finally beginning to understand why that word matters.

If realization concerned manufacturing a completely new divine consciousness, it would need a productive process.

If realization concerns recognition of the consciousness that grounds the very possibility of such processes, the problem is profoundly different.

The recognition can be immediate because its object is not a future entity waiting to be constructed.

And with that, Abhinavagupta concludes his sixteenfold unfolding.

The ordinary meaning of *anuttara* has been taken apart through hierarchies, questions, liberation, ritual, breath, speech, phonemes and action.

Not because the word has sixteen unrelated dictionary definitions.

But because each interpretation removes another way of treating the universal reality as something limited by its own manifestation.

**Sixteenth meaning: Anuttara is the supreme consciousness whose intrinsic, reflexive freedom is not subject to the sequence, space and time governing ordinary differentiated action.**

; \]


## Abhinavagupta's final grammatical argument: Why anuttara and not anuttama?

And just when you thought he'd finally finished abusing this poor word, he turns to Sanskrit comparative grammar.

Because why does the Goddess say:

**अनुत्तर - anuttara**

Rather than:

**अनुत्तम - anuttama**

A brilliant question.

Let's break down the endings.

| Sanskrit | Meaning | Grammatical role |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **उत्तर - uttara** | Higher, further | Comparative-type formation |
| **उत्तम - uttama** | Highest, best | Superlative-type formation |
| **अनुत्तर - anuttara** | Unsurpassable | Negation of *uttara* |
| **अनुत्तम - anuttama** | Unsurpassed or incomparable | Context-dependent negated form |

Abhinavagupta discusses the grammatical suffixes traditionally designated:

**तरप् - tarap**

And:

**तमप् - tamap**

These are Pāṇinian grammatical designations for comparative and superlative formations.

You see the familiar surface elements in:

**शुक्लतर - śuklatara**

Whiter.

And:

**शुक्लतम - śuklatama**

Whitest.

Now imagine three objects.

A white palace.

A white cloth.

A white swan.

One can compare degrees of whiteness.

One may describe one object as whiter than another, or the whitest among a collection.

But the comparison always presupposes an ordering of entities.

Abhinavagupta's point is that neither a comparative nor a superlative expression should tempt us into imagining a transcendent consciousness whose nature depends upon being ranked against other ultimate substances.

Anuttara is not merely a very impressive contender in the cosmic competition.

**It is the reality to which no independent competitor can be ultimately opposed.**

The grammatical discussion is technical, and he disputes simplistic assumptions about how comparative and superlative suffixes express relations among comparison sets.

But the philosophical aim is clear.

There is no higher reality that Anuttara fails to attain.

No superior ontological category from which it has been excluded.

No external measure by which its perfection can be increased.

Abhinavagupta even draws on a scriptural paradox: the supreme is not known as an object even by accomplished knowers, yet neither is it wholly unknown to ordinary beings.

Why?

Because every act of knowing already depends upon consciousness.

You cannot know it as an independently existing object.

But you cannot have any experience whatsoever in its complete absence.

That's an extraordinary epistemological claim.

The most fundamental reality is not concealed behind an object.

It is presupposed by the very manifestation of anything as an object.


## The Heart and its sixteen knots

And then comes a marvellous transition.

Abhinavagupta announces that Anuttara has been explained sixteenfold and invokes a verse from a *Sāraśāstra* connecting Anuttara, the Heart, and sixteen ways of understanding its knot.

Let's learn the important words.

**हृदय - hṛdaya**

Heart.

**ग्रन्थि - granthi**

Knot.

**षोडशधा - ṣoḍaśadhā**

In sixteen ways.

**कर्म - karma**

Action.

**यथासुखम् - yathāsukham**

As is agreeable, according to one's ease or pleasure.

Now this is beautiful.

The highest reality is called the Heart.

But the Heart is associated with a knot.

And knowledge of the knot in sixteen ways is connected with freedom of action.

Don't misunderstand the image.

The knot is not an independently existing metaphysical obstruction somehow stronger than Śiva.

It concerns the complex ways in which consciousness appears contracted and divided through structures of experience.

The sixteen interpretations have shown us how we repeatedly imagine the Absolute to be located somewhere other than where it is.

Above the world.

After liberation.

Beyond a future meditation.

Inside a subtle cakra.

Accessible only through an external transfer.

Higher than other realities.

Separated from the sequence of ordinary experience.

Each of these can become another conceptual knot.

And the commentary has been loosening them.

### Sanskrit laboratory: ṣoḍaśadhā

**षोडशधा - ṣoḍaśadhā**

This word contains:

**ṣoḍaśa** - sixteen.

**-dhā** - in a certain number of ways or divisions.

So *ṣoḍaśadhā* means sixteenfold, in sixteen ways.

Learn the numerical family:

| Number | Sanskrit |
|--------|--------|
| 3 | **त्रि - tri** |
| 4 | **चतुर् - catur** |
| 5 | **पञ्च - pañca** |
| 10 | **दश - daśa** |
| 16 | **षोडश - ṣoḍaśa** |

And now look at the extraordinary relationship between the Heart and the structure of Sanskrit in this tradition.

The alphabet can be interpreted as the unfolding of consciousness into differentiated powers.

The sixteen vowels possess symbolic associations with the fullness of Śakti.

The philosophical interpretations of *anuttara* disclose the same supreme reality from distinct perspectives.

The number sixteen is therefore not merely a random rhetorical flourish.

It participates in the larger Tantric language of fullness and completeness.

But again, don't turn an evocative structural parallel into a mathematical theorem.

Abhinavagupta is practicing sacred exegesis, not demonstrating that the universe can be deduced from the conventional number of Sanskrit vowels.


## Now put all sixteen together

We've covered them in the order of the commentary's unfolding. Here is the whole architecture in one place.

| No. | Interpretation of Anuttara |
|--------|--------|
| 1 | Nothing is higher or additional to it |
| 2 | No separate answer exists beyond it |
| 3 | It requires no liberation conceived as crossing over |
| 4 | It is not attained by ascending subtle bodily centres |
| 5 | It transcends the ultimate dualism of bondage and liberation |
| 6 | It cannot be delimited through descriptive utterances |
| 7 | It cannot be objectified even as "this" |
| 8 | It exceeds gradations of spiritual means and states |
| 9 | It is beyond social and metaphysical rankings |
| 10 | It transcends differentiated levels of speech |
| 11 | It is beyond the differentiated Aghorā powers |
| 12 | It transcends the triadic classification of Goddess powers |
| 13 | It does not depend on an externally imposed transmission of consciousness |
| 14 | It is not confined to the breathing individual or void-experiencer |
| 15 | Its phonemes disclose the primacy and emission-power of consciousness |
| 16 | Its intrinsic activity transcends temporal succession |

These are explanatory labels for the sixteen interpretive divisions, not sixteen separately numbered verses in Abhinavagupta's original Sanskrit.

## The final philosophical synthesis

Now.

Forget the table for a moment.

We've spent this entire installment analysing the Sanskrit of one word.

But what is Abhinavagupta actually trying to accomplish?

He's removing sixteen different assumptions about the nature of ultimate reality.

And look at the pattern.

You assume the Absolute is something higher.

He denies that it depends upon a hierarchy.

You assume liberation is something that happens after a journey.

He distinguishes transformative recognition from the production of a new ultimate substance.

You assume supreme consciousness must be encountered as a particular object.

He shows that the very possibility of objectification presupposes awareness.

You assume divine power has to be transferred through a sequence of causal operations.

He asks how all-pervasive consciousness could literally be transported from one location to another.

You assume manifestation is separate from its ground.

He interprets the phonemes themselves as the unfolding of the expressive freedom of that ground.

And finally, you assume every action must be limited by succession.

He distinguishes the temporal structure of manifestation from the intrinsic, nonsequential freedom of universal consciousness.

This is an astonishingly coherent progression.

The point is not that the word *anuttara* is a magic sound with sixteen arbitrary meanings.

**The point is that every mode of limitation through which we ordinarily understand reality must ultimately be reconsidered in the light of the consciousness that makes it manifest.**

And now think of the relationship to the Pratyabhijñā system.

The individual takes itself to be a bounded subject.

"I am here."

"The world is there."

"I was something yesterday."

"I must become something tomorrow."

"I need to get closer to Śiva."

But recognition challenges the fundamental identification underlying those distinctions.

The universal consciousness is not the bounded individual considered merely as an individual.

Yet the reality of that individual is not other than the universal consciousness.

This is why the language of recognition is so important.

It is not simply the claim that the ego is omnipotent.

Nor that every ordinary thought is infallible.

Nor that limitation and suffering have become practically irrelevant.

It is the recognition that **the contracted experience of individuality does not exhaust the true nature of the experiencer**.

And this is exactly why the Goddess asked how Anuttara grants perfection *immediately*.

The whole question of immediacy is now waiting for us.

Because if consciousness is always itself, what exactly takes place when recognition dawns?

Why is there a guru?

Why is there a teaching?

Why is there practice?

And what, precisely, is **Kaulika Siddhi**?

This is the next development in Abhinavagupta's argument.

We aren't finished with verse one.

Not even remotely.

The man is still warming up.

| **षोडशधा** | *ṣoḍaśadhā* | In sixteen ways |

An especially valuable exercise is writing:

**अन् + उत्तर = अनुत्तर**

Then:

**अ + विकल्पित = अविकल्पित**

Then:

**उत्तर + उत्तर = उत्तरोत्तर**

Three formations, three different things happening.

Learn why the letters change, and Sanskrit begins to become much more transparent.
