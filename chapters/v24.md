# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XXIV - Bhairavī, the Self-Revealing Goddess

*Abhinavagupta has finally finished interrogating the universe's vowels, consonants, pots, cows, lunar calendars, cosmic bodily functions and philosophical identity crises. Now he does something magnificent: he takes the entire argument and recites it back as poetry.*

*Thirty numbered Sanskrit units carry us from the self-shining Goddess through the nature of knowledge, animals and children, Parā Vāk, the divine Aham, Śiva-Śakti union, Visarga, the alphabet's transformation into bondage, liberation while living, and an unexpectedly personal closing reflection on the limitations of his own exposition.*

Kashmir Series pp. 212-216 · Somadeva Vasudeva's GRETIL transcription, corresponding Gnoli pp. 257-259 · Original metrical recapitulation


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

Twenty-three installments.

We began with **अनुत्तर - Anuttara**, the unsurpassable reality.

Then came the letters.

The vowels emerged as powers of bliss, will, knowledge and action. Consonants became matrices of differentiation. The thirty-six *tattvas* appeared. Śiva and Śakti generated the universe through their indivisible freedom.

Then we examined language itself.

Why does **गो - go** mean cow?

Why is recognition possible?

What makes a word meaningful?

And why can the exact same phonemic power participate in spiritual recognition or finite bondage?

Part XXIII supplied the rather uncomfortable answer.

The pure divine phonemes enter **भावसृष्टि - bhāvasṛṣṭi**, the manifestation of cognitive dispositions.

The Sāṃkhya philosophers arrived with fifty classifications.

Five categories of error, twenty-eight incapacities, nine satisfactions and eight attainments.

And the *Spandakārikā* explained that determinate cognition is bound up with the power of speech.

The alphabet wasn't intrinsically evil.

It had never become an independently existing substance outside consciousness.

But its differentiated operation could conceal the very conscious ground through which it appeared.

And then the Pūrvaśāstra described the outward manifestation of Māyic phonemes corresponding to their pure prototypes.

The letters become audible.

Sequential.

Conventional.

Bound up with identification.

And yet **embraced by the divine powers that produced them**.

Now Abhinavagupta says:

**तदयमत्र सङ्क्षेपार्थः।**

*Tad ayam atra saṃkṣepārthaḥ.*

"Now, here is the meaning in summary."

And instead of giving us a tidy little paragraph, he launches into a sequence of original Sanskrit verses.

A complete compressed account of the philosophy we've just studied.

The two primary transcriptions show **30 numbered units** in the metrical summary, although the last unit extends beyond an ordinary four-pāda stanza. The Kashmir Series then prints an Apabhraṃśa passage on pp. 216-217 that GRETIL explicitly omits, before the prose turns to the next root-Tantra verses. We must keep that additional material in view rather than pretend the Sanskrit summary simply ends with no textual complication. ; \]

And there are real variants.

The second verse reads **स्वभावयोग - svabhāvayoga** in the Kashmir Series but **स्वभासायोग - svabhāsāyoga** in GRETIL.

Later, the description of Mātṛkā's liquidity and condensation contains a difficult, apparently corrupt Devanāgarī reading in the Kashmir Series, while GRETIL preserves a more intelligible compound.

And the description of pure Bhairava-consciousness on p. 215 also differs considerably between the witnesses.

We're keeping those differences.

No pretending the manuscript tradition is perfectly clean.

No inventing a missing vowel to rescue a poetic interpretation.

Right.

The Goddess is about to speak through Abhinavagupta's summary.

And the first verse is extraordinarily beautiful.


## 1. The Goddess shines of herself

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S ORIGINAL METRICAL SUMMARY · VERSE 1 · P. 212

**स्वातन्त्र्यैकरसावेशचमत्कारैकलक्षणा।**

*Svātantryaikarasāveśacamatkāraikalakṣaṇā.*

"Whose defining characteristic is the wonder of immersion in the one relish of sovereign freedom."

The complete verse continues by declaring that the supreme Goddess Bhairavī shines eternally **of herself**.

And that is a perfect opening.

Because the whole commentary has been moving toward a single question.

Does consciousness need something else to make it conscious?

Does awareness require another independent awareness to illuminate it?

And if so, what illuminates that second awareness?

Another?

And another?

Congratulations.

You've invented an infinite regression machine.

Abhinavagupta refuses it.

**Bhairavī shines through her own nature.**

Let's dismantle that magnificent compound.

### Svātantrya - स्वातन्त्र्य

Sovereign freedom, self-dependence, autonomy.

From the *sva-tantra* family:

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**तन्त्र - tantra**

System, framework, governing structure.

The adjective:

**स्वतन्त्र - svatantra**

Independent.

The abstract noun:

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Freedom or independence.

In Trika, this is not merely freedom from somebody else's instructions.

It is the supreme conscious power of manifestation.

### Ekarasa - एकरस

**एक - eka**

One.

**रस - rasa**

Taste, flavour, essence, relish.

Thus:

**एकरस - ekarasa**

Of one essence, possessing one undivided relish.

The expression conveys a conscious fullness not fundamentally fragmented into competing ultimate realities.

### Āveśa - आवेश

Entry, immersion, permeation.

From *ā-√viś*, to enter.

The noun:

**आवेश - āveśa**.

Here, the sense is immersion in the one relish of freedom.

### Camatkāra - चमत्कार

Wonder, astonished self-apprehension, delighted revelation.

One of Abhinavagupta's most important philosophical and aesthetic words.

Now this does not merely mean that Bhairavī is impressed by something unexpected.

She's not standing outside the universe saying:

"Bloody hell, look at that."

The wonder concerns the intrinsic self-relish of conscious freedom.

### Ekalakṣaṇā - एकलक्षणा

**एक - eka**

One.

**लक्षण - lakṣaṇa**

Mark, characteristic, defining feature.

Feminine nominative singular:

**एकलक्षणा - ekalakṣaṇā**.

Now the entire compound identifies the Goddess through one defining feature:

**The marvellous self-relish of sovereign conscious freedom.**

### Parā - परा

Supreme.

Feminine nominative singular.

### Bhagavatī - भगवती

Blessed or venerable Goddess.

### Bhairavī - भैरवी

Bhairavī, the supreme Goddess.

### Svayam - स्वयम्

Herself, of herself, spontaneously.

This last word is crucial.

The Goddess does not borrow her luminosity from an independently existing reality.

She shines of herself.

### Metre laboratory

The verse is composed in **anuṣṭubh**, four pādas of eight syllables.

The opening eight-syllable pāda can be scanned:

*svā-tan-tryai-ka-ra-sā-ve-śa*

Eight syllabic positions.

The compound continues through the next pāda.

This is worth noticing.

Abhinavagupta has constructed an enormous philosophical compound while maintaining a regular metrical structure.

The man is performing metaphysics with a ruler.

A very elegant ruler.

### Philosophical synthesis

The first verse gives us the entire doctrine in miniature.

Prakāśa.

Vimarśa.

Svātantrya.

Camatkāra.

The supreme Goddess is self-luminous awareness whose freedom includes intrinsic self-apprehension.

Not a passive substance waiting for an external cause.

Not a psychological ego.

Not a separate divine object occupying a particular location.

But the conscious reality through which locations, objects and minds are manifest.

And because her self-revealing nature is intrinsic, she doesn't need to become complete after producing a universe.

She is already complete.

**Manifestation expresses fullness; it doesn't manufacture it.**

That is where our summary begins.


## 2. The first textual variant - *Svabhāva* or *Svabhāsā*?

VERSE 2 · P. 212

The two witnesses differ immediately.

KASHMIR SERIES

**स्वभावयोगः**

*Svabhāvayogaḥ*

The conjunction or inherent condition of one's own nature.

GRETIL

**स्वभासायोगः**

*Svabhāsāyogaḥ*

The conjunction or operation of one's own luminosity.

The transmitted pāda differs accordingly:

**तस्याः स्वभावयोगो यः** - *tasyāḥ svabhāvayogo yaḥ.*

Or:

**तस्याः स्वभासायोगो यः** - *tasyāḥ svabhāsāyogo yaḥ.*

This is a real lexical variant, not a harmless spelling difference. Both are compatible with the surrounding philosophy, but they emphasize distinct aspects: intrinsic nature versus intrinsic shining. Both fit the eight-syllable pāda, so the metre alone cannot decide between them. ; \]

Let's learn the distinction.

### Svabhāva - स्वभाव

Own nature.

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**भाव - bhāva**

Being, condition, nature.

Thus:

**स्वभाव - svabhāva**

Intrinsic nature.

### Svabhāsā - स्वभासा

Own luminosity or self-manifestation in the alternative compound.

Related to:

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**भास् - bhās**

To shine or appear.

The reading emphasizes self-revealing illumination.

### Yoga - योग

Conjunction, relation, application, operation.

From √**युज् - yuj**, to join.

Now the rest of the verse declares that this power is:

**अनिरुद्ध - aniruddha**

Unobstructed.

And:

**सदोदित - sadodita**

Always arisen, ever manifest.

It then extends across the differentiated field from **Sadāśiva** down to **earth**, and through phenomena such as blue, yellow and pleasure.

Now that is important.

The divine power isn't present only in extraordinary spiritual experiences.

It's the ground of ordinary appearing.

Blue.

Yellow.

Happiness.

The whole scale from the highest divine principle to the most ordinary perceived qualities.

### Aniruddha - अनिरुद्ध

**अन् - an-**

Not.

**निरुद्ध - niruddha**

Obstructed, restrained.

From *ni-√rudh*, to obstruct or restrain.

Thus:

**अनिरुद्ध - aniruddha**

Unobstructed.

### Sadodita - सदोदित

**सदा - sadā**

Always.

**उदित - udita**

Arisen, manifested.

From *ud-√i*, to rise.

Through sandhi:

*sadā + udita → sadodita.*

Because:

**ā + u → o**.

Thus:

**सदोदित - sadodita**

Ever manifest.

### Sadāśiva - सदाशिव

The pure divine principle of Sadāśiva.

### Dharā - धरा

Earth.

The highest and lowest ends of the relevant ontological range are therefore brought together.

### The blue-pot scandal

Imagine somebody saying:

"I can see divine consciousness only in an extraordinary mystical vision."

Abhinavagupta replies:

"What about blue?"

"Blue?"

"Yes."

"That's just a colour."

"And what makes its appearance possible?"

The student looks rather concerned.

"What about happiness?"

"Also consciousness."

"What about yellow?"

"Also."

"Mountains?"

"Also."

"The bloody clay pot?"

"Unfortunately, yes."

The point is not that every object is identical in its manifest properties.

It is that every appearance is grounded in the same conscious power.

**The Goddess is unobstructed in the manifestation of differentiation.**

And that's why the next verse speaks of all these objects as **self-appearing within conscious manifestation**.


## 3. The universal field shines through its own nature

VERSE 3 · P. 212

**स्वयम्प्रथनशालिभिः**

*Svayaṃprathanaśālibhiḥ.*

"Possessing the capacity to appear of themselves."

This is one of the key expressions in verse 3.

Abhinavagupta describes the manifestation of diverse states-blue, yellow, pleasure and the rest-and then speaks of the form of consciousness whose essence is self-awareness.

### Svayam - स्वयम्

Of itself, spontaneously.

### Prathana - प्रथन

Manifestation, spreading forth, becoming apparent.

From √**प्रथ् - prath**, to spread or become evident.

### Śālin - शालिन्

Possessing, endowed with.

Thus:

**प्रथनशालिन् - prathanaśālin**

Possessing the power of manifestation.

Instrumental plural:

**प्रथनशालिभिः - prathanaśālibhiḥ**.

Now add:

**स्वयम् - svayam**

Of themselves.

So the expression describes states appearing through their own manifestation, within the greater conscious reality.

### Saṃvidākāra - संविदाकार

**संविद् - saṃvid**

Consciousness.

**आकार - ākāra**

Form, mode, appearance.

Thus:

**संविदाकार - saṃvidākāra**

The form or aspect of consciousness.

### Svasaṃvedana - स्वसंवेदन

Self-apprehension.

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**संवेदन - saṃvedana**

Conscious apprehension.

Thus:

**स्वसंवेदन - svasaṃvedana**

Awareness of itself.

### Sāraka - सारक

Having something as essence or central nature in the compound's use.

The verse identifies the manifested form of consciousness through self-awareness.

Now here's the crucial distinction.

It is not saying that every physical object possesses a fully developed human personality.

Nor that blue pigment is secretly constructing philosophical arguments.

It is saying that the appearance of any object belongs to consciousness's power of manifestation.

**The manifested content is distinguishable; the light in which it appears is not borrowed from another independent consciousness.**

And now Abhinavagupta asks something wonderful.

What counts as a *pramāṇa*?

What establishes cognition?

And how does the answer apply to a child, an animal and a learned philosopher?

The next verses are among the most philosophically important in the whole sequence.


## 4. What is a *pramāṇa*? And why does a baby already know how to experience?

VERSE 4 · KASHMIR SERIES P. 212

The defining expression is:

**स्वस्वसंवेदनं नाम प्रमाणम्।**

*Svasvasaṃvedanaṃ nāma pramāṇam.*

"What is called a means or establishment of knowledge is self-apprehension."

The verse immediately adds that this appears equally among:

**बाल - bāla** - children.

**तिर्यक् - tiryak** - animals.

**सर्वविद् - sarvavid** - the learned or those possessing comprehensive knowledge.

Now that is a magnificent epistemological intervention.

We've gone from the self-shining Goddess to the nature of proof.

And Abhinavagupta doesn't begin by demanding a university degree.

He asks what is already present in the most elementary experience.

### Sva - स्व

Own, oneself.

The repetition **स्वस्व - svasva** intensifies the reference to one's own condition or to what belongs to each individual cognizer.

### Saṃvedana - संवेदन

Conscious apprehension, awareness.

Related to *sam-√vid*, to know or apprehend.

Thus:

**स्वसंवेदन - svasaṃvedana**

Self-apprehension or awareness of one's own awareness.

### Pramāṇa - प्रमाण

Means or instrument of valid cognition; also a measure or authority, according to context.

The familiar philosophical derivation involves:

**प्र - pra**

Forth, completely.

**√मा - mā**

To measure or ascertain.

With the instrumental nominal formation:

**प्रमाण - pramāṇa**.

Now, the important qualification is that different Indian philosophical schools use *pramāṇa* differently.

Nyāya, Buddhist epistemology, Mīmāṃsā and Pratyabhijñā do not simply share one uncontroversial theory of knowledge.

Abhinavagupta's summary here emphasizes the immediate self-manifestation of conscious apprehension.

### Bāla - बाल

Child, young person.

### Tiryak - तिर्यक्

Literally transverse or going across; traditionally used for nonhuman animals.

The verse isn't assessing an animal's worth.

It is contrasting levels of formal intellectual sophistication.

### Sarvavid - सर्वविद्

**सर्व - sarva**

All.

**विद् - vid**

Knowing.

Thus:

**सर्वविद् - sarvavid**

One who knows everything, or the highly learned in the immediate contrast.

Genitive plural in the verse's combined expression:

**बालतिर्यक्सर्वविदाम् - bālatiryaksarvavidām**.

### Sāmyenaiva - साम्येनैव

Equally, in the same manner.

**साम्य - sāmya**

Equality.

Instrumental singular:

**साम्येन - sāmyena**.

Then:

**एव - eva**

Indeed, precisely.

Through sandhi:

**साम्येनैव - sāmyenaiva**.

### The philosophical point

Now imagine three beings.

A newborn child.

A dog.

A highly trained Sanskrit philosopher.

Their knowledge differs enormously.

The philosopher may understand inference, grammar and metaphysics.

The dog may recognize familiar individuals, places and scents.

The newborn may respond to contact, sound and other sensory conditions without possessing developed linguistic concepts.

Their capacities aren't identical.

But Abhinavagupta emphasizes something they have in common.

**Experience is manifest to them.**

The occurrence of cognition doesn't wait for the acquisition of a philosophical vocabulary.

### The baby and the epistemologist

Imagine an epistemologist approaching a newborn.

"Before you are permitted to experience your blanket, please establish the validity of your sensory apparatus."

The baby cries.

The epistemologist says:

"That's not a proof."

The baby continues experiencing.

A severe administrative defeat.

But the serious point is that **conscious apprehension is not produced by a later theoretical demonstration of its existence**.

And this is why the verse prepares the next two.

The senses, inference, testimony and other means of knowledge may have important roles.

But their ability to operate presupposes the manifestation of cognition.

### A necessary distinction

This is not the claim that a baby's perceptions are infallible.

Nor that an animal possesses the same inferential capacities as a philosopher.

Nor that everything appearing in awareness is automatically valid knowledge about an external object.

**The fact of conscious appearing and the correctness of a particular judgment are distinct questions.**

Abhinavagupta is addressing the former as the ground in which claims of knowledge become possible.

The following verses explain the status of the latter's instruments.


## 5. The grand parade of *pramāṇas* - Senses, inference, testimony and the rest

VERSE 5 · P. 212

Abhinavagupta now names several familiar instruments or procedures of knowledge.

Among the actual terms are:

**इन्द्रियाणि - indriyāṇi**

Sense faculties.

**त्रिरूपं लिङ्गम् - trirūpaṃ liṅgam**

A three-characterized inferential sign.

**परवचः - paravacaḥ**

Another person's statement or testimony.

**सारूप्यम् - sārūpyam**

Similarity.

**अन्यथायोगः - anyathāyogaḥ**

A relation requiring otherwise-explanation, relevant to postulation.

**प्रतीत्यनुदयः - pratītyanudayaḥ**

The nonarising of an apprehension, relevant to nonperception.

And finally:

**यमः - yamaḥ**

A term involving restraint or regulation whose precise function in this tightly compressed list should not be overconfidently assigned without further textual evidence.

Now, this is a rather spectacular philosophical collection.

Abhinavagupta is naming methods associated with several Indian epistemological traditions.

But his purpose is not to demonstrate that every school agrees on precisely the same enumeration.

His purpose is to compare their functions with the self-manifestation of awareness.

### Indriya - इन्द्रिय

Sense faculty.

Neuter nominative or accusative plural:

**इन्द्रियाणि - indriyāṇi**.

These are the faculties through which sensory apprehension becomes possible.

### Trirūpa - त्रिरूप

Three-characterized.

**त्रि - tri**

Three.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form or characteristic.

Thus:

**त्रिरूप - trirūpa**.

The context is the classical three characteristics of a valid inferential sign, particularly prominent in Buddhist and related Indian logical discussions.

A reason must be appropriately present in the subject, connected with instances possessing the property to be proved, and appropriately excluded from contrary instances.

The exact formulation differs by school, but the threefold structure is recognizable.

### Liṅga - लिङ्ग

Sign, mark, inferential indicator.

The word also has other important Śaiva meanings, but here we're discussing inference.

Imagine smoke indicating fire.

The smoke can serve as an inferential sign under appropriate conditions.

But the presence of smoke doesn't independently create consciousness.

Its inferential significance must be apprehended.

### Paravacas - परवचस्

**पर - para**

Another.

**वचस् - vacas**

Speech, statement.

Thus:

**परवचस् - paravacas**

Another person's statement.

In the verse's connected form:

**परवचःक्रमः - paravacaḥkramaḥ**.

The testimony of others belongs to an intelligible mode of acquiring knowledge.

But the hearer must apprehend it.

### Sārūpya - सारूप्य

Similarity, resemblance.

From:

**स - sa**

With, having.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form.

The abstract formation:

**सारूप्य - sārūpya**.

This points toward knowledge acquired by comparison or resemblance.

### Anyathāyoga - अन्यथायोग

**अन्यथा - anyathā**

Otherwise.

**योग - yoga**

Connection or relation.

The expression can be interpreted through reasoning that posits something otherwise inexplicable.

But we shouldn't pretend the present verse gives a complete independent theory of *arthāpatti*.

It names the procedure compactly.

### Pratītyanudaya - प्रतीत्यनुदय

Now here's a difficult compound.

**प्रतीति - pratīti**

Apprehension, recognition, cognition.

**अनुदय - anudaya**

Nonarising.

Thus:

**प्रतीत्यनुदय - pratītyanudaya**

Failure of an apprehension to arise.

This has a relationship with reasoning through nonperception.

But the exact epistemological conditions need to be supplied by the relevant school.

The term's occurrence here does not settle every dispute concerning *anupalabdhi*.

### Yama - यम

Restraint, control, regulation.

The form also names Yama, the deity of death, in other contexts.

Here a sudden identification with the death-god would make very little sense in this catalogue of epistemic procedures.

But the precise technical interpretation of the final item is less secure than that of the preceding ones.

That uncertainty should remain visible.

### The epistemology Olympics

Imagine the philosophical schools arriving with their instruments.

Nyāya brings perception and inference.

Mīmāṃsā brings testimony and arguments concerning postulation.

Buddhist logicians arrive with their rigorous *liṅga* conditions.

Everyone is carrying an enormous methodological clipboard.

Then Abhinavagupta asks:

"Excellent. And through what does any of this become apprehended?"

Silence.

"Through cognition?"

"Precisely."

The point isn't that the instruments are useless.

It's that they operate **within the manifestation of awareness**.

And the next verse says exactly that.


## 6. They are doors, not the consciousness that walks through them

VERSE 6 · P. 212

The pivotal expression is:

**द्वारमात्रे निरूप्यते।**

*Dvāramātre nirūpyate.*

"They are understood as mere doors."

And the conclusion describes self-awareness as:

**अविच्छेदप्रथामयम्**

*Avicchedaprathāmayam.*

"Consisting of uninterrupted manifestation."

Now that's a phenomenal philosophical distinction.

### Dvāra - द्वार

Door, opening, entrance, means of access.

### Mātra - मात्र

Merely, only, no more than.

Thus:

**द्वारमात्र - dvāramātra**

Merely a doorway or instrument.

### Nirūpyate - निरूप्यते

Is characterized, described or determined.

A passive present verbal form.

### Aviccheda - अविच्छेद

Noninterruption, absence of separation.

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**विच्छेद - viccheda**

Interruption or severance.

Thus:

**अविच्छेद - aviccheda**

Unbroken continuity.

### Prathā - प्रथा

Manifestation, disclosure.

Related to √**प्रथ् - prath**, to spread or become evident.

### Maya - मय

Consisting of.

Thus:

**अविच्छेदप्रथामय - avicchedaprathāmaya**

Consisting of uninterrupted manifestation.

Now here's the philosophical point.

A sense faculty is an instrument.

An inferential sign is an instrument.

Testimony is an instrument.

Comparison and reasoning are instruments.

But **the intrinsic appearing of cognition isn't manufactured by the theoretical definition of any particular instrument**.

The instruments contribute to the acquisition and evaluation of specific knowledge.

They do not explain away the conscious reality in which they become operative.

### The door analogy

Imagine someone entering a room.

One doorway is large.

Another narrow.

One has a lock.

Another is open.

Now you can study the architecture of the doors.

Perfectly worthwhile.

But the existence of a doorway isn't itself the person entering.

Similarly, *pramāṇa* procedures are meaningful.

But the self-manifestation of consciousness isn't identical to the formal catalogue of those procedures.

### Does this abolish *pramāṇas*?

No.

Abhinavagupta is not inviting us to believe whatever we like.

An invalid inference can remain invalid.

A false statement can remain false.

A sensory illusion can be corrected.

The claim concerns the **ontological priority of conscious appearing**, not the universal validity of every judgment.

This distinction is absolutely essential if we're going to preserve the philosophical rigor of Pratyabhijñā.

And now, having demolished any temptation to identify consciousness with one formal epistemic procedure, Abhinavagupta introduces animals again.

Because an animal can experience without knowing the Sanskrit terminology for inference.

A wonderfully effective philosophical demonstration.


## 7. The dog hasn't read Dharmakīrti - Yet awareness still appears

VERSE 7 · P. 212

The central phrase is:

**तेषामपि तिरश्चां हि समा संवित्प्रकाशते।**

*Teṣām api tiraścāṃ hi samā saṃvit prakāśate.*

"Even among those animals, consciousness shines forth equally."

Now that is a beautiful statement.

And it's important not to misread it.

Abhinavagupta isn't claiming that all animals possess equal conceptual capacities.

He's distinguishing the occurrence of awareness from knowledge of the technical terms used to explain it.

### Yeṣām - येषाम्

Of whom.

Genitive plural.

### Na - न

Not.

### Akṣa - अक्ष

Sense organ, faculty.

The word also has other meanings, including axis or die, depending upon context.

Here its relation to epistemology is relevant.

### Trirūpa - त्रिरूप

Three-characterized.

We just studied this.

### Nāma - नाम

Name.

The opening of the verse refers to beings unfamiliar even with the **names** of such epistemological categories.

Now that's a lovely distinction.

Knowing how to experience is not the same as knowing the conceptual vocabulary of epistemology.

### Abhijñatā - अभिज्ञता

Familiarity, knowledge, recognition.

Related to:

**अभि - abhi**

Toward.

**√ज्ञा - jñā**

To know.

The abstract noun:

**अभिज्ञता - abhijñatā**

The condition of knowing or being familiar with.

### Tiraścām - तिरश्चाम्

Of animals.

Genitive plural of a form related to **tiryañc**, going across or transverse.

Now the Sanskrit declension is irregular-looking.

It's worth recognizing the form rather than trying to generate it mechanically from an ordinary *-a* stem.

### Samā saṃvit - समा संवित्

Equal consciousness, the same conscious luminosity in the relevant respect.

**समा - samā**

Equal.

**संवित् - saṃvit**

Consciousness.

### Prakāśate - प्रकाशते

Shines forth, becomes manifest.

From *pra-√kāś*, to shine or appear.

Now the philosophical claim.

An animal may not know what *trirūpaliṅga* means.

It may never have encountered the theory of inference.

But it can have experiences.

It may recognize danger.

Respond to familiar individuals.

Find food.

Navigate its environment.

The precise range of capacities differs across species.

But the occurrence of conscious apprehension doesn't depend upon knowing the Sanskrit definition of a *pramāṇa*.

### The gecko and the logician

Imagine a gecko staring at a wall.

A Buddhist logician approaches.

"Before you catch that insect, please establish the three characteristics of your inferential sign."

The gecko stares at the insect.

"Have you satisfied the conditions of *trairūpya*?"

The gecko eats the insect.

The logician is devastated.

But here's the serious point.

**The conscious apprehension of an environment need not presuppose explicit theoretical knowledge of epistemological categories.**

This doesn't invalidate epistemology.

It identifies its subject matter as more fundamental than the formal descriptions we construct.

### A modern qualification

The verse shouldn't be presented as conclusive proof that every organism possesses the same subjective consciousness.

Animal consciousness is a subject of contemporary scientific and philosophical investigation.

Different species have different sensory and neural systems.

The traditional argument emphasizes the applicability of consciousness beyond formally trained human philosophers.

That's the doctrinal claim.

And now Abhinavagupta draws the conclusion.

If the nature of consciousness is self-apprehension, then its intrinsic self-apprehension is precisely **Parā Vāk**.

Supreme speech.

And this is the pivotal transition.

The whole theory of knowledge has led back to the Goddess of speech.



## 8. स्वरूपामर्शनम् - The Goddess is the awareness of awareness

VERSE 8 · KASHMIR SERIES P. 213

The summary now gives the decisive identification:

**स्वरूपामर्शनं यच्च तदेव परवाग्वपुः।**

*Svarūpāmarśanaṃ yac ca tad eva paravāgvapuḥ.*

"And that apprehension of one's own nature is precisely the body of supreme speech."

There it is.

One of the central doctrines of the entire *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*.

**Parā Vāk is not merely the highest audible sound. She is consciousness's intrinsic apprehension of itself.**

Let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Svarūpa - स्वरूप

Own nature.

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**रूप - rūpa**

Nature, form.

Thus:

**स्वरूप - svarūpa**.

### Āmarśana - आमर्शन

Apprehension, taking cognizance, reflexive grasp.

Related to:

**आ - ā**

Toward.

**√मृश् - mṛś**

To touch, apprehend or examine.

Now combine:

**स्वरूपामर्शन - svarūpāmarśana**

Apprehension of one's own nature.

The sandhi is straightforward:

*svarūpa + āmarśana → svarūpāmarśana.*

Because:

**a + ā → ā**.

### Parā - परा

Supreme.

### Vāk - वाक्

Speech.

Now this noun is feminine.

And its inflection is irregular-looking compared with an ordinary *-ā* stem.

**वाक् - vāk**

Speech.

The compound:

**परवाक् - paravāk**

Supreme speech.

Here:

**परवाग्वपुः - paravāgvapuḥ**.

Why *vāg*?

Because the final *k* becomes voiced before the following *v*.

### Vapus - वपुस्

Body, form, appearance, nature.

Neuter nominative or accusative singular:

**वपुः - vapuḥ**.

So:

**परवाग्वपुः - paravāgvapuḥ**

Having the body or nature of supreme speech.

### The crucial philosophical distinction

Now this is where the doctrine becomes enormously interesting.

Normally, we think of speech as sound.

Something spoken aloud.

But Abhinavagupta has already distinguished several levels of speech.

Vaikharī is outwardly articulated language.

Madhyamā involves a subtler differentiation of word and meaning.

Paśyantī is more unified.

And Parā Vāk is the supreme ground of expressive consciousness.

So when he identifies *svarūpāmarśana* with Parā Vāk, he isn't saying:

"Awareness always sounds like someone speaking Sanskrit."

He's saying that **the very nature of awareness includes a self-expressive, reflexive capacity**.

That capacity is the deepest ground of speech.

### The silent philosopher problem

Imagine a person in complete silence.

No audible words.

No movement of the mouth.

No written text.

Can consciousness still be present?

Obviously, within the framework of the doctrine, yes.

So the supreme speech cannot simply be identified with air striking the vocal apparatus.

It is more fundamental.

And this is why Parā Vāk is called the body of self-apprehension.

### The relation to Vimarśa

Remember:

**प्रकाश - prakāśa**

Luminosity, manifestation.

And:

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Intrinsic reflexive awareness.

Parā Vāk expresses this *vimarśa*.

Awareness is not merely something in which objects appear while the ground remains wholly inert.

It has the capacity to reveal itself.

And that's why the supreme Goddess is not merely another object illuminated by consciousness.

She is the self-revealing power itself.

### A useful contrast with Buddhism

Now we should be precise.

Some Buddhist epistemologists also defend forms of reflexive awareness, often discussed under *svasaṃvedana* or *svasaṃvitti*.

But agreement about a self-revealing aspect of cognition does **not** automatically imply agreement about a universal Śiva-consciousness.

Dharmakīrtian Buddhist traditions and Trika differ substantially in their accounts of continuity, the self and ultimate reality.

So the shared vocabulary is philosophically interesting.

It isn't evidence that the systems secretly assert exactly the same ontology.

Abhinavagupta's identification of self-apprehension with **Parā Vāk** belongs specifically to his Śaiva metaphysical framework.

And now the next verse adds an important consequence.

If self-revealing awareness is the ground of manifestation, it is not dependent upon an externally imposed mechanism for each appearance.

The Goddess remains sovereign.


## 9. The Goddess does not outsource her own luminosity

VERSE 9 · P. 213

The key expression is:

**प्रथने पारतन्त्र्यं हि न जातु भजते क्वचित्।**

*Prathane pāratantryaṃ hi na jātu bhajate kvacit.*

"In manifestation, she never at any time becomes dependent upon another."

This is one of the clearest declarations of *svātantrya* in the summary.

### Prathana - प्रथन

Manifestation, becoming apparent.

We've met this word repeatedly.

### Pāratantrya - पारतन्त्र्य

Dependence upon another.

The conceptual opposite of:

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**.

Let's distinguish them.

**स्वतन्त्र - svatantra**

Independent.

**परतन्त्र - paratantra**

Dependent upon another.

The abstract noun:

**पारतन्त्र्य - pāratantrya**

Dependence.

Now here's a magnificent contrast.

The Supreme's characteristic is *svātantrya*.

Not *pāratantrya*.

### Na jātu - न जातु

Never, not at any time.

A strong negative expression.

### Bhajate - भजते

Partakes of, enters into, experiences, assumes.

From √**भज् - bhaj**, to share, distribute or partake.

Third-person singular middle voice.

### Kvacit - क्वचित्

Anywhere, at any time, in any instance.

Now the claim is emphatic.

The supreme conscious power doesn't require an independently existing second source to make its manifestations appear.

### The divine outsourcing department

Imagine Bhairavī attempting to illuminate the universe.

"Unfortunately, I have to wait for the external consciousness supplier."

"Who is that?"

"Some larger deity."

"And who illuminates the larger deity?"

"Another."

"And who illuminates that one?"

"Another."

Congratulations.

We have outsourced self-revealing consciousness to an infinite chain of subcontractors.

Abhinavagupta refuses this.

The very meaning of supreme conscious freedom excludes dependence upon an independently existing illuminator.

**The Goddess doesn't need another light to be luminous.**

### What this does not mean

It doesn't imply that ordinary finite beings are causally independent.

A person needs food, oxygen, sleep and countless other conditions.

Nor does it mean that every worldly event occurs without causal relations.

The claim concerns the supreme self-revealing ground.

Its manifestation cannot be explained through dependence upon an entirely alien conscious principle.

Now the philosophical consequence is immediate.

If the supreme self-apprehension isn't dependent upon another, what about linguistic convention?

Can convention create the ultimate ground?

Can an agreed association between word and meaning be the cause of consciousness itself?

No.

And the next verse says so.


## 10. सङ्केतरहितम् - Supreme speech is not created by convention

VERSE 10 · P. 213

Abhinavagupta concludes:

**सङ्केतरहितं स्वस्वरूपविमर्शनम्।**

*Saṃketarahitaṃ svasvarūpavimarśanam.*

"The apprehension of one's own nature is free from convention."

That is the argument from Part XVIII compressed into one beautiful expression.

### Saṃketa - सङ्केत

Convention, agreement, designation.

In linguistic philosophy, the term can refer to a socially or intentionally established relation between a word and what it signifies.

For example:

The Sanskrit word **गो - go** conventionally denotes a cow.

The English word "cow" has a different sound sequence.

The connection between these conventional words and their referent is learned.

### Rahita - रहित

Without, deprived of, lacking.

Thus:

**सङ्केतरहित - saṃketarahita**

Free from convention.

### Svasvarūpa - स्वस्वरूप

One's very own nature.

The repetition **sva-svarūpa** emphasizes intrinsic selfhood.

### Vimarśana - विमर्शन

Apprehension, reflection, reflexive awareness.

From the *vi-√mṛś* family.

Thus:

**स्वस्वरूपविमर्शन - svasvarūpavimarśana**

The apprehension of one's own nature.

### The philosophical distinction

Now here's the crucial point.

The verse doesn't say ordinary words are meaningless.

Abhinavagupta has spent hundreds of pages studying their meanings.

He is distinguishing **the conventionally established semantic relation** from **the self-revealing conscious power that makes such relations apprehensible**.

The word "cow" depends upon linguistic convention.

But the capacity to apprehend an agreement cannot be explained solely by an endless regress of earlier agreements.

And that's why the supreme level of speech is described as *asāṅketika*, nonconventional.

### The cow returns for the twenty-fourth installment

Imagine a cow standing in a field.

One person says:

"Go."

Another says:

"Cow."

Another uses a different language.

The cow continues eating.

Now the words differ.

The conventional linguistic associations differ.

But those distinctions don't create three independently existing conscious grounds.

The capacity for apprehension is more fundamental than the particular convention.

This is Abhinavagupta's point.

### The danger of turning nonconventional into magical arbitrariness

Now, very important.

**Nonconventional does not mean every sound automatically has whatever meaning someone wishes.**

The ordinary semantics of language still operate through established conditions.

The supreme self-apprehension is nonconventional because it isn't created by a prior linguistic agreement.

That's a different claim.

And this distinction prevents the philosophical account from degenerating into random phonemic mysticism.

The Goddess's freedom doesn't abolish the meaningful structure of conventional speech.

It explains why such structures are possible.

And now comes the next consequence.

If supreme speech is independent of linguistic convention, it is likewise not ultimately delimited by spatial location, temporal sequence, Māyā or the bodily production of sounds.

The eleventh verse is an absolutely magnificent compound.

And a nightmare to pronounce.


## 11. देशकालकलामायास्थानघातक्रियोत्तरम् - Beyond space, time, Māyā and the striking of phonemes

VERSE 11 · P. 213

The verse begins with this enormous compound:

**देशकालकलामायास्थानघातक्रियोत्तरम्**

*Deśakālakalāmāyāsthānaghātakriyottaram.*

"Beyond place, time, limiting power, Māyā, articulatory location, impact and activity."

Now this is one of those compounds that makes learning Sanskrit worthwhile.

Because it looks impossible.

Then you dismantle it.

And it becomes a beautifully organized philosophical statement.

Let's break it apart.

### Deśa - देश

Place, spatial location.

### Kāla - काल

Time.

Notice the long **ā**.

### Kalā - कला

Power, portion, limited agency.

Now don't confuse:

**काल - kāla**

Time.

With:

**कला - kalā**

Power, part, digit.

The vowel lengths differ.

And the philosophical meanings differ.

### Māyā - माया

The differentiating and limiting power.

In Trika, Māyā has a specific role in the manifestation of finite difference.

It isn't simply an absolute denial that anything appears.

### Sthāna - स्थान

Place, location.

From √**स्था - sthā**, to stand.

Now within Sanskrit phonetics, *sthāna* can refer to a place of articulation.

For example:

Dental.

Labial.

Palatal.

Velar.

So the word is important both cosmologically and phonetically.

### Ghāta - घात

Impact, striking.

From √**हन् - han**, to strike.

In traditional phonetic explanations, sound-production can be described through articulatory impact or contact.

### Kriyā - क्रिया

Action.

From √**कृ - kṛ**, to do.

### Uttara - उत्तर

Higher, beyond, surpassing.

Here the compound ends with:

**उत्तरम् - uttaram**.

Thus:

**देशकालकलामायास्थानघातक्रियोत्तरम्**

Beyond the limitations of these differentiating conditions.

### A crucial phonetic insight

Now think about ordinary audible speech.

To pronounce a Sanskrit consonant, you engage a particular articulatory configuration.

Different sounds involve different places and manners of articulation.

The event has duration.

It occurs at a time.

It is physically produced.

Now the supreme Parā Vāk cannot simply be identical with that articulated acoustic event.

Because the acoustic event presupposes the conditions of space, time and bodily activity.

And Abhinavagupta is describing Parā Vāk as their conscious ground.

This is why the verse includes both cosmological and phonetic terminology.

**Parā Vāk is beyond the limiting conditions of audible articulation without being absent from audible speech.**

That's the distinction.

### The cosmic speech therapist

Imagine Parā Vāk arriving for a pronunciation examination.

"Where is your place of articulation?"

"Not restricted to one."

"Are you dental?"

"No."

"Palatal?"

"No."

"Labial?"

"No."

"How do you produce yourself?"

"Through intrinsic freedom."

The speech therapist puts down the clipboard.

"This patient is completely unclassifiable."

Exactly.

Ordinary speech can be classified through articulatory conditions.

Supreme speech is the conscious power through which those differentiated conditions become manifest.

### Paripūrṇa - परिपूर्ण

Completely full.

From:

**परि - pari**

Completely, all around.

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa**

Full.

### Sarvākāravilakṣaṇa - सर्वाकारविलक्षण

Different from, or not reducible to, every particular form.

**सर्व - sarva**

All.

**आकार - ākāra**

Form, configuration.

**विलक्षण - vilakṣaṇa**

Distinct, different in character.

Now here's a subtle philosophical issue.

If the Goddess is beyond all forms, how can she also be the ground of every form?

Because **not being exhausted by a particular form** is different from **being completely absent from every manifestation**.

The supreme conscious ground isn't limited to blue.

Or a pot.

Or a physical vibration.

Or one articulated vowel.

Yet those manifestations become apparent through consciousness.

**Transcendence of limitation doesn't require absence from manifestation.**

And that prepares us for the next verse.

The divine Aham.

The supreme form of Śuddhavidyā.

And the extraordinary fact that it includes both "I" and "this."


## 12. अहम् - The supreme I includes the world without becoming a finite ego

VERSE 12 · P. 213

The pivotal expression is:

**शुद्धविद्यात्मकं रूपमहमित्युभयात्मकम्।**

*Śuddhavidyātmakaṃ rūpam aham ity ubhayātmakam.*

"The form having the nature of pure knowledge is called *Aham*, comprising both aspects."

Now that is a beautiful return to Part XIX.

But the philosophical meaning is richer because we've now studied the relationship between self-apprehension, linguistic convention and differentiated manifestation.

### Śuddha - शुद्ध

Pure.

### Vidyā - विद्या

Knowledge.

Related to √**विद् - vid**, to know.

Thus:

**शुद्धविद्या - śuddhavidyā**

Pure knowledge.

Within the Śaiva tattva system, Śuddhavidyā has a particular place among the pure *tattvas*.

But in this verse, the phrase also identifies a pure form of conscious manifestation.

### Ātmaka - आत्मक

Having the nature of.

Thus:

**शुद्धविद्यात्मक - śuddhavidyātmaka**

Having the nature of pure knowledge.

### Aham - अहम्

I.

The first-person pronoun, interpreted through the universal divine phonemic structure.

### Ubhaya - उभय

Both.

### Ubhayātmaka - उभयात्मक

Having the nature of both.

Now what are the two aspects?

The fundamental Śaiva distinction is:

**अहम् - aham**

I.

And:

**इदम् - idam**

This.

Subject and object.

The divine I-consciousness isn't merely an isolated finite subject confronting an independently ultimate external world.

It contains the manifestation of the apparently objective field within its own freedom.

### The subtle relation between I and this

Now, don't flatten this into:

"The pot is literally identical to my finite personality."

That's not the claim.

The pot is not your biography.

And the moon isn't your left shoe.

The nondual claim concerns the ultimate conscious reality of the subject-object manifestation.

The difference between I and this remains meaningful as a mode of appearance.

But the underlying ground isn't fundamentally divided.

### The editor's gloss

The Kashmir Series inserts a bracket at this point explaining the inclusion of *idantā*, this-ness, within *ahantā*, I-ness.

That gloss is philosophically appropriate.

But it is editorial.

The unbracketed verse itself says **ubhayātmaka**, comprising both aspects. ; \]

### The magnificent cosmic pronoun

Imagine the supreme *Aham*.

Not saying:

"I am this particular body."

But expressing the conscious fullness through which every manifest "this" becomes apparent.

The entire world is included.

Yet its manifest distinctions are not erased.

This is why *Aham* can be described as universal.

And why the divine alphabet becomes an expression of it.

Now the next verse makes that explicit.

**The same reality is Mātṛkā.**

The Mother of the letters.

And its relation to the universe is expressed through a surprisingly tactile metaphor.

**Melting and solidification.**

The philosophical alphabet is about to change consistency.



## 13. Mātṛkā - The Goddess can melt and condense without changing her ultimate nature

VERSE 13 · KASHMIR SERIES P. 213

The verse identifies the same conscious reality as:

**मातृकारूपम्**

*Mātṛkārūpam.*

"Having the nature of Mātṛkā."

And describes it as the intrinsic nature of the principles beginning with earth.

Then comes a difficult phrase.

GRETIL reads:

**द्रुत्याश्यानस्वरूपतः**

*Drutyāśyānasvarūpataḥ.*

"Through the forms of liquefaction and solidification."

The Kashmir Series's Devanāgarī at the corresponding position is seriously distorted: **शुर्त्याश्यानसव्रूपतः**. The GRETIL reading supplies a coherent interpretation, but it is a textual decision, not an invisible correction. ; \]

Now this is fascinating, because it returns to the distinction we learned from Somānanda in Part XXII.

Mātṛkā can be described as:

**Vikṛta** - transformed.

And:

**Avikṛta** - untransformed.

Her phonemic powers become differentiated in manifestation.

But their ultimate conscious ground is not destroyed.

The imagery of melting and solidification expresses a corresponding relation.

### Mātṛ - मातृ

Mother.

The Sanskrit noun:

**मातृ - mātṛ**.

### Mātṛkā - मातृका

A motherly or matrix-like power; in this context, the divine Mother of phonemes.

Now don't confuse the theological Mātṛkā with an ordinary printed alphabet chart.

The alphabet is a representation of her powers.

The sacred interpretation concerns the generating capacities of consciousness.

### Dharā - धरा

Earth.

The verse refers to principles beginning with earth.

This evokes the differentiated tattva-system we've already studied.

### Nija - निज

Own, intrinsic.

### Vapus - वपुस्

Body, form, nature.

Together:

**निजं वपुः - nijaṃ vapuḥ**

Its own nature or body.

### Pāramārthika - पारमार्थिक

Pertaining to ultimate reality.

From:

**परमार्थ - paramārtha**

Supreme or ultimate reality.

### Ākara - आकर

Mine, source, repository, place of origin.

Now this is worth distinguishing from the ordinary noun **आकार - ākāra**, form.

The transmitted *ākara* has a short second *a*.

The word can convey a source or reservoir.

### Druti - द्रुति

Melting, liquefaction.

Related to √**द्रु - dru**, to run, flow or melt in the relevant lexical family.

### Āśyāna - आश्यान

Congealed, thickened, condensed.

The compound describes two contrasting states.

Flowing.

And congealed.

Now the philosophical image becomes intelligible.

The divine phonemic power can be considered in a relatively fluid, undifferentiated state.

Or a more condensed, differentiated one.

But the underlying reality isn't a second independent substance in either case.

### The cosmic alphabet changes consistency

Imagine Mātṛkā as a vast sea of conscious power.

Its expressive forms become distinguishable.

Letters emerge.

Consonants condense.

The tattvas become manifest.

The differentiated universe appears.

Then the forms are gathered back toward their undivided source.

The analogy isn't literal thermodynamics.

We're not saying Śiva has a melting point.

But the relationship is clear.

**Manifest differentiation is not equivalent to the creation of another ultimate reality.**

And that prepares the next verse.

Because the fluid-condensed distinction becomes represented through two complementary generative powers.

**Bīja.**

Seed.

**Yoni.**

Matrix.

Śiva and Śakti.

The source and its expressive capacity.

And the supreme divine union that makes the universe manifest.


## 14. बीजयोन्यात्मकम् - Seed and matrix, Śiva and Śakti

VERSE 14 · P. 213

Abhinavagupta says:

**बीजयोन्यात्मकं प्रोक्तं शिवशक्तिस्वरूपकम्।**

*Bījayonyātmakaṃ proktaṃ śivaśaktisvarūpakam.*

"It is described as having the nature of seed and generative matrix, constituted by Śiva and Śakti."

Now we return directly to Part XVI.

The seed.

The flower.

The triangle.

The Yoni.

The universe emerging through divine generative freedom.

But here's the key.

This is **not** a dualism of two independent substances.

The verse's next movement describes their intimate conjunction.

### Bīja - बीज

Seed.

An ordinary botanical word that acquires enormous symbolic significance in Tantra.

A seed contains the capacity for further development under appropriate conditions.

Within sacred phonemic systems, *bīja* denotes a concentrated generative sound-power.

### Yoni - योनि

Womb, source, matrix.

Now this is a particularly important word in Kaula literature.

It can have literal anatomical and ritual significance.

It can also be used as a cosmological and metaphysical designation for generative power.

The context determines the level of meaning.

Here the summary concerns the universal generation of differentiated manifestation.

### Bījayoni - बीजयोनि

Seed and matrix.

### Ātmaka - आत्मक

Having the nature of.

Thus:

**बीजयोन्यात्मक - bījayonyātmaka**

Having the nature of seed and matrix.

Notice the glide:

*bīja-yoni + ātmaka → bījayonyātmaka.*

The *i* before the following vowel becomes *y* in the compound.

### Śivaśakti - शिवशक्ति

Śiva and Śakti.

Now the theological distinction.

Śiva represents the self-luminous conscious reality.

Śakti represents its intrinsic self-apprehending, expressive power.

But the tradition does not treat them as ultimately separate.

**Śakti isn't an external battery attached to an otherwise powerless Śiva.**

And Śiva isn't an inert object requiring another independent creator.

They are inseparable aspects of one divine reality.

### Saṅghaṭṭa - सङ्घट्ट

The verse then turns to:

**सङ्घट्ट - saṅghaṭṭa**

Union, conjunction, mutual encounter.

Related to the verbal family involving collision, joining or intimate contact.

The term has a particularly important role in Kaula descriptions of divine union.

### Anyonyocchalita - अन्योन्योच्छलित

Now here's a magnificent compound.

**अन्योन्य - anyonya**

One another, mutually.

**उच्छलित - ucchalita**

Surged up, burst forth, arisen.

Thus:

**अन्योन्योच्छलित - anyonyocchalita**

Mutually upsurging.

Now the vowel sandhi:

*anyonya + ucchalita → anyonyocchalita.*

Because:

**a + u → o**.

### The philosophical meaning

The union of Śiva and Śakti isn't a meeting between two previously independent ultimate substances.

The generative symbolism expresses the dynamic inseparability of luminosity and self-apprehension.

Their mutual upsurge is the manifestation of the world.

### The divine wedding nobody had to arrange

Imagine a cosmic wedding planner.

"Where did Śiva and Śakti first meet?"

### The great textual variant: *Māyīya* versus *Amāyīya*

Now the following phrase is extremely important.

"From the Māyic collection of phonemes."

GRETIL points explicitly to the pure, non-Māyic sound-totality as the source from which the next differentiated creation is described. The Kashmir Series form lacks that negative prefix. The immediate transition to verse 18 makes the non-Māyic reading contextually attractive, but we must not silently replace the printed Kashmir Series wording. ; \]

Let's learn it.

### Māyīya - मायीय

### Amāyīya - अमायीय

### Varṇa - वर्ण

### Puñja - पुञ्ज

**वर्णपुञ्ज - varṇapuñja**

Collection of phonemes.

**वर्णपुञ्जात् - varṇapuñjāt**

### The liberation misconception

"Then why must their appearance be destroyed before the ground can be recognized?"

This is why the tradition can speak of liberation while living.

### A necessary qualification

Now, don't turn this into:

"I have read that everything is Śiva, so I am already a fully liberated sage."

The doctrinal claim about the underlying conscious reality is not identical to the psychological or contemplative achievement of recognizing it.

The tradition distinguishes the supreme reality of consciousness from the limited subject's actual mode of apprehension.

So:

**The possibility of liberation is not negated by manifestation.**

But the recognition of that freedom remains the crucial issue.

And now Abhinavagupta returns to the concentrated divine seed-power.

Vīrya.

Mantra.

Aham.

Visarga.

Anuttara.

The entire philosophy is about to be gathered into the sacred phonemic heart again.

### Aham as the concentrated philosophical heart

**ह - ha**

The expressive culmination associated with Visarga.

### The hidden mantra isn't merely a secret password

Imagine somebody discovering a sacred syllable.

They pronounce it.

Then say:

"Excellent. I have discovered the hidden power."

But Abhinavagupta's philosophical account asks a more demanding question.

**What is the conscious reality through which the mantra becomes manifest and meaningful?**

The secret isn't exhausted by the acoustic sound.

Nor by its written representation.

Nor by the conventional association.

It concerns the very nature of consciousness.

That is why the mantra's hiddenness is connected with *vīrya*, its generative potency.

And now the summary moves from the nature of the mantra to the condition of the practitioner.

The person who recognizes the supreme nature.

And those who approach the same phonemic power through more limited aims.

The distinction is delicate.

## 23. जीवन्मुक्तः सकृद्बुधः - The one who recognizes while living

The verse uses the striking phrase:

**जीवन्मुक्तः सकृद्बुधः।**

*Jīvanmuktaḥ sakṛd budhaḥ.*

"Liberated while living, awakened through recognition."

Now let's unpack it.

### Jīvan - जीवन्

Living.

A present participial form related to √**जीव् - jīv**, to live.

### Mukta - मुक्त

Released, liberated.

Thus:

**जीवन्मुक्त - jīvanmukta**

Liberated while living.

Masculine nominative singular:

**जीवन्मुक्तः - jīvanmuktaḥ**.

### Sakṛt - सकृत्

Once, on one occasion.

In sandhi before a voiced consonant:

**सकृद् - sakṛd**.

### Budha - बुध

Awakened, knowing, wise.

Related to √**बुध् - budh**, to know or awaken.

Masculine nominative singular:

**बुधः - budhaḥ**.

### Jānan - जानन्

Knowing.

From √**ज्ञा - jñā**, to know, through the present participial formation of *jānāti*.

The verse's broader formulation associates liberation with knowing one's own supreme nature.

Now here's the crucial point.

**Recognition concerns the nature of the Self, not the acquisition of one more external object.**

That is what distinguishes it from a merely finite accomplishment.

### What does *sakṛt* mean here?

We should avoid a simplistic interpretation.

It is tempting to say:

"One second of thinking 'I am Śiva' automatically produces permanent liberation."

But the verse doesn't reduce the entire spiritual process to the mechanical repetition of a sentence.

*Sakṛt* emphasizes the decisive character of recognition.

The doctrinal point is that liberation concerns recognition of one's supreme nature, not accumulation of an infinite number of external objects.

The precise relation among initiation, grace, practice, recognition and stability is treated differently across Śaiva texts.

We shouldn't erase that complexity.

### Siddhyādiprepsavaḥ - सिद्ध्यादिप्रेप्सवः

Now the verse contrasts the liberated knower with those seeking accomplishments.

Let's dismantle it.

**सिद्धि - siddhi**

Accomplishment, attainment.

**आदि - ādi**

And so forth.

**प्रेप्सु - prepsu**

Desiring to obtain.

Related to the desiderative of √**आप् - āp**, to obtain.

Thus:

**सिद्ध्यादिप्रेप्सु - siddhyādiprepsu**

Desiring accomplishments and related results.

Masculine nominative plural:

**सिद्ध्यादिप्रेप्सवः - siddhyādiprepsavaḥ**.

Now the important distinction.

The same sacred power can be approached through different aims.

One practitioner seeks recognition of the supreme conscious nature.

Another seeks particular ritual accomplishments.

The tradition makes a distinction between these.

But the verse is not a blanket dismissal of all ritual practice.

Abhinavagupta writes extensively about ritual, initiation and appropriate means.

The distinction concerns the **scope of the desired result**.

### Kḷpta - कॢप्त

Arranged, fashioned, established.

A form associated with √**कॢप् - kḷp**, to arrange or be fitting.

Now this is a particularly unusual Devanāgarī spelling.

Notice the vocalic **ऌ - ḷ** represented in the traditional form.

We encountered the rare vocalic *ḷ* sounds in the alphabetic exposition.

Here's a real lexical form in which the learned Sanskrit root appears with that syllabic vowel.

### Saṃkoca - सङ्कोच

Contraction, restriction.

Now the verse associates the more limited ritual orientation with a structure of contraction.

That doesn't mean the supreme ground is fundamentally destroyed.

It means the practice is organized around a determinate aim.

### The distinction between infinite freedom and a particular accomplishment

Imagine two practitioners.

One asks:

"What is the ultimate nature of consciousness?"

The other asks:

"Can I obtain this particular power?"

Both may employ sacred phonemes.

But the aims differ.

The first concerns recognition of the universal conscious ground.

The second concerns a specific result within manifestation.

Now the result may be understood as legitimate within the ritual tradition.

But it isn't identical with liberation.

**A particular accomplishment is not the same thing as recognizing the ultimate reality through which accomplishments appear.**

That's the point.

And now the summary turns to specific loci of meditation and worship.

The navel.

The coiled or circular locus.

The heart-space.

And the supreme *Aham*.

This is where we need to be especially careful.

Because the Sanskrit is compact, and it would be easy to invent an entire chakra manual out of a single compound.

We won't.


## 24. नाभि, कुण्डल and हृद्व्योमन् - Worship of Aham in the subtle body

In particular, the ending **-vyomnoḥ** can reflect a dual case-form within the compound.

**Nābhi.**

**Kuṇḍala.**

**Hṛd.**

**Vyoman.**

And the contemplation of *Aham*.

Let's learn them.

### Nābhi - नाभि

Navel.

### Kuṇḍala - कुण्डल

Coil, ring, circular structure.

Related to the *kuṇḍala* family.

### Hṛd - हृद्

Heart.

Related to:

**हृदय - hṛdaya**

Heart.

### Vyoman - व्योमन्

Space, expanse, sky.

Heart-space.

Yogins.

They worship, contemplate or attend upon.

To sit.

### Aham - अहम्

The divine I-consciousness.

Now the verse associates worship of *Aham* with these subtle-body expressions.

The philosophical point is that the practitioner can approach universal consciousness through embodied and contemplative means.

The supreme ground isn't confined to one physical location.

But particular loci can function as means of attention or ritual contemplation.

### The danger of inventing a yoga manual

Imagine a student reading:

"Navel. Coil. Heart-space."

And immediately constructing an elaborate practice involving exact breath ratios, spinal pressure, visualization colours and timed retention.

Where did those instructions come from?

Not from this verse alone.

The textual discipline matters.

A short verse naming contemplative loci does not supply all the procedures necessary to practise safely or traditionally.

### The conclusion of the verse

Abhinavagupta then says the teaching has been determined:

**यथागुर्वागमम् - yathāgurvāgamam**

According to the teacher and the revealed tradition.

And:

**मनाक् - manāk**

A little, slightly, in brief.

Now that's a beautiful transition.

After twenty-four astonishingly dense Sanskrit units, Abhinavagupta says that his explanation is only a little account of what has come down through teacher and scripture.

Yes.

A little.

After hundreds of pages.

The man's definition of brevity is frankly terrifying.

But the next verses become unexpectedly personal.

He stops merely explaining the doctrine.

And begins addressing the reader.

Almost directly.

He says:

I've shown you a portion of the path.

I haven't measured the entirety of Bhairavī.

And you should continue investigating.

It's one of the most intellectually beautiful moments in the entire commentary.



## 25. मार्गांशस्तु प्रदर्शितः - I've shown you only part of the path

**मार्गांशस्तु प्रदर्शितः।**

*Mārgāṃśas tu pradarśitaḥ.*

"Only a portion of the path has been shown."

Now that is a remarkably modest statement.

Especially after the extraordinary scale of the preceding commentary.

The man has explained the entire Sanskrit alphabet.

The thirty-six tattvas.

The structure of divine consciousness.

The metaphysics of space and time.

The relation between pure and Māyic language.

And the liberation of the finite subject.

Magnificent.

Let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Mārga - मार्ग

Path, way, method.

### Aṃśa - अंश

### Enāṃ saṃvidam - एनां संविदम्

### Svavit - स्ववित्

Own.

Knowing.

### The importance of intellectual openness

Now here's the remarkable philosophical point.

Abhinavagupta isn't saying:

The supreme reality isn't exhausted by verbal explanation.

Even a highly sophisticated philosophical presentation remains a method.

A path.

A means.

### The philosopher who refuses to close the case

Imagine somebody finishing the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* and announcing:

"Excellent. All philosophical questions are permanently resolved."

Abhinavagupta replies:

"I've shown you only part of the path."

"But you wrote hundreds of pages."

"Yes."

"Surely that was enough."

"Enough to point toward something, not to measure all of Bhairavī."

Now that's a very important distinction.

**A teaching can be rigorous without claiming that its verbal presentation exhausts the reality it teaches.**

And that's why the next verse is so beautiful.

It asks:

And then attributes even Abhinavagupta's own exposition to a particular manifestation of divine grace.

This is not merely doctrinal humility.

It's a statement about the relation between finite expression and unlimited consciousness.


## 26. इयतीति व्यवच्छिन्द्यात् - Who could set a limit to Bhairavī?

"Who, indeed, could delimit Bhairavī-consciousness by declaring, 'It is only this much'?"

Now this is one of the most beautiful questions in the entire summary.

Because it returns directly to the problem of conceptual delimitation.

And the Sanskrit is extraordinarily precise.

### Iyat - इयत्

So much, of such extent, this much.

The form:

**इयती - iyatī**

Of this much extent, in the relevant feminine context.

Now the connected expression:

**इयतीति - iyatīti**

"It is just this much," thus.

The particle:

**इति - iti**

Marks the quoted thought.

### Vyavacchindyāt - व्यवच्छिन्द्यात्

One might delimit, cut off, determine boundaries.

From:

**वि - vi**

Apart.

**अव - ava**

Down, away.

**√छिद् - chid**

To cut.

The optative:

**व्यवच्छिन्द्यात् - vyavacchindyāt**.

Now this is a wonderful verb for philosophical writing.

### Bhairavīm - भैरवीम्

Bhairavī.

### Saṃvidam - संविदम्

Consciousness.

Feminine accusative singular.

### Kaḥ - कः

Who?

Masculine nominative singular interrogative pronoun.

Now the rhetorical question is clear.

Who can say:

"This is the complete measure of Bhairavī-consciousness, and nothing lies beyond my description"?

### The relation between definition and reality

Now, a good philosophical definition is enormously useful.

We should be precise about terms.

We should distinguish *Prakāśa* from *Vimarśa*.

*Svātantrya* from ordinary causal independence.

*Aham* from finite *ahaṃkāra*.

Parā Vāk from audible Vaikharī.

They don't establish that the supreme reality is simply identical with our particular sentence about it.

That's the distinction.

### The divine measuring tape

"Fine. I'll measure your duration."

"Time is one of my manifestations."

"Right. I'll classify your nature."

"Go ahead."

After thirty verses, the classifier announces:

"I have completed the description."

Bhairavī smiles.

"Have you?"

The classifier looks at the last page.

Abhinavagupta has written:

"Who could delimit her?"

An extremely effective philosophical trap.

### Śaktipāta - शक्तिपात

Power.

Falling, descent.

**शक्तिपात - śaktipāta**

Descent of power, divine grace.

Expanded, unfolded, blossomed forth.

Apart, extensively.

To expand or unfold.

**प्रविजृम्भित - pravijṛmbhita**

Unfolded or expanded.

Now the expression is beautiful.

The power of grace has unfolded within Abhinavagupta's own condition, making the exposition possible.

### The philosophical nuance

He doesn't present himself as an independently ultimate author manufacturing the truth from nothing.

He sees the teaching as an expression of the same divine freedom he's been describing.

Now that is both theological and personally significant.

The author is included within the doctrine.

The act of commentary is itself a manifestation of Śakti.

And that's why the next verse continues with an unusual statement about the possibility of further understanding.

Not only in Abhinavagupta.

But in other knowers.

Today.

Or in another time.

He's about to make a remarkably open-ended statement about philosophical investigation.


## 27. अस्माकमन्यमातॄणाम् - Other knowers may understand more

### The philosopher who welcomes being surpassed

### The fundamental philosophical distinction

Therefore it should not be surprising if further genuine understanding becomes possible.

This isn't a relativistic claim that every idea is equally correct.

It's a distinction between **the ultimate referent** and **the finite scope of any individual account of it**.

And it is precisely why the next verse speaks of *tarka*.

The most subtle reasoning.

## 28. तर्कः सूक्ष्मतमः - Reasoning can become still more subtle

The crucial expression is:

**तर्कः सूक्ष्मतमोऽपि।**

*Tarkaḥ sūkṣmatamo 'pi.*

"Even reasoning of the subtlest kind."

Now this is one of my favourite features of Abhinavagupta's philosophical personality.

He has just admitted that his exposition doesn't exhaust the infinite conscious reality.

He acknowledges that further understanding may arise among other knowers.

And now he explicitly invokes **tarka**.

Reasoning.

Not blind repetition.

Not merely a declaration of scriptural authority.

Actual philosophical investigation.

Let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Tarka - तर्क

Reasoning, critical reflection, examination of possibilities.

Now the term has different technical uses across Indian philosophical traditions.

In Nyāya, *tarka* can designate hypothetical reasoning that supports the determination of truth without itself functioning as an independent *pramāṇa*.

In Śaiva texts, *tarka* can have an important role as an instrument of contemplative and philosophical clarification.

The exact functions shouldn't be flattened into one universal definition.

### Sūkṣma - सूक्ष्म

Subtle, fine, minute.

Now the comparative and superlative formation:

**सूक्ष्मतर - sūkṣmatara**

More subtle.

**सूक्ष्मतम - sūkṣmatama**

Most subtle.

Masculine nominative singular:

**सूक्ष्मतमः - sūkṣmatamaḥ**.

Thus:

**तर्कः सूक्ष्मतमः - tarkaḥ sūkṣmatamaḥ**

The most subtle reasoning.

### Api - अपि

Even.

In sandhi:

**सूक्ष्मतमोऽपि - sūkṣmatamo 'pi**.

The final visarga of *sūkṣmatamaḥ* becomes *o* before the following vowel, with the avagraha marking the elision.

### Abhūtvā - अभूत्वा

Without having previously occurred or come into being.

Here, within the expression concerning further reasoning, the sense is that a new insight can arise where it had not previously been formulated.

### Bhavitā - भविता

Will become, will arise.

The future-oriented form belongs to the √**भू - bhū** family.

Now the argument is striking.

A subtler *tarka* may arise in the future.

Among other knowers.

Or even through further understanding in the present lineage.

### Gabhastimān - गभस्तिमान्

Radiant, furnished with rays.

**गभस्ति - gabhasti**

Ray, beam of light.

The possessive formation:

**गभस्तिमत् - gabhastimat**.

Masculine nominative singular:

**गभस्तिमान् - gabhastimān**.

Now this is a beautiful word.

Reasoning is associated with illumination.

It reveals distinctions.

Exposes contradictions.

Clarifies the philosophical path.

But here's the important distinction.

**Reasoning illuminates; it doesn't manufacture the supreme reality from nothing.**

The conscious ground is already self-revealing.

The reasoning can help eliminate misunderstandings.

### The distinction between philosophical critique and spiritual hostility

Imagine a student discovering a difficult passage.

They notice a variant.

They question a translation.

They identify a possible contradiction.

They investigate the grammar.

Now that isn't necessarily irreverence toward the tradition.

It can be a genuine act of philosophical engagement.

And Abhinavagupta's recognition of the possibility of subtler future reasoning supports precisely that kind of seriousness.

But reasoning can also become a form of ego-identification.

Someone can become more interested in demonstrating superiority than in understanding the subject.

The distinction concerns the aim and manner of inquiry.

That's why the next verse speaks of the *Pūrvaśāstra* and the possibility of liberation through the relevant knowledge.

And then Abhinavagupta gives a remarkable instruction.

**Investigate the teaching, rather than reject it through envy.**

It's a wonderfully practical conclusion.

Especially from a philosopher who has spent hundreds of pages arguing with nearly every philosophical school in India.


## 29. विमृश्यं च परेप्सुभिः - Those seeking the Supreme should examine this

VERSE 29 · P. 216

The central instruction is:

**विमृश्यं च परेप्सुभिः।**

*Vimṛśyaṃ ca parepsubhiḥ.*

"And it should be examined by those who desire the Supreme."

Now that's magnificent.

Because **vimṛśya** comes from the same root-family as *vimarśa*.

The entire philosophy of reflexive awareness returns as an instruction to investigate.

### Vimṛśya - विमृश्य

To be examined, considered, reflected upon.

From:

**वि - vi**

Distinctly, thoroughly.

**√मृश् - mṛś**

To touch, examine or apprehend.

The gerundive:

**विमृश्य - vimṛśya**

To be reflected upon.

The grammatical form here is not simply the absolutive "having examined."

It functions as something that ought to be examined.

### Para - पर

Supreme.

### Īpsu - ईप्सु

Desiring, wishing to obtain.

Related to the desiderative of √**आप् - āp**, to attain.

Now:

**परेप्सु - parepsu**

One who desires the Supreme.

Instrumental plural:

**परेप्सुभिः - parepsubhiḥ**.

Thus the sentence means:

It is to be examined by those who seek the supreme reality.

### Grāhya - ग्राह्य

To be grasped, accepted or understood.

From √**ग्रह् - grah**, to grasp.

The verse pairs the injunction to receive the teaching with the need to reflect upon it.

This is important.

Acceptance and investigation aren't presented as mutually exclusive.

### The Pūrvaśāstra

The preceding half of the verse refers to the **Śrīpūrvaśāstra** as an authority in which the relevant teaching has been established.

It also speaks of liberation and the liberating agent or means.

Now, the precise syntactic relation of **mukta** and **mocaka** is compact.

We shouldn't simply invent a detailed initiation narrative not supplied by this verse.

But the general point is clear.

The teaching is connected to liberation, and its scriptural authority is acknowledged.

### Mukta - मुक्त

Liberated.

From √**मुच् - muc**, to release.

### Mocaka - मोचक

One who liberates, releases or sets free.

A causative agent formation from the same root-family.

Now the relation is beautiful.

**Mukta:** Liberated.

**Mocaka:** Liberator.

The Sanskrit differentiates the recipient and the liberating function.

### The philosophical invitation

Now this is perhaps the most important practical instruction in the entire summary.

Those seeking the supreme reality should **examine** the teaching.

Not simply memorize.

Not merely proclaim affiliation.

Not reduce the whole system to impressive Sanskrit terminology.

Examine.

Investigate.

Reflect.

### The arrogant scholar problem

Imagine a student reading the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*.

They discover a difficult verse.

Instead of analysing it, they announce:

"I already know what it means."

Another student asks:

"How do you know?"

"Because I'm spiritually advanced."

"Have you checked the grammar?"

"No need."

"Compared the readings?"

"Unnecessary."

"Distinguished the editor's bracket from Abhinavagupta's text?"

"Stop being so academic."

Abhinavagupta, who has spent this entire treatise producing astonishingly careful philosophical distinctions, would be a rather unfortunate authority to invoke for that attitude.

His own summary asks the aspirant to examine the teaching.

**Vimarśa is not an excuse to abandon discrimination.**

It is precisely the kind of conscious engagement through which the distinction between appearance and its ground becomes intelligible.

And now we reach the final numbered unit.

A beautiful closing appeal.

Give up envy.

Examine the teaching.

And the little clouds obscuring the Sun of consciousness may dissolve through their own nature.

This is how Abhinavagupta ends the Sanskrit recapitulation.

With a metaphor of illumination.

And an invitation to serious inquiry.


## 30. चिदर्काभ्रलवाः - The little clouds over the Sun of consciousness

FINAL NUMBERED SANSKRIT UNIT · KASHMIR SERIES P. 216

The concluding unit begins with an appeal to the wise to abandon **asūyā**, the envy or resentful fault-finding easily encountered in human life.

Then it turns toward a moment of examination and repose in the Self.

Its final image is:

**चिदर्काभ्रलवाः**

*Cidarkābhralavāḥ.*

"The little fragments of cloud obscuring the Sun of consciousness."

And these are described as becoming pacified or dissolving spontaneously.

Now that is a magnificent conclusion.

Let's learn it carefully.

### Martya - मर्त्य

Mortal.

Related to √**मृ - mṛ**, to die.

### Martyatva - मर्त्यत्व

The condition of mortality.

### Sulabha - सुलभ

Easily obtained, readily encountered.

**सु - su**

Easily, well.

**लभ - labha**

Obtaining, from √**लभ् - labh**, to obtain.

Thus:

**मर्त्यत्वसुलभ - martyatvasulabha**

Readily encountered in mortal existence.

### Asūyā - असूया

Envy, resentment, malicious fault-finding.

Now this is a useful distinction.

*Asūyā* is not identical with rigorous intellectual criticism.

Abhinavagupta has just invited further *tarka*.

He is not forbidding examination.

He's warning against a particular emotional disposition that can obstruct fair inquiry.

### Hitvā - हित्वा

Having abandoned or given up.

From √**हा - hā**, to leave or abandon.

The absolutive:

**हित्वा - hitvā**.

Now the grammatical structure tells us that the reader is to abandon the resentful disposition before engaging the teaching properly.

### Vicakṣaṇa - विचक्षण

Discerning, intelligent, perceptive.

Instrumental plural:

**विचक्षणैः - vicakṣaṇaiḥ**.

By discerning people.

### Ālocana - आलोचन

Examination, attentive consideration.

From *ā-√lok*, to see or examine.

Now remember this root from the earlier discussion of cognition.

It can express an act of apprehension.

### Kṣaṇa - क्षण

Moment, instant.

Now:

**आलोचनक्षण - ālocanakṣaṇa**

A moment of examination.

And:

**आलोचनक्षणादूर्ध्वम् - ālocanakṣaṇād ūrdhvam**

After or beyond the moment of examination.

Notice the ablative ending:

**-आत् - -āt**.

And the following:

**ऊर्ध्वम् - ūrdhvam**

Above, beyond, afterward.

### Ātmani sthitiḥ - आत्मनि स्थितिः

Abiding in the Self.

**आत्मनि - ātmani**

In the Self, locative singular.

**स्थिति - sthiti**

Abiding, establishment.

Feminine nominative singular:

**स्थितिः - sthitiḥ**.

Now here's the philosophical movement.

The discerning person abandons envy.

Examines.

And the examination can lead toward repose in the Self.

### Cidarka - चिदर्क

Now the most beautiful compound.

**चित् - cit**

Consciousness.

**अर्क - arka**

Sun.

Thus:

**चिदर्क - cidarka**

The Sun of consciousness.

Now the sandhi:

*cit + arka → cidarka.*

The final *t* becomes voiced before the following vowel in connected expression.

### Abhra - अभ्र

Cloud.

### Lava - लव

Small piece, fragment, particle.

Now the full compound:

**चिदर्काभ्रलव - cidarkābhralava**

Little fragments of cloud associated with the Sun of consciousness.

Plural:

**चिदर्काभ्रलवाः - cidarkābhralavāḥ**.

Now that is exquisite Sanskrit imagery.

The Sun of awareness is not necessarily destroyed by the clouds.

The obscurations are comparatively insubstantial.

And they can dissolve.

### Saṃśāmyante - संशाम्यन्ते

They become quiet, settle, subside.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Completely.

**√शम् - śam**

To become calm or pacified.

The present middle/passive form:

**संशाम्यन्ते - saṃśāmyante**.

Third-person plural.

### Svataḥ - स्वतः

Of itself, spontaneously, from its own nature.

### Rasāt - रसात्

From relish, essence or intrinsic delight.

Ablative singular of:

**रस - rasa**.

Now the final philosophical image is complete.

The little obscuring clouds subside through the intrinsic relish of conscious self-abiding.

**The Sun of consciousness does not need to be manufactured by removing the clouds.**

The clouds obscure its manifestation to the limited knower.

Their dissolution makes the already-present luminosity evident.

### Why this is such a beautiful ending

Now imagine the entire commentary.

The original question concerned Anuttara.

The unsurpassable reality.

Abhinavagupta has answered through:

Phonemic metaphysics.

Grammar.

Epistemology.

Śakti.

The tattvas.

Mātṛkā.

Language.

Spanda.

Lunar and solar powers.

Mantra.

The divine Aham.

And finally, after this enormous intellectual journey, he offers a remarkably simple image.

**The Sun of consciousness.**

And little clouds.

Not an external darkness powerful enough to annihilate the Sun.

Not a second ultimate reality.

But obscurations that can subside.

That's the culmination of the summary.

### A significant metrical detail

GRETIL labels the concluding material **v30**, but the transmitted unit extends across three metrical half-lines-six eight-syllable pādas-rather than the ordinary four pādas of a standard anuṣṭubh stanza.

We should preserve that fact.

It would be a textual mistake to silently discard the final half-line merely to force the passage into a regular four-pāda verse.

Similarly, the Kashmir Series lays out the closing lines continuously across p. 216.

So:

**Thirty numbered units** in GRETIL.

But not necessarily thirty uniformly four-pāda ślokas in every transmitted detail.

The distinction matters for exact metre analysis. ; \]

And now comes the final textual complication.

A real one.

The Sanskrit summary is followed by an extensive passage in **Apabhraṃśa**.

And the text is so badly transmitted that even specialist scholarship has struggled to recover it.

We cannot omit its existence.

But we cannot honestly translate a sequence of unreadable letter-groups as though it were a securely established poem.

So here's what we actually know.


## 31. The Apabhraṃśa passage on pp. 216-217 - A genuine textual problem

Immediately following the Sanskrit summary, the Kashmir Series prints a long non-Sanskrit passage beginning with letter-groups corresponding to:

*Para-saṃveaṇa-bhāsa…*

The surviving transcription is heavily corrupted.

Its word division is unreliable.

Some groups contain obvious misreadings.

And the manuscript transmission is much less secure than the preceding Sanskrit verses.

Now here's the important discovery.

The great Indologist Harivallabh C. Bhayani devoted a detailed study to precisely these passages.

In his study, **“The Apabhraṃśa Passages from Abhinavagupta's Tantrasāra and Parātriṃśikāvṛtti,”** published in *Indological Studies*, he identifies seven Apabhraṃśa passages within the *Parātrīśikā* commentary.

Their function is similar to the Sanskrit summary verses: they recapitulate doctrinal material through literary language.

But he singles out the long passage on pp. 216-217 as particularly problematic.

Bhayani reports that it remains largely unintelligible in the transmitted text. He tentatively suggests that it may contain about ten stanzas, possibly in paired three-line metrical units, but he does not claim to have recovered a complete, secure text or translation. ; \]

### What can we establish?

The passage is genuine material in the printed commentary tradition.

It occurs after the Sanskrit summary.

It is not an editorial square-bracketed note.

It is not ordinary classical Sanskrit.

And GRETIL's transcription explicitly states that its source omits the Apabhraṃśa passages.

### What remains uncertain?

The correct word divisions.

The complete metrical arrangement.

Some individual readings.

And much of the detailed meaning.

### Why this matters

We could invent ten beautiful Apabhraṃśa verses.

We could write elegant Sanskrit translations.

We could claim that Abhinavagupta has supplied an additional complete philosophical synthesis.

It might sound wonderful.

But it wouldn't be philology.

It would be fiction.

And the user specifically requested no invented text.

So the responsible conclusion is:

**The passage is present and important, but its transmitted state does not support a confident continuous translation.**

### What Bhayani was able to suggest

His research identifies some probable metrical features.

The first recoverable-looking portion may fit a three-line arrangement of approximately:

16 mātrās.

16 mātrās.

10 mātrās.

He also proposes the possibility of five paired sets of such units.

But this remains an editorial hypothesis rather than a complete reconstruction.

And the study emphasizes the larger methodological difficulty: corrupted letters, missing material, incorrect word divisions and faulty line divisions.

Those problems cannot be fixed simply by confidently inserting spaces into the Kashmir Series transcription.

### The linguistic significance

Now here's something genuinely fascinating.

Bhayani argues that Abhinavagupta's Apabhraṃśa belongs to the wider literary Apabhraṃśa tradition, rather than simply reflecting an isolated Kashmiri vernacular.

That matters for the history of language.

Because the same Kashmiri philosopher composing sophisticated Sanskrit Śaiva metaphysics could also employ a literary register shared across a wider Indian cultural world.

The language of philosophical recapitulation wasn't restricted to classical Sanskrit.

Abhinavagupta's literary practice was more varied.

And the passage is valuable evidence of that.

### The appropriate stopping point

We have therefore completed the **securely readable Sanskrit metrical summary** through the final Sun-of-consciousness image.

We have accounted for the intervening Apabhraṃśa material without pretending to reconstruct it.

And the next securely readable prose begins on Kashmir Series p. 217.

That's the natural boundary for Part XXV.

Not a new stanza invented to repair the damaged passage.

Not an arbitrary pause halfway through the argument.

The Sanskrit summary is complete.

The Apabhraṃśa continuation is acknowledged, with its philological limits.

And the commentary turns to its next major subject.

The other important textual distinction concerns **square-bracketed material**.

At several points, the Kashmir Series inserts explanations.

For example:

- The gloss about self-apprehension and the absence of need for an external *pramāṇa*.
- The explanation of dependence and convention.
- The note clarifying that *Aham* includes *idantā*.

## 34. The final philosophical insight - The commentary ends by refusing to end consciousness

The entire summary returns us to one question.

**What is the nature of awareness?**

Not merely what it contains.

Not merely how it becomes associated with objects.

Not merely what words we use to describe it.

But the intrinsic reality through which any object, word, inference or concept becomes manifest.

Abhinavagupta's answer is that supreme consciousness is self-revealing.

Its nature includes its own self-apprehension.

Its freedom is not dependent upon another independent conscious source.

And the very ground of language is that self-expressive awareness.

That's why the Goddess is Parā Vāk.

And why the divine *Aham* expresses more than an ordinary finite pronoun.

The world unfolds through her powers.

The vowels.

The consonants.

The tattvas.

The categories of knowledge.

The objectifying structures of Māyā.

Even the forms of bondage.

All remain dependent upon the supreme conscious reality.

But their manifestation doesn't fragment that reality into independently ultimate pieces.

Now here's the beautiful thing.

Abhinavagupta doesn't end the summary by saying:

"Memorize these thirty verses and never question anything again."

He says, in effect:

**I've shown you part of the path.**

Who could delimit the infinite nature of Bhairavī?

Further understanding may arise.

Subtler reasoning may appear.

Those seeking the supreme reality should examine the teaching.

And the little clouds obscuring the Sun of consciousness can subside.

That is an extraordinarily sophisticated conclusion.

Because it distinguishes three things that are often confused.

**The reality.**

**The teaching about the reality.**

**The recognition toward which the teaching points.**

The reality isn't created by the teaching.

The teaching can be examined.

And the recognition cannot be reduced to merely possessing the correct sentence.

That's why Abhinavagupta can employ intricate Sanskrit analysis while refusing to identify his own account with an exhaustive measure of Bhairavī.

### The ultimate irony of the Sanskrit scholar

Imagine spending years studying:

The vowels.

The consonants.

Sandhi.

Cases.

Tattvas.

Mātṛkā.

Visarga.

Śiva-Śakti.

The Divine I.

Then finally understanding every word of the summary.

And declaring:

"Excellent. I now possess the supreme consciousness."

The philosophical reply is immediate.

**The awareness through which those words were understood was not manufactured at the moment you completed the vocabulary list.**

The teaching may clarify its nature.

It doesn't create the luminous ground from nothing.

And that is why the final metaphor is so beautiful.

The clouds disappear.

The Sun shines.

But the Sun was not produced by the act of removing the clouds.

**Recognition is not the construction of a new Absolute. It is the disclosure of the conscious ground that was never fundamentally absent.**

And that is the heart of Part XXIV.

From the self-shining Goddess.

To ordinary cognition.

To supreme speech.

To the divine Aham.

To the differentiation of pure and Māyic sound.

To liberation while living.

To the scholar's acknowledgement that the path remains open.

All the while:

**परा भगवती नित्यं भासते भैरवी स्वयम्।**

*Parā bhagavatī nityaṃ bhāsate bhairavī svayam.*

The supreme Goddess Bhairavī shines eternally of herself.

And after twenty-four installments, the entire Sanskrit alphabet has finally brought us back to that one statement.
