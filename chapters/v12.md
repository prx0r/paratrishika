# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XII - The First Vowel, the Heart of Time

*Abhinavagupta spends eleven installments explaining why the universe is nothing but the expression of consciousness, finally returns to the original bloody verse, and announces that everything begins with the letter A. Then he explains why even an Absolute without Śakti would be about as spiritually useful as being unconscious.*

Commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series, pp. 164-169


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

We've just emerged from the great simultaneity dispute.

The opponent wanted to know whether speaking, thinking and perceiving could really occur together.

Abhinavagupta replied by interrogating the nature of time itself.

The opponent brought flowers.

Abhinavagupta brought epistemology.

The opponent introduced infinitesimal temporal intervals.

Abhinavagupta demanded to know how the intervals were being apprehended.

The opponent attempted to explain awareness through a succession of cognitions.

Abhinavagupta asked what makes the succession intelligible as one continuous experience.

Eventually, everybody was surrounded by flowers, temporal distinctions, memory, self-awareness and a rather impressive Sanskrit argument.

Then we passed through the Aghorā and Brāhmī powers, divine agents, purification, and the astonishing conclusion that even the purifier must ultimately be recognized as a manifestation of the one consciousness.

And now, finally, the man says:

**Right. Back to the verse.**

Thank fucking Bhairava.

Except here's the wonderful thing.

The argument about time wasn't an irrelevant digression.

It was preparing us to understand the Moon, Sun, vowels and powers of manifestation.

Because if the Moon and Sun are being interpreted through divine *kalanā*, the power of articulation and differentiation, we must first understand why **the supreme consciousness is not subject to the temporal sequence it manifests**.

That's exactly what the previous philosophical argument established.

So now Abhinavagupta can explain the phonemic cosmology without treating time as a pre-existing independent substance.

And he begins with an utterly magnificent sentence.


## 1. एवं स्थिते प्रकृतमनुसरामः - Right, back to the bloody subject

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · KASHMIR SERIES P. 164

**एवं स्थिते प्रकृतमनुसरामः।**

*Evaṃ sthite prakṛtam anusarāmaḥ.*

A close translation:

"With matters thus established, we return to the subject under discussion."

Now learn every word.

### Evaṃ - एवम्

Thus, in this manner.

An indeclinable.

It does not change form according to gender, number or case.

### Sthite - स्थिते

From:

**स्थित - sthita**

Established, stood, situated.

The past participle of √**sthā**, to stand or remain.

Here the locative singular neuter participates in a locative-absolute construction with *evam* supplying the manner:

*evaṃ sthite* - when matters stand thus, with this established.

It's a compact way of closing the previous argument.

And now:

### Prakṛtam - प्रकृतम्

The subject currently under discussion.

From the verbal family of *pra-√kṛ*, to undertake, bring forward or treat.

As an adjective, *prakṛta* can mean begun, introduced or relevant to the present discussion.

The accusative singular neuter **prakṛtam** functions as the object of the implied explanatory continuation.

And:

### Anusarāmaḥ - अनुसरामः

We follow, pursue, continue along.

From:

**अनु - anu**

Along, after, following.

**√सृ - √sṛ**

To go, move, flow.

The verb *anusarati* means to follow or proceed along something.

Here:

**अनुसरामः - anusarāmaḥ**

First-person plural present.

We follow.

Now notice the lovely thing.

He uses *we*.

Abhinavagupta is not announcing that he alone is marching toward truth while the reader struggles behind with an armful of Sanskrit dictionaries.

The grammatical first-person plural draws us into the exposition.

And, after that enormous detour, it amounts to:

"Right, we've resolved the preliminary questions. Back to work."

This is not merely a conversational transition. It marks a real textual boundary: the extended explanation of differing phonemic orders, speech and purification is complete, and the commentary returns to verse five. ; \]


## 2. The fifth verse returns - The Moon and Sun are waiting

The root Tantra's verse is:

## अथाद्यास्तिथयः सर्वे स्वरा बिन्द्ववसानगाः।

## तदन्तः कालयोगेन सोमसूर्यौ प्रकीर्तितौ॥५॥

*Athādyās tithayaḥ sarve svarā bindvavasānagāḥ.*

*Tadantaḥ kālayogena somasūryau prakīrtitau.*

Remember the principal terms.

**अ - a:** the primal phoneme.

**स्वर - svara:** vowel.

**तिथि - tithi:** lunar day or phase-unit.

**बिन्दु - bindu:** point, dot.

**काल - kāla:** time, as well as a term receiving a special interpretation in this passage.

**सोम - soma:** Moon.

**सूर्य - sūrya:** Sun.

Now Abhinavagupta provides the syntactic connection of the verse.

His explanation begins:

**अकाराद्या एव कालयोगेन सोमसूर्यौ...**

*Akārādyā eva kālayogena somasūryau...*

The vowels beginning with *a*, in their relation to the power of *kāla*, are proclaimed as Moon and Sun at the terminus indicated by the verse.

But here's the crucial question.

**What does *tad*, "that," refer to?**

Because the verse says *tadantaḥ*.

At the end of that.

And without identifying the referent, we haven't actually understood the Sanskrit.

### Tad - तत्

That.

A demonstrative pronoun.

We already know:

**इदम् - idam**

This.

And:

**तत् - tat**

That.

Now:

**अन्त - anta**

End, limit, conclusion.

The expression **तदन्तः - tadantaḥ** involves *tad* and *anta*, but its interpretation here depends upon the preceding context.

Abhinavagupta doesn't simply say it means "at the end of the alphabet."

He explicitly identifies the referent.

And this is one of the most important moves in the entire commentary.

**"That" refers to Akula, whose nature is Bhairava.**

Not to an independent earlier event.

Not to a physical object.

Not simply to the conventional written vowel.

The entire phonemic and solar-lunar manifestation is being related to the supreme reality already introduced by the teaching.

And the Sanskrit says so directly.


## 3. तच्छब्देन - What exactly does “that” refer to?

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 164

**तच्छब्देन प्राक्तनश्लोकोक्तमकुलं भैरवात्म परामृश्यते।**

*Tacchabdena prāktanaślokoktam akulaṃ bhairavātma parāmṛśyate.*

A close rendering:

The word *tat* refers back to Akula, of the nature of Bhairava, mentioned in the preceding verse.

This is an astonishingly important sentence.

Not because it contains some wonderfully obscure esoteric metaphor.

But because Abhinavagupta is doing something absolutely ordinary and philologically responsible.

**He's identifying the antecedent of a pronoun.**

Before we start interpreting cosmic vowels, he asks what the actual word refers to.

Excellent.

The man is permitting himself a tiny moment of normal grammatical behaviour.

### Tacchabdena - तच्छब्देन

Start with:

**तत् - tat**

That.

**शब्द - śabda**

Word, sound, expression.

The compound is:

**तच्छब्द - tacchabda**

The word "that."

Why *tacchabda* rather than *tatśabda*?

The final **त् - t** assimilates before the following palatal **श - ś** in this compound.

The written result is:

**च्छ = च् + छ**

So:

*tat + śabda → tacchabda.*

The instrumental singular is:

**तच्छब्देन - tacchabdena**

By the word "that."

Now this is beautiful grammar.

The instrumental identifies the expression by which the referent is indicated.

### Prāktana - प्राक्तन

Earlier, former, previous.

Related to the adverb *prāk*, before or previously.

### Ślokoktam - श्लोकोक्तम्

Mentioned in the verse.

**श्लोक - śloka**

Verse.

**उक्त - ukta**

Spoken, said, mentioned.

From √**vac**, to speak, with a past participial formation.

Now the sandhi:

**śloka + ukta → ślokokta**

Because:

**a + u → o**.

Thus:

**प्राक्तनश्लोकोक्तम् - prāktanaślokoktam**

Mentioned in the preceding verse.

And now:

### Akulam - अकुलम्

Akula.

The transcendent reality of Śiva.

Finally:

### Parāmṛśyate - परामृश्यते

Is referred to, indicated, apprehended.

From:

**परि / परा - pari / parā** in related verbal formations of apprehension.

And √**मृश् - mṛś**, to touch, examine or apprehend.

The technical word *parāmarśa* comes from this same family.

Here the verb is passive:

**परामृश्यते - parāmṛśyate**

It is referred to.

Now we understand the sentence properly.

The prior verse named the supreme Akula reality.

The present verse speaks about the vowels, the Moon and the Sun.

And *tad* connects them.

This means the entire phonemic cosmology is being explained **in relation to Akula**.

That's the philosophical architecture.

And now Abhinavagupta explains why Akula cannot be understood without Kulaśakti.

; \]


## 4. अकुल - Akula: The Absolute is not a cosmic bachelor

Let's revisit the two terms.

**कुल - kula**

Family, collection, totality, the integrated field of manifestation.

**अकुल - akula**

That which is not a differentiated Kula; the transcendent Śiva reality.

Now, ordinary Sanskrit grammar supplies the privative prefix *a-*.

So:

*kula → akula*.

But **Akula does not mean a total absence of reality**.

In the Trika system, it denotes what transcends the differentiated collection without being ultimately separate from the power through which that collection manifests.

Think of the relation between Śiva and Śakti.

Not two independent substances.

Not two rival gods who signed a cosmic cooperation agreement.

Their inseparability is fundamental.

And this is why the next statement is so important.

Abhinavagupta describes Akula as containing *kalanā* within itself.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE

**तेनाकुलमेवान्तर्गृहीतकलनाकम्।**

*Tenākulam evāntargṛhītakalanākam.*

Thus Akula itself contains or includes the power of *kalanā*.

Now there's a magnificent compound.

### Antargṛhīta - अन्तर्गृहीत

**अन्तर् - antar**

Within.

**गृहीत - gṛhīta**

Taken, grasped, contained.

From √**grah**, to grasp or receive.

So:

**अन्तर्गृहीत - antargṛhīta**

Contained within, internally included.

And:

### Kalanā - कलना

Articulation, reckoning, differentiation, productive measurement or the operation of divine power in this context.

The word is associated with the Sanskrit *kal* family, including senses of calculating, forming and arranging.

But don't mechanically identify every occurrence of *kalanā* with ordinary counting.

Here its theological function is more comprehensive.

It is the power through which undivided reality can manifest differentiated structures.

Now the complete compound:

**अन्तर्गृहीतकलनाक - antargṛhītakalanāka**

Containing *kalanā* within itself.

Why?

Abhinavagupta tells us.

Because **Kulaśakti resides within Akula**.

### Kulaśakti - कुलशक्ति

**कुल - kula**

The totality of manifestation.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

Thus:

**कुलशक्ति - kulaśakti**

The power through which the differentiated totality is manifest.

And the Sanskrit uses:

**निवेश - niveśa**

Entry, residing, placement, established presence.

Related to *ni-√viś*, to enter or settle.

Now the philosophical point.

**Akula isn't a supreme reality from which Śakti must be imported.**

It's not that Śiva exists first as an isolated, inert object.

Then Śakti arrives from another universe, carrying the alphabet, lunar phases and a bag full of tattvas.

The power of manifestation belongs intrinsically to the supreme reality.

And that is why Akula can be the ground of Kula.

If Akula were fundamentally devoid of expressive power, it could not account for manifestation.

And if expressive power were an independent second reality, the system would cease to be nondual.

So the doctrine insists on their inseparability.

Now that is already an extremely important result.

But Abhinavagupta goes further.

He identifies *kalanā* with **vimarśaśakti**.

And this is where the whole metaphysics becomes extraordinarily sharp.


## 5. कलनात्मिका हि विमर्शशक्तिः - The power that differentiates is the power that knows itself

Now comes the sentence that unlocks this entire passage.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · PP. 164-165

**कलनात्मिका हि विमर्शशक्तिः।**

*Kalanātmikā hi vimarśaśaktiḥ.*

The power of reflexive awareness is, indeed, of the nature of *kalanā*.

This is an extremely compact statement.

But its implications are enormous.

First, the grammar.

### Kalanātmikā - कलनात्मिका

Start with:

**कलना - kalanā**

Articulation, differentiation, the capacity for measuring or producing determinate manifestation.

Then:

**आत्मिका - ātmikā**

Having the nature of.

The feminine form of *ātmaka*, associated with *ātman*, self or nature.

Now the sandhi:

*kalanā + ātmikā → kalanātmikā.*

The two long *ā* vowels coalesce into one.

So:

**कलनात्मिका - kalanātmikā**

Having the nature of *kalanā*.

Then:

**हि - hi**

Indeed, for, because.

An indeclinable particle.

And:

### Vimarśaśaktiḥ - विमर्शशक्तिः

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness, self-apprehension.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

Together:

**विमर्शशक्ति - vimarśaśakti**

The power of reflexive awareness.

Nominative singular:

**विमर्शशक्तिः - vimarśaśaktiḥ**

Now both *kalanātmikā* and *vimarśaśaktiḥ* are feminine nominative singular.

The grammar agrees.

And the statement identifies the very power through which the Absolute knows itself with the power through which differentiated manifestation becomes possible.

That's extraordinary.

### Why vimarśa isn't merely intellectual reflection

We've encountered *vimarśa* as contemplation, examination and reflexive awareness.

But in Abhinavagupta's metaphysics, supreme *vimarśa* is not merely somebody sitting quietly and thinking:

"Am I conscious? Yes. Excellent."

It's the intrinsic self-apprehension of consciousness.

Its capacity not merely to illuminate but to be aware of its own manifestation.

The familiar distinction:

**प्रकाश - prakāśa**

Luminosity, manifestation.

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness.

Now Abhinavagupta's position is that pure consciousness isn't just an inert luminous substance.

Its luminosity and self-apprehension are inseparable.

And the power of *kalanā* belongs to that same conscious freedom.

### The great distinction

If the supreme consciousness lacked *vimarśa*, how would we distinguish it from something that merely appears but possesses no self-awareness?

And if it lacked all expressive power, how could the differentiated universe be explained without invoking something outside the Absolute?

Abhinavagupta refuses both possibilities.

The supreme reality includes the power of self-awareness.

And that power includes the possibility of manifestation.

Thus:

**The freedom through which consciousness knows itself is also the freedom through which it can appear as the differentiated universe.**

Now, don't turn that into an automatic modern scientific claim that the universe has been experimentally shown to be produced by mind.

It's a metaphysical thesis.

A sophisticated one.

And its implications are what Abhinavagupta is developing.

### The cosmic architect problem

Imagine a philosopher declaring:

"The Absolute is completely undifferentiated, entirely without reflexive awareness, wholly incapable of action and utterly unrelated to manifestation."

Fine.

Then a universe appears.

The philosopher says:

"Excellent. That's the Absolute expressing itself."

Hang on.

You just defined the Absolute as incapable of expression.

Where did the expressive power come from?

"Somewhere."

Where?

"Well, perhaps from another principle."

And now you've introduced an independent second reality.

Congratulations.

Your nondualism has become a complicated dualism wearing nondual clothing.

Abhinavagupta avoids that by insisting on the inseparability of consciousness and Śakti.

**Akula already includes the power of Kula.**

And this is why the next sentence is so devastating.

Without Śakti, even Turyātīta would amount to nothing.


## 6. Turya and Turyātīta - The difference between supreme awareness and a magnificent blank

The Sanskrit says:

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 165

**तामन्तरेणाकुलमपि तुर्यातीतं नाम न किञ्चित्।**

*Tām antareṇākulam api turyātītaṃ nāma na kiñcit.*

Without her, even Akula, supposedly beyond the Fourth, would amount to nothing.

Now that is a shocking sentence.

Let's learn it carefully.

### Tām - ताम्

Her.

Feminine accusative singular of the demonstrative pronoun *tad*.

Here it refers to the previously discussed Śakti.

### Antareṇa - अन्तरेण

Without, apart from.

An indeclinable or postpositional expression used with the accusative.

Thus:

**तामन्तरेण - tām antareṇa**

Without her.

Now the sandhi:

*antareṇa + akulam → antareṇākulam.*

Because:

**a + a → ā**.

### Akulam api - अकुलमपि

Even Akula.

The particle:

**अपि - api**

Even, also, too.

And:

### Turyātītam - तुर्यातीतम्

Beyond the Fourth.

Start with:

**तुर्य - turya**

Fourth.

A technical designation for the fourth state of consciousness.

Then:

**अतीत - atīta**

Gone beyond, transcended, passed.

From *ati-√i*, to go beyond.

Now:

*turya + atīta → turyātīta.*

Because:

**a + a → ā**.

Thus:

**तुर्यातीत - turyātīta**

That which is beyond Turya.

And:

**न किञ्चित् - na kiñcit**

Nothing, not anything.

Now the philosophical meaning.

Abhinavagupta is not rejecting Turyātīta.

He's rejecting the idea of a supposedly transcendent state **devoid of the self-revealing power of consciousness**.

He presses the point by invoking *sauṣupta*, the state of deep sleep.

### The four states

The standard terminology is:

| State | Sanskrit | Ordinary characterization |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Waking | **जाग्रत् - jāgrat** | Experience oriented toward the waking world |
| Dreaming | **स्वप्न - svapna** | Dream experience |
| Deep sleep | **सुषुप्ति - suṣupti** | Deep sleep |
| Fourth | **तुर्य - turya** | The Fourth, transcending ordinary state-distinctions |

Then:

**तुर्यातीत - turyātīta**

Beyond the Fourth.

Now, in the Śaiva interpretation, these aren't merely categories in a modern sleep laboratory.

They participate in a technical contemplative and metaphysical account of consciousness.

And Turyātīta isn't simply a fifth temporal state occurring at 5 a.m. after deep sleep.

Its designation expresses a level beyond the ordinary state hierarchy.

### Sauṣuptapada - सौषुप्तपद

**सौषुप्त - sauṣupta**

Pertaining to deep sleep.

**पद - pada**

State, station or condition.

So:

**सौषुप्तपद - sauṣuptapada**

The condition of deep sleep.

Now Abhinavagupta's objection.

If we identify the supreme reality merely with the absence of differentiated cognition, we risk making it indistinguishable from a condition of insentient blankness.

And that is not the supreme self-revealing consciousness of Trika.

The transcendent must not simply be an empty gap from which *vimarśa* has vanished.

### An important distinction about the editions

The square-bracketed Kashmir Series annotation expands the passage with a verse beginning **शुद्धबोधैकरूपः**, and interprets *turya* in connection with a particular state of pure knowledge. It also adds a Spanda citation about the great space in which Moon and Sun have dissolved.

Those are explanatory materials attached to the edition, not additional unbroken sentences of Abhinavagupta's main prose at this point.

The **main** sentence is more concise and more radical: without *vimarśaśakti*, a supposedly transcendent Akula would lose its character as conscious transcendence. ; \]

### The spiritual blank-screen trap

Imagine a meditator.

They enter a condition with virtually no differentiated thought.

No ordinary narrative.

No sense of time.

No remembered body.

And they conclude:

"Wonderful. This must be the Absolute."

But Abhinavagupta asks:

What is the nature of awareness in that condition?

Is there reflexive luminosity?

Or have you simply identified the suppression of differentiated mental activity with realization?

The distinction is crucial.

**The absence of mental contents is not identical, by definition, with the full self-awareness of Śiva.**

And that is why the power of *vimarśa* cannot be removed from the Absolute.

It is not an optional accessory.

It's intrinsic to the reality under discussion.

Śiva without Śakti is not the supreme Trika reality.

It's a philosophical abstraction that has lost the very power making consciousness conscious.

The cosmic bachelor has been rejected.

The Goddess stays.


## 7. The source isn't an unconscious substance; it is Parā herself

Now Abhinavagupta gives the identity of this *vimarśaśakti*.

**परा परमेश्वरी - parā parameśvarī**

Parā, the Supreme Goddess.

And he says she is of the nature of:

**निरतिशयस्वातन्त्र्य - niratiśayasvātantrya**

Unsurpassable sovereign freedom.

Let's learn that carefully.

### Niratiśaya - निरतिशय

**निर् - nir-**

Without.

**अतिशय - atiśaya**

Excess, superiority, something surpassing.

Thus:

**निरतिशय - niratiśaya**

Unsurpassed, without superior.

It's wonderfully close in meaning to *anuttara*, though the morphology is different.

### Svātantrya - स्वातन्त्र्य

Sovereign independence or freedom.

Now notice the consonant cluster:

**न्त्र्य = न् + त् + र् + य**

It looks alarming.

But once you identify the component consonants, the spelling is intelligible.

The word derives from *svatantra*, independent, through an abstract nominal formation.

And we've already learned:

**स्व - sva**

Own, oneself.

**तन्त्र - tantra**

A system, framework, loom-related formation or governing structure, depending on context.

The ordinary historical derivation and the deeper Tantric interpretation are not interchangeable.

In this context, *svātantrya* means the freedom of supreme consciousness, not simply the legal independence of an individual person.

Now the full description:

**निरतिशयस्वातन्त्र्यात्मिका - niratiśayasvātantryātmikā**

Having unsurpassable freedom as her nature.

That is Parā.

Not simply another goddess somewhere near the top of the metaphysical chart.

The power through which the chart itself is possible.

And now Abhinavagupta describes her through four seemingly contradictory conditions.

Full.

Thin.

Both.

Neither.

He's apparently determined to make the Goddess impossible to fit into any single adjective.

Which is exactly the point.


## 8. पूर्ण, कृश, both and neither - The Goddess refuses every category

Here is the extraordinary compound:

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 165

**पूर्णकृशतदुभयात्मतदुभयरहितत्वेनावतिष्ठते।**

*Pūrṇakṛśatadubhayātmatadubhayarahitatvenāvatiṣṭhate.*

She abides as full, attenuated, both, and free from both.

Right.

We've reached the stage where Abhinavagupta is making the Goddess simultaneously everything and not-everything.

And before anybody announces that this is a contradiction proving the doctrine meaningless, we should examine the Sanskrit and the distinct interpretive standpoints.

### Pūrṇa - पूर्ण

Full, complete.

From the root-family √*pṝ*, to fill.

In Trika, *pūrṇatā*, fullness, is often used to express the complete nature of supreme consciousness.

It is not deficient in anything external.

### Kṛśa - कृश

Thin, lean, emaciated, attenuated.

Now this is a strange adjective to use of supreme Śakti.

Why would the Goddess be thin?

Is the Absolute on a diet?

Has Bhairava refused to share his cosmic lunch?

No.

The term expresses a contrast with fullness.

At the level of differentiated manifestation, particular powers can appear restricted, narrowed or diminished relative to the totality.

The Goddess can manifest in that condition without becoming independently separate from her own supreme reality.

### Tadubhaya - तदुभय

Both of those.

**तत् - tat**

That.

**उभय - ubhaya**

Both.

Thus:

**तदुभय - tadubhaya**

Both previously mentioned conditions.

And:

### Tadubhayarahita - तदुभयरहित

Free from both of those.

**रहित - rahita**

Without, deprived of, lacking.

A participial adjective associated with √*rah*, to leave or abandon.

Now the philosophy.

Parā can be characterized as full because every manifestation is included within her freedom.

She can be characterized through attenuation because that freedom includes the appearance of limited, contracted forms.

She can be described through both because fullness and contraction are not ultimately independent realities.

And she can be described as beyond both because neither category exhausts her own nature.

The four predications aren't four mutually exclusive physical measurements being asserted of one object in precisely the same respect.

They concern distinguishable modes of understanding the supreme power.

### The actual interpretive layers

**पूर्ण**

pūrṇa

Fullness: nothing manifested is independent of consciousness.

**कृश**

kṛśa

Attenuation: consciousness can manifest apparent limitation and differentiated forms.

**उभय**

ubhaya

Both: the two descriptions belong to one expressive power.

**रहित**

rahita

Neither: the supreme nature is not exhausted by the distinction.

The bracketed annotation following this sentence explains the same fourfold characterization through the inclusion of everything within the Self and the absence of any heavy, independent object outside it.

That interpretive explanation is useful, but its detailed wording belongs to the edition's bracketed apparatus. The remarkable fourfold compound itself is Abhinavagupta's.

### The philosophical significance

Now here's why this matters to the Moon and Sun.

The lunar power will be associated with fullness and blissful completion.

The solar power with an outgoing, emptying or attenuating operation.

But the supreme Śakti is not ultimately restricted to one side of that relationship.

She is the ground of both.

And their difference is a manifestation of her own freedom.

You see the structure emerging?

Moon.

Sun.

Fullness.

Attenuation.

Creation.

Withdrawal.

All are going to be interpreted through different operations of one consciousness.

But before we reach the celestial symbolism, Abhinavagupta must settle something.

**How can he describe a sequence of manifestations within a reality that is beyond sequence?**

Because if the Absolute is not subject to time, why is he about to tell us that *a* becomes *ā*, and then *i*, and then *ī*?

Has he accidentally invented a cosmic timeline for something he just declared timeless?

No.

And his explanation is one of the most philosophically important in the whole work.


## 9. Krama and yaugapadya - Why the Absolute cannot be imprisoned by “before” and “together”

We now move to Kashmir Series p. 166.

Abhinavagupta says that the supreme Goddess is not stained by the emergence of succession or simultaneity.

The relevant Sanskrit includes:

**क्रमयौगपद्योदयकलङ्कः**

*Krama-yaugapadya-udaya-kalaṅkaḥ.*

The blemish associated with the arising of sequence and simultaneity.

Now let's dismantle that.

### Krama - क्रम

Sequence, order, succession.

From √**kram**, to step or proceed.

This is a crucial word in Kashmir Śaivism, especially when studying the Krama tradition.

But here its immediate sense is the relation of before and after.

### Yaugapadya - यौगपद्य

Simultaneity, occurring together.

Related to:

**युगपत् - yugapat**

At the same time.

Remember Part XI?

We spent a frankly unreasonable amount of time investigating this one word.

And now it's returned.

The normal grammatical form *yaugapadya* is an abstract noun.

Not merely the adverb *yugapat* repeated.

### Udaya - उदय

Arising, emergence.

From *ud-* and √*i*, to arise or go upward.

### Kalaṅka - कलङ्क

Stain, blemish, defect.

Now put it together.

**Krama-yaugapadya-udaya-kalaṅka**

The supposed blemish arising from succession and simultaneity.

And Abhinavagupta says that such a blemish doesn't apply to Parā in the ultimate sense.

Why?

Because both succession and simultaneity are themselves ways of relating differentiated manifestations.

They're not independent laws standing above universal consciousness.

### The strange dependence of simultaneity upon sequence

This is worth examining carefully.

Suppose two events happen at the same time.

We say they are simultaneous.

But the expression has meaning because events can be assigned temporal positions and related to one another.

If there were no possibility of distinguishing temporal position or succession, what would it mean to say two events were simultaneous?

Now, the logical point is not that simultaneity is physically impossible.

It's that simultaneity is itself a relational temporal determination.

And Abhinavagupta argues that the ultimate conscious ground is not constituted by those determinations.

Thus:

**Parā is not simply a state in which every possible event occurs simultaneously.**

That would still describe her through a relation defined within manifested time.

The deeper claim is that time, sequence and simultaneity are all manifested within her freedom.

This is much more subtle than simply saying "everything happens at once."

### The timelessness isn't a frozen moment

Imagine a photograph.

It captures one instant.

You might call it a frozen moment.

But the photograph isn't timeless in the sense Abhinavagupta means.

It's a physical object with a history.

And the image depicts a particular temporal configuration.

Now imagine somebody treating the supreme consciousness as a giant cosmic photograph containing every object frozen in place.

That's not an adequate Trika account.

Because supreme consciousness is not merely a static arrangement.

It possesses **svātantrya**, the freedom of manifestation.

And *vimarśa*, reflexive awareness.

The absence of dependence upon succession doesn't imply the absence of divine activity.

It's the difference between not being bound by a temporal mechanism and being incapable of manifestation.

And Abhinavagupta insists upon that distinction.


## 10. अनन्तागामिप्रलयोदय - Infinite manifestations and dissolutions within one awareness

Now comes another glorious Sanskrit construction.

**अनन्तागामिप्रलयोदयात्मकस्वस्वभावविमर्शैकघनत्व**

*Anantāgāmipralayodayātmakasvasvabhāvavimarśaikaghanatva.*

Yes.

That is one enormous compound.

The sort of thing that makes a Sanskrit student stare at the page and quietly reconsider every decision that led to this moment.

But we can dismantle it.

### Ananta - अनन्त

Endless, infinite.

*an-* \+ *anta*, end.

### Āgāmin - आगामिन्

Coming, future, approaching.

From *ā-√gam*, to come.

### Pralaya - प्रलय

Dissolution, reabsorption.

From *pra-* and √*lī*, to dissolve or become absorbed.

### Udaya - उदय

Arising, manifestation.

From *ud-* and √*i*.

### Ātmaka - आत्मक

Having the nature of.

### Svasvabhāva - स्वस्वभाव

One's own nature.

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**स्वभाव - svabhāva**

Own nature, inherent character.

### Vimarśa - विमर्श

Reflexive self-awareness.

### Ekaghana - एकघन

One compact mass, homogeneous fullness.

**एक - eka**

One.

**घन - ghana**

Dense, compact, solid, mass.

And finally:

**-त्व - -tva**

The condition of being.

Now the doctrinal meaning.

The supreme consciousness is described as an indivisible fullness of reflexive awareness containing the possibility of innumerable manifestations and dissolutions.

The world appears.

The world is reabsorbed.

Differentiated relations arise.

They cease.

And the supreme reality doesn't become a different substance each time.

Now don't interpret this as a scientifically verified cosmological prediction about cycles of physical universes.

We're studying the internal metaphysics of Trika.

The point is that creative and reabsorptive powers belong to the self-awareness of Bhairava.

They aren't foreign processes imposed upon him.

### The universe's infinite opening and closing

Imagine the entire universe as an enormous theatre.

Scenes appear.

Characters enter.

Actions unfold.

Then the curtain closes.

Another scene begins.

But the stage's possibility isn't created from nothing each time a character walks onto it.

Now the analogy is imperfect because the stage is an inert physical structure while Bhairava is self-revealing consciousness.

But the difference between the **ground of manifestation** and the **particular manifestations** is useful.

And it explains why the succession of cosmic scenes doesn't require the supreme reality to participate in time as another character.

The Goddess is the power through which the scenes can arise and dissolve.

But she is not simply one scene among others.

And this brings us to the passage's next attested verse.


## 11. The spotless mirror of Bhairava

Abhinavagupta invokes the following verse as an authoritative formulation:

VERSE QUOTED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA · P. 166

## स्वतन्त्रः परिपूर्णोऽयं भगवान्भैरवो विभुः।

## तन्नास्ति यन्न विमले भासयेत्स्वात्मदर्पणे॥

*Svatantraḥ paripūrṇo 'yaṃ bhagavān bhairavo vibhuḥ.*

*Tan nāsti yan na vimale bhāsayet svātmadarpaṇe.*

A close rendering:

Bhairava, the Blessed Lord, is independent, complete and all-pervading. Nothing exists that he cannot reveal within the spotless mirror of his own Self.

This is quoted in Abhinavagupta's main prose, not supplied by the surrounding editorial annotation. The passage does not identify an immediate external source for the stanza, so I won't attribute it to a particular Tantra without independent verification. ; \]

Now let's learn it properly.

### Svatantraḥ - स्वतन्त्रः

Independent, sovereign, free.

Masculine nominative singular.

The noun it describes is:

**भगवान् - bhagavān**

The Blessed or venerable Lord.

And:

**भैरवः - bhairavaḥ**

Bhairava.

### Paripūrṇaḥ - परिपूर्णः

Completely full.

Again masculine nominative singular.

Now:

**विभुः - vibhuḥ**

All-pervading, mighty, expansive.

From:

*vi-* \+ the root-family √*bhū*, to be or become, in its established lexical formation.

Notice the ending **-uḥ**.

It is not the same inflectional pattern as masculine *-a* stems ending in *-aḥ*.

The Sanskrit agrees semantically, though the nouns and adjectives follow their respective declensional classes.

### Svātmadarpaṇe - स्वात्मदर्पणे

Now the beautiful expression.

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**आत्मन् - ātman**

Self.

**दर्पण - darpaṇa**

Mirror.

Thus:

**स्वात्मदर्पण - svātmadarpaṇa**

The mirror of one's own Self.

Locative singular:

**स्वात्मदर्पणे - svātmadarpaṇe**

In the mirror of one's own Self.

And:

### Vimale - विमले

Spotless, stainless, immaculate.

Locative singular agreeing with *darpaṇe*.

So the phrase is:

**विमले स्वात्मदर्पणे**

In the spotless mirror of his own Self.

Now this is a tremendously important image.

The world is not being placed into an external container that happens to belong to Śiva.

It is said to manifest within his own self-luminous reality.

And the mirror metaphor expresses the capacity to display many different forms without becoming divided into independently existing substances.

### The crucial difference between a mirror and consciousness

An ordinary mirror doesn't know that it reflects anything.

It merely participates in physical optics.

But Bhairava's metaphorical mirror is **self-revealing consciousness**.

That means the mirror image must be interpreted through both:

*prakāśa* - illumination.

And:

*vimarśa* - reflexive awareness.

Otherwise the analogy would reduce consciousness to an inert reflecting surface.

And that is exactly what Abhinavagupta has just rejected in the discussion of Turyātīta.

So the two arguments are connected.

**Bhairava is not merely a mirror containing images; he is the self-aware reality through which images become manifest.**

Now the verse is genuinely beautiful.

And it's the perfect preparation for the next claim.

To explain the universe through words, we must use sequence.

But the reality being explained isn't sequential in the same way.

The commentator is about to admit that his entire explanation has a limitation.

And it's an unavoidable one.


## 12. How do you explain the nonsequential using sequential speech?

Now comes one of the most intellectually impressive observations in the entire passage.

Abhinavagupta explicitly distinguishes the order of verbal exposition from the nature of the consciousness being explained.

Here is the important statement:

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · PP. 166-167

**सर्व एवायं वाग्रूपः परामर्शः क्रमिक एव।**

*Sarva evāyaṃ vāgrūpaḥ parāmarśaḥ kramika eva.*

All such apprehension taking the form of speech is necessarily sequential.

Then:

**अन्तःसंविन्मयस्त्वक्रम एव।**

*Antaḥsaṃvinmayas tv akrama eva.*

But that which consists of inner consciousness is nonsequential.

This is absolutely magnificent.

Because Abhinavagupta is explicitly identifying a limitation of his own method of explanation. ; \]

### Vāgrūpa - वाग्रूप

**वाक् - vāk**

Speech.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form.

Thus:

**वाग्रूप - vāgrūpa**

Having the form of speech.

The compound undergoes the ordinary consonant adjustment associated with the *vāc/vāk* stem before the following element.

Now:

### Parāmarśa - परामर्श

Apprehension, reflective reference, conscious grasping.

Related to the root-family √*mṛś*, to apprehend or examine.

### Kramika - क्रमिक

Sequential, ordered.

From:

**क्रम - krama**

Sequence.

With an adjectival derivative formation.

Now:

**क्रमिक एव - kramika eva**

Necessarily or indeed sequential.

And:

### Antaḥsaṃvinmaya - अन्तःसंविन्मय

This is the beautiful counterpart.

**अन्तः - antaḥ**

Inner, within.

**संविद् - saṃvid**

Consciousness.

**मय - maya**

Consisting of, constituted by.

Thus:

**अन्तःसंविन्मय - antaḥsaṃvinmaya**

Consisting of inner consciousness.

The compound's consonants adapt in the joined form.

And:

**अक्रम - akrama**

Nonsequential.

The contrast is explicit.

**Speech unfolds through sequence. Inner consciousness is not fundamentally constituted by that sequence.**

### The problem of explaining the Absolute

Imagine I want to explain a complete painting to you.

I must begin somewhere.

Perhaps the top-left corner.

Then the background.

Then the figures.

Then the colours.

Then the perspective.

You listen to my explanation in sequence.

But the painting, considered as one complete composition, isn't produced piece by piece by the order in which I describe it.

The explanation is sequential.

The whole being explained is not identical with the explanation's sequence.

Now apply this to the phonemic cosmology.

Abhinavagupta wants to explain the supreme conscious power through the Sanskrit alphabet.

So he begins with one vowel.

Then another.

Then another.

That is unavoidable if the teaching is presented through articulated language.

But the order of explanation doesn't establish that **the Absolute must literally wait for the first vowel before the second vowel becomes possible**.

The sequence is an instructional presentation of the differentiated power.

The nonsequential conscious ground makes the presentation possible.

And that's why the commentary can use the alphabet without making Śiva subject to an external alphabetical law.

### The philosophical danger of confusing map with territory

Here's a particularly seductive mistake.

You learn:

- a - Anuttara.
- ā - Ānanda.
- i - Icchā.
- ī - Īśana.
- u - Unmeṣa.
- ū - the further unfolding of knowledge.

And then you conclude:

"Ah, consciousness first existed as a, then became ā, then became i, then became ī, and so on."

But you're already treating the sacred phonemic series as a chronological chain of independent events.

Abhinavagupta has just warned you against exactly that.

The verbal exposition necessarily proceeds step by step.

The supreme conscious ground isn't a product of those steps.

**The alphabet unfolds for the student; it need not unfold as an externally imposed temporal necessity for Bhairava.**

This is why the previous debate about time was essential.

And why the whole sequence has been philosophically coherent despite its extraordinary complexity.

Now we finally understand why Abhinavagupta can begin explaining the first vowel without contradicting his account of divine freedom.

The sequence belongs to the expression.

Not to an independently existing power above consciousness.

And at last-

**अ - a.**

The beginning.


## 13. अ - A: Consciousness before the universe has been articulated

We're now at Kashmir Series p. 167.

And Abhinavagupta identifies the first vowel.

# अ

a - Anuttara, primordial self-awareness

Here is his essential statement, separated into two short portions:

**परमेश्वरस्य स्वात्मनि इच्छात्मिका स्वातन्त्र्यशक्तिः।**

*Parameśvarasya svātmani icchātmikā svātantryaśaktiḥ.*

The supreme Lord's power of freedom, of the nature of will, abiding within his own Self.

And:

**अ इत्युच्यते।**

*A ity ucyate.*

It is called *a*.

Now this is the actual metaphysical identification.

The phoneme *a* represents the supreme freedom of consciousness before differentiated objects have unfolded as explicitly distinct manifestations.

Let's dissect the Sanskrit.

### Parameśvarasya - परमेश्वरस्य

Of the Supreme Lord.

Start with:

**परम - parama**

Highest, supreme.

**ईश्वर - īśvara**

Lord.

The compound:

*parama + īśvara → parameśvara.*

Because:

**a + ī → e**.

Now the genitive singular:

**परमेश्वरस्य - parameśvarasya**

Of Parameśvara.

### Svātmani - स्वात्मनि

In one's own Self.

Start with:

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**आत्मन् - ātman**

Self.

Then the locative singular:

**आत्मनि - ātmani**

In the Self.

And:

*sva + ātmani → svātmani.*

Because:

**a + ā → ā**.

### Icchātmikā - इच्छात्मिका

Having the nature of will.

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will, desire, intention.

**आत्मिका - ātmikā**

Having the nature of.

Thus:

**icchā + ātmikā → icchātmikā.**

And:

### Svātantryaśaktiḥ - स्वातन्त्र्यशक्तिः

The power of sovereign freedom.

Now the crucial phrase.

The power has not yet unfolded the expansion of differentiated entities.

Abhinavagupta describes it as:

**अनुन्मीलितभावविकासा - anunmīlitabhāvavikāsā**

Without the development of manifest entities having opened out.

Let's unpack it.

**अन् - an-**

Negative prefix.

**उन्मीलित - unmīlita**

Opened, unfolded.

Related to *ud-* and the root-family √*mīl*, to open or close the eyes.

**भाव - bhāva**

Being, entity, manifested condition.

**विकास - vikāsa**

Expansion, unfolding, development.

Thus:

**anunmīlita-bhāva-vikāsā**

A power whose expansion into differentiated entities has not yet opened.

Now, the important thing.

This isn't the absence of consciousness.

Nor the absence of freedom.

It's the condition in which differentiated objects have not yet become explicit.

### The difference between no object and no awareness

Imagine a thought arising.

Before you formulate the specific thought, the capacity for awareness isn't necessarily absent.

The particular content hasn't yet emerged.

But the conscious reality isn't manufactured by that content.

Now, the analogy is limited because an ordinary preconceptual moment is still a conditioned mental state.

But it helps illuminate the relation.

**The absence of a particular differentiated object does not imply the absence of awareness.**

And Abhinavagupta's *a* represents the supreme self-awareness prior to the explicit expansion of objects.

This is why it symbolizes Anuttara.

The unsurpassable.

The ground.

The nondual reality.

Not because the written character अ has magically generated all the other characters through a historical linguistic mechanism.

But because the Tantric system assigns it the position of primordial conscious freedom.

### The orthographic beauty

Now here's why the script itself offers a wonderful contemplative analogy.

The ordinary Devanāgarī consonant:

**क - ka**

Already includes an inherent *a* unless modified.

And:

**क् - k**

The same consonant with its vowel suppressed by the virāma **्**.

Now compare:

**का - kā**

A long-*ā* vowel sign added to the consonant.

The written forms make the presence and alteration of vowels particularly visible.

But remember the distinction.

This is an orthographic convention.

It's not proof that universal consciousness historically emerges from Devanāgarī.

The philosophical meaning belongs to the Tantric interpretation.

And within that interpretation, *a* represents the supreme conscious freedom underlying every possible phonemic manifestation.

### The final distinction about will

Now Abhinavagupta makes the interpretation more precise.

This *a* represents **icchā** in a state without a predominant object of willing.

The relevant expression includes:

**इष्यमाणानुद्रेका - iṣyamāṇānudrekā**

Without the predominance of what is to be willed.

Let's learn the words.

**इष्यमाण - iṣyamāṇa**

Being desired, being willed.

Related to √*iṣ*, to wish or desire.

And:

**अनुद्रेक - anudreka**

Absence of predominance or heightened emergence.

From *an-* and *udreka*, prominence, excess or rising up.

Thus the will-power is present without a distinctly emerging object of desire.

Now that's a magnificent distinction.

Ordinary desire usually appears as:

"I want that."

A distinct subject.

A distinct object.

A movement toward possession or achievement.

But supreme *icchā* in this context isn't dependent upon an independently existing object.

It's the intrinsic freedom of consciousness.

The capacity to manifest.

Not a craving produced by incompleteness.

**Śiva's will does not arise because he lacks something.**

It's an expression of fullness.

And that's why the next vowel will be **ā**, the power of bliss.

Because the first differentiation doesn't begin with a frustrated god trying to solve a cosmic deficiency.

It begins with the expansion of an already complete conscious freedom.


## 14. Why *a* is also Anuttara-sattā-parāmarśa

Abhinavagupta further characterizes this state as:

**अनुत्तरसत्तापरामर्शात्मिका**

*Anuttarasattāparāmarśātmikā.*

Consisting in the reflexive apprehension of unsurpassable Being.

Now this is a tremendously important compound.

### Anuttara - अनुत्तर

Unsurpassable.

We spent sixteen interpretations on this in Part II.

### Sattā - सत्ता

Being, existence.

Related to √*as*, to be.

Now compare:

**सत् - sat**

Being, existent.

And:

**सत्ता - sattā**

Existence, the condition of being.

### Parāmarśa - परामर्श

Reflexive apprehension, conscious grasping.

### Ātmikā - आत्मिका

Having the nature of.

So the full compound means:

**Having the nature of the reflexive self-apprehension of unsurpassable Being.**

Now think carefully about that.

It's not merely:

"There exists something called the Absolute."

That's an ordinary proposition.

Nor:

"The Absolute has a property called consciousness."

That risks treating consciousness as an addition to something otherwise inert.

The point is that supreme Being is intrinsically self-aware.

Its nature includes reflexive apprehension.

And that self-awareness isn't produced by an external object.

That's why *a* can symbolize the absolute ground.

And why *icchā* can be present even before the differentiated desired object becomes explicit.

The self-aware freedom is already complete.

### The difference between wanting and willing

Ordinary desire:

"I'm hungry. I want food."

A lack is experienced.

A desired object appears.

An action may follow.

But supreme *icchā* isn't being explained as a need resulting from an absence.

It is the sovereign freedom to manifest.

Now here's a deliberately ridiculous analogy.

Imagine Bhairava sitting in an empty universe, becoming anxious.

"There's nothing here."

"I feel bored."

"Perhaps I should create some stars."

"But first I need to invent time so I can schedule the project."

That's precisely the wrong model.

The divine freedom doesn't depend upon an antecedent psychological deficiency.

And the universe isn't being produced because the Absolute has run out of things to do.

The freedom is an expression of completeness.

And *a* represents that primordial self-aware condition.

But now Abhinavagupta must distinguish **Akulaśakti** from the further apprehension of Kulaśakti.

Because the same consciousness can be aware of its transcendent nature and manifest its differentiated powers.

And that gives us the movement toward *ā*.


## 15. Akulaśakti and Kulaśakti - The distinction without a second substance

Now the commentary describes the Supreme Lord continually apprehending his own nature.

Even while the differentiated powers of Kula are manifested, his nature as the possessor of Śakti remains.

And Abhinavagupta says that the apprehension of Akula and the apprehension of Kula can be distinguished.

But the distinction does not establish two separate ultimate realities.

This is a subtle but important step in the argument.

### Śaktimat - शक्तिमत्

Possessing power.

From:

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

And the possessive suffix:

**-मत् - -mat**

Having or possessing.

So:

**शक्तिमत् - śaktimat**

The possessor of power.

Now, the ordinary grammatical distinction between Śakti and Śaktimat is real.

The power.

And the possessor of power.

But their relationship in Trika isn't that of two independent substances.

A fire's capacity to burn is not ordinarily conceived as a second completely separate physical substance stored next to the fire.

Similarly, Śiva's expressive freedom is not a foreign entity added onto a self-sufficient unconscious Absolute.

### The problem of two absolutes

Suppose we make Śiva one ultimate substance.

And Śakti another.

Now the relation between them requires explanation.

How do they interact?

What makes interaction possible?

Is there a third ultimate principle joining them?

And if so, what joins that third principle to the first two?

We are heading toward a rather unpleasant metaphysical regress.

Abhinavagupta avoids this by understanding Śakti as intrinsic to Śiva.

Distinguishable for explanation.

Not independent in ultimate reality.

And this is why the movement from *a* to *ā* doesn't describe two separate gods.

It expresses a development within one conscious power.

### The beautiful transition

The supreme awareness represented by *a* is not lacking bliss.

But its own freedom can expand as **Ānandaśakti**, the power of bliss.

And that expansion is represented by the long vowel.

**आ - ā.**

Now we have finally reached the second phoneme.

After pages and pages of philosophy.

The man has explained two letters in approximately the time another teacher might spend covering the entire alphabet.

And yet the distinction is genuinely beautiful.


## 16. आ - Ā: The unsurpassable expands as bliss

# आ

ā - Ānandaśakti, the power of bliss

Now here is the actual transition in Abhinavagupta's prose, which begins at the bottom of p. 168:

**पुनः प्रसरन्ती आनन्दशक्तिः आ इति।**

*Punaḥ prasarantī ānandaśaktiḥ ā iti.*

The same power, expanding further as the power of bliss, is designated *ā*.

Now that's a remarkably simple statement after all the preceding philosophical machinery.

But don't underestimate it. ; \]

### Punaḥ - पुनः

Again, further, once more.

An indeclinable.

### Prasarantī - प्रसरन्ती

Expanding, spreading forth.

From *pra-* and √**sṛ**, to move or flow.

The present participial feminine form is:

**प्रसरन्ती - prasarantī**

Why feminine?

Because it describes **Śakti**, which is feminine.

### Ānanda - आनन्द

Bliss, delight, joy.

The word is traditionally connected with the verbal family √*nand*, to rejoice or delight, with the prefix *ā-*.

Now:

**आनन्दशक्ति - ānandaśakti**

The power of bliss.

And the nominative singular:

**आनन्दशक्तिः - ānandaśaktiḥ**.

Thus the same conscious power expands as bliss.

### Why ā is long

Now let's connect the metaphysical interpretation with the ordinary phonology.

**अ - a**

The short vowel.

**आ - ā**

The corresponding long vowel.

In Sanskrit phonology, *a* and *ā* are distinct phonemes differing in vowel quantity and, in traditional descriptions, certain articulatory characteristics.

You cannot simply assume that long *ā* is always identical to a short *a* spoken with more enthusiasm.

It has a contrastive role in the language.

For example:

**बल - bala**

Strength.

**बाला - bālā**

A girl, young woman.

The words aren't interchangeable merely because one has an extra vowel sign.

Now look at the writing.

**अ**

Independent short *a*.

**आ**

Independent long *ā*.

And in dependent form:

**क - ka**

**का - kā**

The long vowel sign is:

**ा**

Now the Tantric interpretation takes that phonemic distinction and assigns it a divine significance.

The long vowel expresses expansion of the original conscious freedom into the power of bliss.

But this is **esoteric phonemic exegesis**, not an ordinary historical derivation of the vowel *ā* from the psychological state of bliss.

The sound-symbol relationship belongs to the Tantric doctrine.

### The crucial philosophical distinction

You might think:

"If *a* is the Absolute, why would it need to become bliss?"

But that question assumes the earlier state was deficient.

Abhinavagupta explicitly rejects that.

The first vowel already expresses complete self-aware freedom.

The second expresses that freedom in an expanded mode.

It's not the addition of something previously missing.

It's the manifestation of an aspect already intrinsic to consciousness.

The distinction is between:

**पूर्णता - pūrṇatā**

Fullness.

And:

**प्रसर - prasara**

Expansion.

The fullness remains.

The expansion is its expression.

### The ocean and its movement

Imagine an ocean.

A wave appears.

The wave isn't produced by importing an entirely new substance from outside the ocean.

Its form is a manifestation of the water's movement.

Now, the physical analogy has limitations.

Śiva isn't literally water, and *ā* isn't a fluid dynamic equation.

But it captures the central point.

The differentiated expression doesn't require a second substance independent of the ground.

And the original fullness isn't diminished by manifesting the expression.

So:

**अ - a:** the unsurpassable self-aware ground.

**आ - ā:** the expansion of that ground as bliss.

And now we have the beginning of a sixfold phonemic sequence whose significance is much greater than a normal alphabet lesson.

Because the next sound is **इ - i**, and here the power of will becomes more explicit.


## 17. इ - I: The fullness of will

# इ

i - Icchāśakti, the power of will

Now Abhinavagupta's statement is extraordinarily concise.

**परिपूर्णेच्छा इ इति।**

*Paripūrṇecchā i iti.*

Complete will is designated *i*.

That's it.

One little vowel.

One short statement.

And an enormous metaphysical implication.

### Paripūrṇa - परिपूर्ण

Completely full.

### Icchā - इच्छा

Will, desire, intention.

Now the sandhi:

**परिपूर्ण + इच्छा → परिपूर्णेच्छा**

*paripūrṇa + icchā → paripūrṇecchā.*

Because:

**a + i → e**.

So:

**परिपूर्णेच्छा - paripūrṇecchā**

Fully complete will.

Now here's the important distinction.

At *a*, the power of will was present without a predominant object of willing.

At *i*, that will is now expressed in its fullness as a differentiated divine power.

But this is still not ordinary limited desire.

### The difference between icchā and rāga

Now compare:

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will, desire, intention.

And:

**राग - rāga**

Attachment, attraction, desire in the limiting tattva-system.

These aren't simply identical technical categories.

The supreme *icchāśakti* is a divine power of sovereign freedom.

The limited *rāga* involves the finite subject's experience of incompleteness and attraction toward particular objects.

In ordinary language they can overlap semantically.

But in Trika metaphysics they occupy different explanatory roles.

And this is crucial.

Otherwise you might say:

"Śiva desires the universe, therefore the universe is the product of a cosmic craving."

No.

That is not Abhinavagupta's argument.

The supreme will is an expression of completeness.

The limited desire arises within the manifested structure of contraction.

### The difference between willing and needing

Imagine an artist creating something.

One possible motive is deficiency.

They feel incomplete and hope the work will make them whole.

Another possibility is expressive freedom.

They create because the capacity for expression belongs to their nature and activity.

Now that analogy isn't perfect.

Human artists are finite organisms with complex psychological motivations.

But it illustrates the distinction.

**Supreme *icchā* is not simply a desire to repair an absence.**

It's the freedom through which manifestation becomes possible.

And now we can see why *i* follows *a* and *ā* in the symbolic exposition.

The unsurpassable ground.

Its blissful expansion.

And the fullness of the power of will.

But the next long vowel introduces an important further distinction.

**ई - ī**.

Because will can be considered as becoming oriented toward knowledge.

And that orientation is associated with the power of Īśana.


## 18. ई - Ī: The power of willing to know

# ई

ī - Īśana, sovereign orientation toward knowledge

Now the Sanskrit becomes extremely interesting.

Abhinavagupta describes the will-power as desiring to apprehend through the freedom that takes the form of the forthcoming knowledge-power.

His essential statement includes:

**जिघृक्षन्ती ईशनरूपा ई इति।**

*Jighṛkṣantī īśanarūpā ī iti.*

Desiring to apprehend, taking the form of Īśana, she is designated *ī*.

This is an extraordinary little transition.

And the key word is:

### Jighṛkṣantī - जिघृक्षन्ती

Desiring to grasp, seeking to apprehend.

This is a desiderative present participial formation associated with √**grah**, to grasp or apprehend.

Now compare:

**ग्रहण - grahaṇa**

Grasping, apprehension.

**गृहीत - gṛhīta**

Grasped, taken.

And:

**जिघृक्षा - jighṛkṣā**

Desire to grasp or apprehend.

The form:

**जिघृक्षन्ती - jighṛkṣantī**

Is feminine nominative singular.

She who desires to apprehend.

Now look at the consonant cluster:

**क्ष = क् + ष**

Again.

The desiderative formation has introduced this familiar cluster.

And:

### Īśana - ईशन

Ruling, mastery, sovereign exercise of power.

Related to √**īś**, to rule or possess power.

So:

**ईशनरूपा - īśanarūpā**

Having the form or nature of sovereign mastery.

Now the philosophical development.

At *i*, will is expressed in its complete nature.

At *ī*, the will-power takes on a relation to the emerging power of knowledge.

It becomes, as Abhinavagupta puts it, oriented toward apprehension.

But here's the subtlety.

**There need not yet be a fully articulated external object standing independently outside consciousness.**

The distinction is still within the divine power itself.

The orientation toward knowing belongs to Śakti's unfolding.

And so the long vowel represents a further expression of will through sovereign apprehension.

### I and Ī are not simply “small desire” and “big desire”

This is important.

The difference between *i* and *ī* isn't being interpreted as one desire having half the emotional intensity of another.

The distinction is structural.

*i* expresses complete will.

*ī* expresses will in its relation to the emerging possibility of knowledge and mastery.

It's a different mode of the divine power.

And this is why ordinary Sanskrit phonology and Tantric *nirvacana* must be distinguished.

The short and long vowels have their own phonological reality.

Abhinavagupta interprets their relation through the metaphysics of Śakti.

The correspondence isn't established merely by vowel duration.

It belongs to the broader theological system.

### The extraordinary transition

Now think about the sequence.

Awareness is intrinsically complete.

Its freedom expands as bliss.

Its power of willing becomes explicit.

That will begins to take on the orientation toward apprehension.

And now the next stage is ready.

**Knowledge itself begins to unfold.**

Which brings us to the short vowel:

**उ - u.**


## 19. उ - U: Knowledge opens its eyes

# उ

u - Unmeṣa, the opening of knowledge

Now Abhinavagupta writes:

**उन्मिषन्ती तु ज्ञानशक्तिः ... उ इति।**

*Unmiṣantī tu jñānaśaktiḥ ... u iti.*

The knowledge-power, opening into manifestation, is designated *u*.

The omitted portion specifies the emergence of the totality of objects or entities toward which the preceding will was oriented.

Now this is an extremely important transition.

The power of knowledge becomes explicit.

### Unmiṣantī - उन्मिषन्ती

Opening, beginning to unfold.

From the root-family associated with **unmeṣa**, opening or emergence.

The feminine present participial form:

**उन्मिषन्ती - unmiṣantī**

She who opens.

Now:

### Jñānaśakti - ज्ञानशक्ति

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge, awareness.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

Thus:

**ज्ञानशक्ति - jñānaśakti**

The power of knowing.

Now the philosophical progression makes sense.

The supreme will is no longer considered solely as an undifferentiated freedom.

The power of knowing begins to manifest the field of possible determinate objects.

But again, don't imagine objects already standing outside universal consciousness waiting for Śiva to notice them.

The objects themselves belong to the manifestation.

And the power through which they are apprehended is the same consciousness's expressive freedom.

### Unmeṣa - उन्मेष

We've encountered this in the Spanda teaching.

Opening, emergence, blossoming.

Now notice the relation between *icchā* and *unmeṣa*.

Will is the orientation toward possible manifestation.

Knowledge is the opening through which manifestation becomes apprehensible.

They are distinguishable.

But not metaphysically independent.

And this is precisely why Abhinavagupta can treat them as successive phonemic expressions without turning them into separate substances.

### The ordinary writing lesson

**उ - u**

Independent short vowel.

Dependent sign:

**ु**

So:

**कु - ku**

Now compare:

**ऊ - ū**

And:

**कू - kū**

Again, these are actual Sanskrit vowel distinctions.

And the next phoneme uses the long counterpart to express a further development of the knowledge-power.

But here's the wonderful thing.

The Sanskrit becomes so compressed that Abhinavagupta invokes an image of a divine cow and its udder.

Yes.

After all the metaphysics, we have arrived at a celestial dairy.

An extraordinary moment.

And yet the image belongs to the text's traditional interpretation of the unfolding of the vowel powers.


## 20. ऊ - Ū: The divine cow carries the universe

# ऊ

ū - The expanded, object-bearing power of knowledge

At the transition from p. 168 to p. 169, Abhinavagupta describes the knowledge-power as increasingly explicit, bearing the whole multitude of possible entities within itself.

The Sanskrit contains the striking image of the supreme Goddess as a heavenly, wish-fulfilling cow whose udder bears the generative power.

The associated words include:

**ऊधस् - ūdhas**

Udder.

**ऊढ - ūḍha**

Carried, borne, taken up.

And:

**भावराशि - bhāvarāśi**

The mass or multitude of entities.

Now, we need to be careful with the philology.

The text is using an esoteric phonemic interpretation involving the *ū* sound and the Sanskrit words associated with bearing or nourishment.

It would be a mistake to claim that the vowel *ū* historically derives from the word *ūdhas*.

That's not ordinary Sanskrit etymology.

It's an intentional symbolic association.

### Bhāvarāśi - भावराशि

Start with:

**भाव - bhāva**

Being, entity, manifestation.

**राशि - rāśi**

Heap, mass, collection.

Together:

**भावराशि - bhāvarāśi**

The mass of entities.

Now:

**सकल - sakala**

Complete, entire, with all parts.

And:

**ऊढ - ūḍha**

Carried, borne.

So:

**ऊढसकलभावराशिः - ūḍhasakalabhāvarāśiḥ**

Bearing the complete multitude of manifested entities.

That's an enormous word.

But the grammar is perfectly intelligible once divided.

And the philosophical meaning is clear.

The power of knowledge is not merely an empty capacity.

It now bears the possible differentiated forms within its own manifestation.

### The knowledge-power isn't made from external objects

Imagine a painter.

They possess the capacity to paint.

Then they form an intention.

Then the possible image becomes increasingly determinate.

But within Abhinavagupta's metaphysics, the supreme consciousness isn't a painter facing an independently existing canvas and a collection of foreign materials.

The entire power of manifestation belongs to consciousness.

So the emerging differentiated forms are included within the knowledge-power itself.

Now the divine cow image expresses this condition of nourishing or bearing.

The power contains the possible manifestation.

It can support its development.

And eventually the forms become more explicit.

But the supreme conscious ground is not thereby destroyed.

### The sixfold progression

We've now reached a very useful natural grouping.

The first six vowel-powers in Abhinavagupta's exposition. The last includes a complex symbolic account of contraction and the expanded power of knowledge; its meaning cannot be reduced to the ordinary adjective "deficient."

Now here's the important sequence.

**अ - a**

Unsurpassable being, self-aware freedom.

**आ - ā**

The expansion of bliss.

**इ - i**

Complete will.

**ई - ī**

Will oriented toward knowledge and sovereign apprehension.

**उ - u**

The opening of knowledge.

**ऊ - ū**

The further manifestation of knowledge bearing the differentiated multitude.

But these aren't six independent gods.

Nor are they six chronological stages that the supreme consciousness must undergo in order to become complete.

They are distinguishable expressions of the one divine freedom.

And now Abhinavagupta draws the next major conclusion.

The sixfold phonemic progression has revealed two principal powers.

**Will and knowledge.**

And those two are going to become the Moon and Sun.

Which is what the original verse promised all along.


## 21. The two great powers - Why the Moon is full and the Sun is lean

Now we reach Kashmir Series p. 169.

And at last Abhinavagupta brings us back to the two celestial powers from verse five.

He has established the initial vowel-sequence and identifies two principal Śaktis of Bhairava:

**इच्छाशक्ति - icchāśakti**

The power of will.

And:

**ज्ञानशक्ति - jñānaśakti**

The power of knowledge.

He then interprets these through the Moon and Sun.

Now this is beautiful.

Because we can finally understand why he introduced the seemingly bizarre fourfold description of the Goddess:

Full.

Lean.

Both.

Neither.

That description was preparing us for these two divine operations.

### The lunar power - Soma

Abhinavagupta describes the first Śakti as:

**पूर्णा - pūrṇā**

Full.

And:

**सोमरूपा - somarūpā**

Having the nature of Soma, the Moon.

The relevant explanation associates her with:

**स्वानन्दविश्रान्ति - svānandaviśrānti**

Repose in her own bliss.

Let's learn the compound.

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**आनन्द - ānanda**

Bliss.

**विश्रान्ति - viśrānti**

Rest, repose, settling.

So:

**स्वानन्दविश्रान्ति - svānandaviśrānti**

Repose in one's own bliss.

Now why the Moon?

Within this symbolism, the lunar power expresses fullness, completion and blissful repose.

It is the power of will understood as a complete divine manifestation.

Not ordinary desire seeking to repair a lack.

### The delightful Soma wordplay

Abhinavagupta also offers an esoteric interpretation of *Soma* through the expression:

**सहोमया वर्तते इति सोमरूपा।**

*Sahomayā vartate iti somarūpā.*

The sense is that the divine reality is together with Umā.

Now be very careful.

**This is symbolic Tantric wordplay, not the ordinary historical etymology of the Sanskrit word *soma*.**

The phonemic resemblance is being used to express the inseparability of Śiva and Umā.

The familiar religious language of the Lord being together with his Goddess becomes an explanation of the lunar power.

The theological meaning is entirely appropriate to the passage.

Śiva is not independently complete while Śakti is an optional addition.

The divine fullness includes her.

And the Moon symbolizes that fullness in the present arrangement.

### The Moon as Mahāsṛṣṭi

Abhinavagupta then associates this power with:

**महासृष्टि - mahāsṛṣṭi**

Great creation or emission.

Now this is important because we might initially expect the Moon simply to represent passive repose.

But here the power is not inert.

Its fullness is generative.

It is a divine mode of creative manifestation.

The Sanskrit:

**सृष्टि - sṛṣṭi**

Creation, emission.

From √*sṛj*, to send forth, release or emit.

And:

**महा - mahā**

Great.

Thus:

**महासृष्टि - mahāsṛṣṭi**

Great emission or creation.

The associated scriptural reference points forward to the root Tantra's later instruction concerning the worship of *sṛṣṭi*.

We haven't reached that verse yet.

So we're not going to jump ahead and construct a ritual exposition from a later passage.

For now, the important identification is:

**Lunar Śakti - Will - Fullness - Bliss - Great creation.**

And now the Sun arrives.

Not simply as another illuminating object in the physical sky.

But as the second principal power.

Knowledge.

And its activity is astonishingly different.


## 22. The solar power - Sūrya, the great reabsorber

Abhinavagupta calls the second Śakti:

**सूर्यरूपा - sūryarūpā**

Having the nature of the Sun.

And associates her with:

**ज्ञानाख्या - jñānākhyā**

Known as knowledge.

Now the really interesting thing.

He also calls this power:

**कृशा - kṛśā**

Lean, attenuated.

Why?

Because its operation is described through emptying, emitting or releasing the multitude of manifested entities.

### Recana - रेचन

Emptying, expulsion, release.

Related to √*ric*, to empty or leave, in its causative nominal family.

And:

**प्रसारण - prasāraṇa**

Spreading forth, extending, expanding.

Related to *pra-√sṛ*, to move or flow.

Now consider what the commentary is doing.

The lunar power was associated with fullness and repose.

The solar power is associated with an operation of releasing or extending the manifested field.

And through the resulting relation, it is described as lean or attenuated.

But don't imagine Śiva has literally lost mass.

This is an account of divine activity.

The fullness and attenuation belong to different modes of manifesting and reabsorbing the universe.

### Mahāsaṃhāra - महासंहार

Now here's the surprising designation.

**महासंहार - mahāsaṃhāra**

Great reabsorption or withdrawal.

Break it down.

**महा - mahā**

Great.

**संहार - saṃhāra**

Gathering in, withdrawal, reabsorption.

Related to *saṃ-√hṛ*, to gather or take away.

And the commentary associates the solar knowledge-power with this great reabsorptive activity.

Now that's philosophically interesting.

Because the Sun is often associated with outward illumination.

Yet here its activity also participates in the return or reabsorption of manifestation.

The apparently opposing movements of expansion and withdrawal are related within the same power of knowledge.

### The twofold symbolism

**सोम - Soma**

Fullness, bliss, repose, will and great creation

**सूर्य - Sūrya**

Knowledge, illumination, release, extension and great reabsorption

Modern astronomical imagery illustrates the celestial referents, not the historical Tantric iconography or a scientific basis for these correspondences.

Now the philosophical distinction.

The Moon and Sun aren't independent ultimate principles.

They're manifestations of Śakti's operation.

And the relation between them expresses the complementary movements through which conscious freedom manifests and reabsorbs its own differentiated forms.

That's why the original verse identified the two through *kālayoga*, their relation to the power of articulation.

The solar-lunar distinction belongs to the manifestation of divine power.

But it doesn't establish a second reality outside Bhairava.

### The Sun isn't “bad” because it is lean

Now don't overinterpret the imagery.

The Moon being full doesn't make the Moon morally superior to the Sun.

And the Sun being lean doesn't make it spiritually deficient.

The terms designate different operations.

Likewise, creation and reabsorption aren't simply good versus evil.

Both are expressions of divine freedom.

The manifested universe can expand.

It can withdraw.

The power of awareness remains.

And the distinction between the powers doesn't remove their underlying unity.

**The same Śakti can be understood as creative fullness and reabsorptive illumination.**

This is the deeper meaning of the solar-lunar pair in the present passage. ; \]


## 23. Why even the Moon-Sun mapping can be reversed

Now comes a final qualification that is extremely important for understanding the earlier alphabet disputes.

Abhinavagupta says that, depending on the standpoint and the manner in which the powers are considered, their relationship can also be reversed.

Yes.

We've spent pages establishing Moon as will and Sun as knowledge.

And now the man announces that their roles may be considered from another direction.

For fuck's sake.

We're back to the cosmic departmental reassignment.

But this time we know exactly why it happens.

Remember the great argument about Mālinī and Mātṛkā?

Different phonemic sequences can express different modes of one divine reality.

Similarly, the solar and lunar powers can be interpreted according to their relation to creation, reabsorption, outward manifestation and inward repose.

The assignment depends upon the mode being considered.

Now the crucial distinction.

**Abhinavagupta isn't saying the Moon and Sun are physically interchangeable astronomical objects.**

Nor is he saying that the literal meaning of *soma* suddenly becomes *sūrya*.

The reversal concerns the symbolic relations among divine powers.

### Viparyaya - विपर्यय

Reversal, inversion, contrary arrangement.

From *vi-* and *pari-√i*, in a conventional lexical formation.

Now the phrase in the commentary concerns:

**सोमसूर्यात्मकसृष्टिसंहृतिकलना**

*Somasūryātmakasṛṣṭisaṃhṛtikalanā.*

The lunar-solar articulation of creation and reabsorption.

Now look at the structure.

**सोम - soma**

Moon.

**सूर्य - sūrya**

Sun.

**सृष्टि - sṛṣṭi**

Creation.

**संहृति - saṃhṛti**

Withdrawal, reabsorption.

**कलना - kalanā**

Articulation, differentiation.

The phrase relates the celestial powers to the two great movements.

Now, why can the relation reverse?

Because the very distinction between expansion and reabsorption is not independent of the conscious ground.

The same power may appear through different relations according to the aspect emphasized.

That does **not** make all distinctions meaningless.

The correspondence still has an internal structure.

But the ultimate reality isn't restricted to one compulsory conceptual arrangement.

And we have already established the necessary metaphysical foundation.

The supreme consciousness is **akrama**, nonsequential.

Its manifestation can appear through multiple ordered relations.

The Moon and Sun symbolism belongs to those manifested relations.

Thus the possibility of different interpretive standpoints does not destroy the nondual ground.

Now this is genuinely elegant.

It also explains why Abhinavagupta was so insistent earlier that ordinary linguistic and ritual distinctions must be investigated rather than simply rejected.

Their differences have meanings.

But those meanings aren't independently ultimate.


## 24. The next objection - Doesn't this produce an infinite regress?

And now, at the transition to Kashmir Series p. 170, the opponent sees another opportunity.

"If will can expand and contract, and knowledge can expand and contract, then perhaps the expansion of will requires another will."

"And the expansion of that will requires another will."

"And the expansion of that..."

Oh, wonderful.

We've started another infinite regress.

The Sanskrit problem concerns:

**प्रसर - prasara**

Expansion.

And:

**अप्रसर - aprasara**

Non-expansion.

The opponent suggests that the different modes of will and knowledge might require endlessly further distinctions of expansion and non-expansion.

Now Abhinavagupta's response is sharp.

He effectively tells the reader to stop wandering through conceptual constructions of the external manifestation and to enter the subtle path of *vimarśa*.

The next line begins:

**उपसंहरत बाह्यविभ्रमभ्रमभ्रमणं तावत्।**

*Upasaṃharata bāhyavibhramabhramabhramaṇaṃ tāvat.*

Withdraw, for now, from the roaming within the bewilderment of outward appearances.

And then the instruction to enter the subtle path of reflexive awareness.

This is a wonderful next boundary because the commentary is about to move from the sixfold vowel and solar-lunar classification into the direct structure of cognition, using an ordinary object such as a pot.

That argument belongs to Part XIII. ; \]

### Upasaṃharata - उपसंहरत

Withdraw, gather in, bring to a close.

Second-person plural imperative.

From:

**उप - upa**

Near, toward.

**सम् - sam**

Together.

**√हृ - √hṛ**

To take, carry, gather.

Thus:

**उपसंहरत - upasaṃharata**

Withdraw or gather in!

This is a striking contrast with:

**प्रसर - prasara**

Expansion.

The discussion has moved outward through a series of increasingly differentiated divine powers.

Now the teacher tells us to gather that attention inward again.

### Bāhya - बाह्य

External, outward.

### Vibhrama - विभ्रम

Confusion, wandering, bewilderment.

Related to *vi-* and √*bhram*, to wander or move around.

### Bhrama - भ्रम

Wandering, error, turning.

Again from √*bhram*.

And:

### Bhramaṇa - भ्रमण

Wandering, roaming, turning.

The alliterative cluster is almost comical.

*Bāhya-vibhrama-bhrama-bhramaṇa.*

It's as though Abhinavagupta has deliberately constructed a little Sanskrit tongue-twister describing the mind's tendency to get lost in its own distinctions.

Now the philosophical point.

The conceptual proliferation can continue indefinitely.

Will.

The expansion of will.

The will to expand.

The knowledge of the will to expand.

The awareness of the knowledge of the will to expand.

At some point you have to investigate the underlying awareness in which those distinctions are being constructed.

Not because reason is useless.

Abhinavagupta has used a tremendous amount of reason.

But because the ultimate reality cannot be established by an infinite chain of independent conceptual objects.

**The path of *vimarśa* concerns the reflexive consciousness underlying the entire proliferation.**

And now we have reached a perfect place to stop.

Because Part XIII will begin precisely with the command to enter that subtle path and then follow the argument through the cognition of a pot, the reality of knowing, and the unfolding of the further vowel-powers.


## 25. The complete sixfold progression - What the first vowels actually teach

Let's gather what we've learned.

| Vowel | Sanskrit name | Principal significance here |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **अ - a** | **अनुत्तर - anuttara** | Unsurpassable self-aware freedom |
| **आ - ā** | **आनन्द - ānanda** | Expansion as bliss |
| **इ - i** | **इच्छा - icchā** | Complete will |
| **ई - ī** | **ईशन - īśana** | Sovereign will oriented toward knowledge |
| **उ - u** | **उन्मेष - unmeṣa** | Opening of knowledge |
| **ऊ - ū** | **ऊनता - ūnatā**, with further symbolism | Knowledge manifesting differentiated contents |

Now the most important distinction.

These are not simply six independent cosmic objects.

Nor six literal stages through which the Absolute must evolve in external time.

They are **six phonemic expressions of the powers of one consciousness**, presented sequentially for the purposes of teaching.

And that's why the whole passage begins with the relation between Akula and Kulaśakti.

The supreme reality doesn't lack expressive power.

Its self-awareness includes the freedom to manifest.

And the six vowels give us an articulated representation of that freedom.

### The relation to Icchāśakti

Notice something particularly beautiful.

*Icchā*, will, appears explicitly at **i**, but Abhinavagupta has already described the first vowel *a* through an **icchātmikā svātantryaśakti**, a freedom whose nature is will.

So don't construct a crude distinction:

"*a* has no will. *i* suddenly creates will."

That's not the doctrine.

Rather, the power of will is intrinsic to supreme consciousness and becomes **more explicitly differentiated** in the vowel sequence.

At *a*, it has no predominant object.

At *ā*, its own blissful fullness expands.

At *i*, the will-power is expressed in complete form.

At *ī*, it becomes oriented toward knowledge.

And at *u* and *ū*, the knowledge-power unfolds into the possibilities of differentiated manifestation.

That's an exceptionally sophisticated account of the relationship between **intrinsic freedom and articulated activity**.

It avoids the idea that divine freedom is created from nothing at a later stage.

The powers were never ultimately foreign to consciousness.

They are becoming expressible through the sacred phonemic sequence.

### The relation to creation and reabsorption

And now we have the solar-lunar interpretation.

**Soma** expresses the fullness of the will-power, blissful repose and the great creative movement.

**Sūrya** expresses the knowledge-power, the extension of manifestation and the great reabsorptive movement.

But the two are not ultimately separate.

Their relations can be interpreted from different standpoints because the entire manifestation belongs to one universal freedom.

This is why Abhinavagupta can sustain both the distinctions and their ultimate nonduality.

## 27. The final insight - The first vowel is not a thing before all other things

Now we can finally understand why Abhinavagupta places so much importance on **अ - a**.

The first vowel is not merely the first item in a chronological chain.

If it were, the question would immediately arise:

What existed before it?

What caused it?

What mechanism produced it?

And what temporal order made that production possible?

But the entire point of Anuttara is that it is not another object subject to an antecedent independent cause.

The phoneme *a* symbolizes the unsurpassable ground.

And the subsequent vowel-powers express the manifestation of that ground through bliss, will and knowledge.

Now here's the philosophical conclusion.

**The Absolute does not become complete by manifesting the universe. It manifests the universe through the freedom that belongs to its completeness.**

That's why *a* already possesses the nature of *icchā*.

And why *ā* is not a repair of something deficient.

And why the solar and lunar powers can express different operations without creating independently existing substances.

The differentiated universe is not outside Śakti.

Śakti is not outside Śiva.

And Śiva is not an inert being waiting for Śakti to arrive.

The ground, the power and the manifestation are distinguishable for the purposes of explanation.

But they are not ultimately independent.

This is the philosophical purpose of the sacred alphabet.

Not to replace ordinary Sanskrit grammar.

Not to prove cosmology by phonetic resemblance.

But to disclose, through a structured symbolic system, the relation between undivided consciousness and its expressive freedom.

And now we've reached the remarkable climax.

After all those arguments about time, cognition and divine power, we can finally understand the first two vowels:

**अ - a**

The unsurpassable conscious freedom.

**आ - ā**

The blissful expansion of that freedom.

The rest of the alphabet will unfold as further expressions.

And the whole process will remain grounded in the same self-revealing reality.

Which means that the very consciousness through which you're reading and understanding these Sanskrit words is not, within the Trika metaphysics, something fundamentally outside the reality the text is describing.

That's the invitation to recognition.

Not merely to acquire another complicated piece of information.

But to investigate the nature of the awareness through which all information becomes manifest.

And that, after twelve installments, is exactly where Abhinavagupta wanted us.

At the beginning.

The letter **अ**.

The Heart.

The unsurpassable.
