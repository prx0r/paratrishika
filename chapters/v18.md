# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XVIII - Who Decided That “Cow” Means Cow?

*Abhinavagupta has spent seventeen installments turning the Sanskrit alphabet into the living body of Śakti. Now he discovers a rather inconvenient problem: somebody had to decide what the words mean. He interrogates the meaning of “cow,” summons Utpaladeva, catches linguistic convention in a philosophical infinite regress, and finally brings the entire bloody dictionary back to Anuttara, Bindu and Visarga.*

Commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series, pp. 193-197 · Ending immediately before the new exposition of **अहम् - aham** on p. 198


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

We've established something rather extraordinary.

The vowel-powers of Śiva are not merely vowels.

The consonantal powers of Śakti are not merely consonants.

Even indistinct sounds, animal cries, drum signals, musical notes and the subtler resonances of *Nāda* participate, according to Abhinavagupta, in the universal expressive power of **Mātṛkā**.

And then he made the alphabet larger.

Fifty letters.

Sixty-four letters.

Eight groups of eight.

Special forms of Visarga.

Different articulatory efforts.

Different locations in the mouth.

Different ritual classifications.

There was a real danger of the whole commentary becoming an infinite Sanskrit spreadsheet.

And at precisely this moment, Abhinavagupta announces:

**We're not going to get stuck in the enumeration.**

Why?

Because the point is **पूर्णता - pūrṇatā**.

Fullness.

The supreme conscious reality is not manufactured by adding up all its manifestations.

And the number of ways to classify those manifestations does not change its fundamental nature.

But here's the wonderful thing.

His refusal to become absorbed in the alphabetic enumeration doesn't lead him away from language.

It leads him deeper into language than we've ever gone before.

Because the real problem isn't merely how we produce a sound.

It's how we understand it.

How does a sound become a word?

How does a word acquire a meaning?

How do we learn that the Sanskrit sound **गो - go** means cow?

And how is that learning possible unless consciousness already possesses a capacity for apprehension that wasn't created by the convention itself?

This is the great philosophical problem of Part XVIII.

We'll follow the primary Sanskrit through pp. 193-197. Three important textual variations affect the interpretation: whether conceptual cognition arises through *prasāda* or *pramāda*, how repetition transforms the conventional word *go*, and whether Śrīkaṇṭha's verse explicitly says *asāṅketikī* or *sāṅketikī*. I'll keep those disagreements visible, rather than pretending the surviving transcriptions constitute one perfectly uniform text. ; ; \]

And now, finally, Abhinavagupta makes an unusually sensible decision.

He refuses to count any more letters.


## 1. इह तु तत्प्रक्रियानभिनिवेशः - Enough with the fucking alphabet

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · KASHMIR SERIES P. 193

**इह तु तत्प्रक्रियानभिनिवेशः - पूर्णतैकसारत्वात्।**

*Iha tu tatprakriyānabhiniveśaḥ - pūrṇataikasāratvāt.*

"Here, however, there is no insistence upon that particular procedure, because fullness alone is the essence."

What a beautiful sentence.

Seventeen installments of phonemic cosmology, and now the man essentially says:

"Look, don't become obsessed with the arrangement."

Not because the sacred alphabet is unimportant.

He has just demonstrated its significance at extraordinary length.

But because the alphabetic arrangement is an expression of the supreme reality.

**It isn't something standing above that reality.**

Let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Iha - इह

Here.

An indeclinable.

In philosophical prose it often means *in the present discussion*, rather than merely at this physical location.

### Tu - तु

But, however.

A contrastive particle.

This is important because Abhinavagupta is marking a genuine change of emphasis.

Previously: the detailed classification of Mātṛkā.

Now: the nondual fullness underlying the classifications.

### Tatprakriyā - तत्प्रक्रिया

**तत् - tat**

That.

**प्रक्रिया - prakriyā**

Procedure, method, systematic exposition.

The word *prakriyā* belongs to the family of *pra-√kṛ*, to undertake, perform or organize.

And:

**तत्प्रक्रिया - tatprakriyā**

That particular procedure.

Here referring back to the detailed phonemic classifications.

### Anabhiniveśaḥ - अनभिनिवेशः

Now here's the magnificent word.

**अन् - an-**

Negative prefix.

**अभिनिवेश - abhiniveśa**

Firm adherence, fixation, insistence, absorption.

The verbal family includes *abhi-ni-√viś*, entering into or becoming fixed upon.

Thus:

**अनभिनिवेश - anabhiniveśa**

Noninsistence, absence of fixation.

Masculine nominative singular:

**अनभिनिवेशः - anabhiniveśaḥ**.

Now join the compound:

**तत्प्रक्रियानभिनिवेशः**

*tatprakriyānabhiniveśaḥ.*

The lack of fixation on that procedure.

Notice the long **आ** at the junction.

*prakriyā + anabhiniveśa → prakriyānabhiniveśa.*

The vowels coalesce:

**ā + a → ā**.

### Pūrṇatā - पूर्णता

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa**

Full, complete.

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract noun suffix.

Thus:

**पूर्णता - pūrṇatā**

Fullness, completeness.

### Ekasāra - एकसार

**एक - eka**

One, sole.

**सार - sāra**

Essence, core, essential substance.

Thus:

**एकसार - ekasāra**

Having only one essential reality.

### Pūrṇataikasāratvāt - पूर्णतैकसारत्वात्

Now put it all together.

*pūrṇatā + eka + sāra + tva*.

Fullness.

Sole essence.

The condition of that being the defining principle.

And the ablative ending:

**-आत् - -āt**.

Thus:

**पूर्णतैकसारत्वात् - pūrṇataikasāratvāt**

Because fullness alone is the essential principle.

Now that's not just an ornamental phrase.

It explains **why he refuses to insist upon the exact enumerative procedure**.

If the supreme reality were constituted by one particular arrangement of sixty-four letters, a different arrangement would threaten its very existence.

But if the arrangement is a manifestation of fullness, the existence of alternative classifications doesn't destroy the ground.

### The metaphysical spreadsheet problem

Imagine a scholar announcing:

"I have discovered the correct sixty-four divine letters."

Excellent.

Another scholar says:

"My tradition recognizes fifty."

A third says:

"Actually, certain ritual systems distinguish still more."

They begin fighting.

Very respectfully, of course.

With increasingly aggressive Sanskrit compounds.

Eventually one scholar declares:

"If your alphabet has fourteen fewer letters, your Supreme Consciousness is incomplete."

And Abhinavagupta replies:

**You have mistaken the classification for what is being classified.**

The categories may matter.

But counting more manifestations doesn't manufacture more supreme consciousness.

The point is fullness.

And that is where his argument about meaning begins.


## 2. संविदनुप्रवेशक्रम - Every encounter with an object is a possible entrance into consciousness

Abhinavagupta immediately generalizes the preceding teaching.

He introduces:

**संविदनुप्रवेशक्रमः**

*Saṃvidanupraveśakramaḥ.*

A method or progression of entering consciousness.

And then describes an object in two very different conditions:

**सङ्कल्प्यमानः - saṅkalpyamānaḥ**

Being conceived or mentally constructed.

**साक्षात् क्रियमाणः - sākṣāt kriyamāṇaḥ**

Being directly performed or brought into immediate actuality.

Now that is an important distinction.

The teaching applies not only when one is sitting quietly thinking about consciousness.

It applies to the apprehension of things in thought and action.

The entire field of manifestation can become the starting point of this investigation.

### Saṃvid - संविद्

Consciousness, awareness.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Together, fully.

**√विद् - √vid**

To know.

The noun:

**संविद् - saṃvid**

Consciousness.

Now the compound with *anupraveśa*:

### Anupraveśa - अनुप्रवेश

**अनु - anu**

Following, into, along.

**प्र - pra**

Forth.

**√विश् - √viś**

To enter.

Thus:

**अनुप्रवेश - anupraveśa**

Entry, penetration.

And:

### Krama - क्रम

Sequence, order, method.

So:

**संविदनुप्रवेशक्रम - saṃvidanupraveśakrama**

A method of entering into consciousness.

Now, don't mistake *krama* here for a claim that the ultimate consciousness itself is produced by temporal stages.

We've already distinguished:

**क्रम - krama**

Order within manifestation or instruction.

And:

**अक्रम - akrama**

Nonsequentiality of the supreme reality.

A method may proceed in stages even when the reality it discloses isn't made from those stages.

### Saṅkalpyamānaḥ - सङ्कल्प्यमानः

This is a very useful Sanskrit formation.

Start with:

**सङ्कल्प - saṅkalpa**

Intention, conception, mental formulation.

Related to *sam-√kḷp*, to arrange or conceive.

Then:

**सङ्कल्प्यते - saṅkalpyate**

Is conceived, formulated or imagined.

And the present passive participle:

**सङ्कल्प्यमान - saṅkalpyamāna**

Being conceived.

Masculine nominative singular:

**सङ्कल्प्यमानः - saṅkalpyamānaḥ**.

It agrees with:

**पदार्थः - padārthaḥ**.

The thing or object under consideration.

### Padārtha - पदार्थ

**पद - pada**

Word, expression, term; also position or step in other contexts.

**अर्थ - artha**

Meaning, object, referent, purpose.

Thus:

**पदार्थ - padārtha**

The referent or meaning of a word; more broadly, an object or category.

Now the same term is important in logic, semantics and ontology.

The word's precise meaning depends upon context.

Here it encompasses the object being conceived or directly engaged.

### Sākṣāt - साक्षात्

Directly, immediately, before one's eyes.

### Kriyamāṇaḥ - क्रियमाणः

Being done, performed, made.

From √**कृ - kṛ**, to do.

The present passive participial formation:

**क्रियमाण - kriyamāṇa**.

Masculine nominative singular:

**क्रियमाणः - kriyamāṇaḥ**.

Now the contrast is clear.

An object being imagined.

Or an object being actively dealt with.

And Abhinavagupta says that either can be traced into the same conscious ground.

### The beautiful practical point

Imagine a pot.

You can conceive a pot.

You can remember a pot.

You can make a pot.

You can see a pot.

You can describe a pot.

These are different activities.

Yet in every case there is an occurrence of apprehension or manifestation.

The object and its mode of presentation may differ.

But Abhinavagupta asks us to recognize the conscious reality through which they become manifest.

**The opportunity for recognition isn't confined to one special mental state.**

Now this matters enormously.

Because the next statement identifies that common ground with a form of the Great Mantra.

And here's where the distinction between conventional and nonconventional becomes crucial.


## 3. The great radiance of Parā Vāk - Before words become words

Abhinavagupta describes the object as finding its establishment within:

**शुद्धविमर्शात्मपरवाङ्मन्त्रमहामहस्**

*Śuddhavimarśātmaparavāṅmantramahāmahas.*

The great radiance of the mantra of supreme speech, whose nature is pure reflexive awareness.

This is the technical heart of the passage.

Let's dismantle it.

### Śuddha - शुद्ध

Pure, undefiled.

In Trika, purity in this context doesn't simply mean physical cleanliness.

It concerns awareness not being fundamentally reduced to the limiting differentiation under discussion.

### Vimarśa - विमर्श

Reflexive awareness, self-apprehension.

We've encountered this word throughout the entire audiobook.

It is not merely intellectual examination.

In the metaphysics of supreme consciousness, *vimarśa* is the intrinsic capacity of awareness to apprehend itself.

### Ātman - आत्मन्

Self, nature.

The compound:

**विमर्शात्मन् - vimarśātman**

Having reflexive awareness as its nature.

### Parāvāk - परावाक्

**परा - parā**

Supreme.

**वाक् - vāk**

Speech.

Thus:

**परावाक् - parāvāk**

Supreme speech.

Now remember the relationship between Parā and Vaikharī.

Vaikharī is overtly articulated speech.

Parā is the supreme, nondual power of speech.

This isn't a distinction between audible and inaudible sound **alone**.

It is a distinction between levels of manifestation and their conscious ground.

### Mantra - मन्त्र

Here it isn't merely a conventional collection of syllables.

It refers to the supreme expressive power of consciousness.

### Mahāmahas - महामहस्

**महा - mahā**

Great.

**महस् - mahas**

Brilliance, radiance, splendour.

Thus:

**महामहस् - mahāmahas**

Great splendour.

Now the Sanskrit places the object within that radiance.

And that is philosophically important.

A conceived or directly engaged object does not become intelligible solely because we attach a conventional name to it.

The conscious power of manifestation is already required.

### The decisive adjectives

The underlying state is described as:

**अमायीय - amāyīya**

Not of the limiting character of Māyā.

And:

**असाङ्केतिक - asāṅketika**

Nonconventional, not dependent upon an agreed linguistic designation.

Here there is a small textual issue. The connected Sanskrit at the end of p. 193 has a difficult word boundary following **वा - vā**. The parallel transcription supports segmentation as *vā amāyīya-asāṅketika...*, rather than reading it as an assertion that the supreme ground is itself merely Māyic and conventional.

That interpretation also agrees with the explicit discussion of the *asāṅketika* mantra beginning immediately afterward. ; \]

### What “nonconventional” actually means

Suppose English speakers agree that the sound "cow" refers to a particular animal.

That's a linguistic convention.

Other languages use different words.

Sanskrit: **गो - go**.

Hindi: **गाय - gāy**.

French: *vache*.

The conventions differ.

But the possibility of apprehending sounds, recognizing things, forming distinctions and learning meanings cannot itself be explained by appealing endlessly to previously learned word-conventions.

There must already be some capacity for awareness.

And that is what the present metaphysical discussion is targeting.

**The supreme mantra isn't simply another word whose meaning must be learned from an earlier dictionary.**

It is the conscious ground within which word-meaning relations become possible.

Now this is not a denial that actual human languages depend upon learned conventions.

Abhinavagupta is going to make the conventional character of ordinary words explicitly central to the argument.

But he insists that convention cannot constitute the ultimate reality of consciousness.

And now he introduces the term that connects this to the wider Indian philosophical debate.

**Avikalpa.**

The nonconceptual condition.


## 4. अविकल्पा दशा - The condition before conceptual distinctions

Abhinavagupta identifies the supreme ground with what different philosophical traditions describe as:

**अविकल्पा दशा**

*Avikalpā daśā.*

The nonconceptual condition.

His prose says:

**यत्र सर्ववादिभिरविकल्पा दशा गीयते।**

*Yatra sarvavādibhir avikalpā daśā gīyate.*

Where a nonconceptual condition is proclaimed by the proponents of the various philosophical systems.

Now we need to be precise.

Because *avikalpa* is very easily misunderstood.

### Vikalpa - विकल्प

A distinction, conceptual construction, alternative or determinate cognition.

The word is associated with the *vi-√kḷp* family, involving differentiation, arrangement and conceptual formation.

Now:

**वि - vi**

Apart, distinctly.

**कल्प - kalpa**

Arrangement or conception in the relevant lexical family.

Thus:

**विकल्प - vikalpa**

Conceptual differentiation.

And:

### Avikalpa - अविकल्प

Nonconceptual, free from conceptual differentiation.

The negative prefix:

**अ - a-**.

Thus:

**अविकल्प - avikalpa**.

The feminine nominative singular:

**अविकल्पा - avikalpā**.

Because it qualifies:

**दशा - daśā**

Condition, state.

So:

**अविकल्पा दशा - avikalpā daśā**.

Now the philosophical issue.

Does nonconceptual mean unconscious?

No.

That would be the very misunderstanding Abhinavagupta has spent hundreds of pages attacking.

For him, the supreme reality isn't a blank absence of cognition.

It is intrinsically self-revealing.

It possesses *prakāśa*, luminosity, and *vimarśa*, reflexive awareness.

So the absence of ordinary conceptual differentiation does not imply an absence of consciousness.

### The difference between silence and unconsciousness

Imagine sitting silently.

No deliberate verbal thought.

No internally rehearsed sentence.

Perhaps no clearly formulated object of attention.

You might call that nonconceptual.

But simply stopping words isn't enough to establish the supreme state described by Abhinavagupta.

Someone could also be drowsy.

Distracted.

Unconscious.

Or temporarily unable to formulate a sentence.

Those conditions are not automatically identical with realization.

The question is the nature of awareness.

**Avikalpa in this passage concerns the nondual conscious ground, not merely the suppression of mental activity.**

Now here's the crucial distinction.

Abhinavagupta isn't making a crude opposition in which:

Conceptual = false.

Nonconceptual = true.

Because he's about to say that conceptual cognition itself arises through the same supreme reality.

That's why the argument matters.

The differentiated cognition doesn't establish an independent second consciousness.

It is an expression of the one ground.

And this is where things become interesting.

Because the supreme radiance also has an explicitly phonemic nature.

### The mantra beyond ordinary naming

The text describes the supreme mantra-radiance as taking the form of genuine:

**बीज - bīja**

Seed-powers.

And:

**पिण्ड - piṇḍa**

Condensed forms or compact phonemic bodies.

It also distinguishes their pure and mixed manifestations.

Now recall Part XIV.

Vowels are interpreted as seed-powers.

Consonants as condensed generative matrices.

And the entire alphabet expresses the manifestation of consciousness.

So the nonconceptual ground isn't being described as a complete absence of expressive potency.

The powers are present within it.

Their differentiations can manifest.

But they need not be understood as independently existing ultimate substances.

This is why Abhinavagupta can discuss the phonemic content of the supreme mantra while refusing to reduce it to conventional spoken syllables.

And now comes the objection that demonstrates the difference.

If everything has one conscious ground, aren't all things simply the same?

Abhinavagupta replies by producing one of his most delightfully absurd lists.

A mountain.

A jujube tree.

Water.

Fire.

Existence.

Nonexistence.

A pot.

Happiness.

And nonconceptual knowledge.

Apparently the entire universe has been summoned as witnesses.


## 5. Meru, a jujube, water, fire, a pot and happiness - The problem with making everything identical

Abhinavagupta's list includes:

**मेरु - meru**

Mount Meru.

**बदर - badara**

Jujube, a type of fruit or tree.

**जल - jala**

Water.

**ज्वलन - jvalana**

Fire, burning.

**भाव - bhāva**

Existence, being.

**अभाव - abhāva**

Nonexistence or absence.

**घट - ghaṭa**

Pot.

**सुख - sukha**

Happiness.

And:

**निर्विकल्पज्ञान - nirvikalpajñāna**

Nonconceptual cognition.

He says that without the differentiated expressive powers, all these would become indistinguishable in the relevant sense.

Now why is this important?

Because nondualism is often caricatured as the claim that all differences are simply false.

But Abhinavagupta has been arguing for the reality of **manifest differentiation within one conscious freedom**.

He does not need to deny the ordinary difference between water and fire.

Nor between a pot and happiness.

Nor between an existing object and the absence of that object.

### Meru - मेरु

The sacred cosmological mountain.

Here it functions as one definite object among others.

### Badara - बदर

Jujube.

Notice the contrast.

A cosmic mountain.

And a little fruit.

Abhinavagupta really knows how to construct an ontological shopping list.

### Jala - जल

Water.

### Jvalana - ज्वलन

Fire, flame, burning.

Related to √**ज्वल् - jval**, to burn or shine.

Now compare:

**जल - jala**

Water.

**ज्वलन - jvalana**

Fire.

They resemble one another a little in sound.

But their meanings are not interchangeable.

This is already a useful illustration of the fact that phonetic resemblance alone doesn't establish semantic identity.

### Bhāva and Abhāva

**भाव - bhāva**

Being, existence or a manifested condition.

**अभाव - abhāva**

Nonbeing, absence.

The negative prefix changes the conceptual content.

And that distinction matters in Indian logic and ontology.

### Nirvikalpajñāna - निर्विकल्पज्ञान

**निर् - nir-**

Without.

**विकल्प - vikalpa**

Conceptual distinction.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

Thus:

**निर्विकल्पज्ञान - nirvikalpajñāna**

Nonconceptual knowing.

Now here's the fascinating thing.

Even nonconceptual cognition can become an **object of conceptual discussion**.

We can identify it.

Name it.

Distinguish it from other modes of cognition.

The philosophical account can therefore be conceptual, even when the reality being discussed is nonconceptual.

That's not necessarily a contradiction.

It means the explanation and the explained reality operate at different levels.

### The philosophical argument

Suppose someone says:

"All these things ultimately depend upon consciousness."

Fine.

Now someone replies:

"Therefore a pot is the same ordinary object as happiness."

No.

That doesn't follow.

It confuses unity of ultimate ground with identity of manifested form.

Abhinavagupta's position requires both claims:

**The ground is one.**

**Its manifestations are genuinely distinguishable as manifestations.**

And the power through which those distinctions appear is *svātantrya*.

Now we can finally understand why the nonconventional mantra is not an undifferentiated nothingness.

It is the source of differentiated manifestation.

Its power is not exhausted by any one of its expressions.

And that's why conceptuality doesn't destroy it.

Which brings us to the next sentence.

And a particularly important manuscript variation.


## 6. Does conceptuality arise through grace or through error?

Now Abhinavagupta discusses *vikalpa* itself.

The Kashmir Series reads:

**विकल्पोऽपि तत्प्रमादोत्थः।**

*Vikalpo 'pi tatpramādotthaḥ.*

Even conceptual cognition arises through a lapse or error associated with that reality.

But the parallel GRETIL transcription reads:

**विकल्पोऽपि तत्प्रसादोत्थः।**

*Vikalpo 'pi tatprasādotthaḥ.*

Even conceptual cognition arises through its favour or grace.

These are not identical readings.

And we shouldn't pretend they are. ; \]

Let's learn the distinction.

### Pramāda - प्रमाद

Carelessness, negligence, error, lapse of attention.

From the *pra-√mad* family.

**प्रमाद - pramāda**

A lapse or negligence.

### Prasāda - प्रसाद

Clarity, serenity, favour, grace.

Related to *pra-√sad*, to settle or become clear, in its established lexical history.

**प्रसाद - prasāda**

Clarity or grace.

Now compare the written forms.

**प्रमाद - pramāda**

**प्रसाद - prasāda**

Only one consonant changes.

**म - ma**

Versus:

**स - sa**.

But the philosophical meaning changes dramatically.

In one reading, differentiated conceptuality arises through a lapse or misapprehension.

In the other, it arises through the favour or expressive manifestation of the supreme power.

### Uttha - उत्थ

Arisen, originating.

From *ud-√sthā*, to rise or stand forth.

Thus:

**तत्प्रमादोत्थ - tatpramādottha**

Arising from its lapse or error.

Versus:

**तत्प्रसादोत्थ - tatprasādottha**

Arising through its grace.

Both are morphologically intelligible.

The immediate text doesn't give enough evidence to establish which is the original reading with certainty.

But the next sentence is secure in both witnesses.

**न तु प्रत्युत तत्स्वरूपं भिन्द्यात्।**

*Na tu pratyuta tatsvarūpaṃ bhindyāt.*

But it should not, conversely, divide that reality's own nature.

Now this is the essential philosophical point.

Conceptual differentiation does not create an independent conscious substance.

Nor does the arising of *vikalpa* divide the supreme reality into fundamentally separate parts.

### Bhindyāt - भिन्द्यात्

Might divide, would split.

From √**भिद् - bhid**, to split or divide.

The optative:

**भिन्द्यात् - bhindyāt**.

Now the conjunct:

**न्द्य = न् + द् + य**.

And:

**स्वरूप - svarūpa**

Own form or intrinsic nature.

So the argument is:

Whatever the precise relation between conceptuality and its supreme source, *vikalpa* cannot establish a metaphysically independent division within the self-revealing ground.

### The philosophical difference between the readings

If we follow *prasāda*, conceptual cognition can be understood positively as an expression of Śakti's freedom.

If we follow *pramāda*, the passage places greater emphasis on the lapse through which differentiation is apprehended as separate.

Those emphases are not identical.

And both have resonances elsewhere in nondual Śaiva discussions of manifestation and misrecognition.

But I won't silently replace the Kashmir Series reading with the GRETIL reading because one sounds more attractive.

The philological question remains open.

The argument we can securely retain is narrower:

**Conceptual cognition belongs to the unfolding of consciousness and cannot independently fracture its ultimate nature.**

Now this is the point at which the commentary turns from conceptuality to conventional language.

And here Abhinavagupta makes a genuinely radical claim.

The nonconventional mantra is precisely what makes conventional signification possible.

Excellent.

The dictionary has apparently become dependent upon something that isn't in the dictionary.


## 7. The nonconventional mantra makes conventional language possible

Here's the next phrase:

**यदेव तदसाङ्केतिकं मन्त्रवपुः।**

*Yad eva tad asāṅketikaṃ mantravapuḥ.*

That very mantra-form which is nonconventional.

Now the sentence continues to explain that omniscient teachers, seeing its diverse manifestations, teach it as both an object of worship and a means involving linguistic convention.

This is the crucial transition.

The supreme mantra itself isn't constituted by an agreement that one word means one object.

Yet it is capable of manifesting the differentiated relations through which conventional language becomes meaningful.

### Saṅketa - सङ्केत

Convention, agreed designation, sign or signal.

From the *sam-√kit* family of indicating or making known.

The word is particularly important in Indian philosophical theories of language.

A *saṅketa* can be an agreement or established association connecting an expression with a meaning.

Now:

**साङ्केतिक - sāṅketika**

Conventional, dependent on an agreed designation.

And:

**असाङ्केतिक - asāṅketika**

Nonconventional.

Notice the initial **अ - a**.

It changes the sense.

But here's a subtle point.

Nonconventional does **not** necessarily mean meaningless.

It means not dependent upon a particular agreement assigning a sign to a referent.

That's the distinction.

### Mantravapus - मन्त्रवपुस्

**मन्त्र - mantra**

Mantra.

**वपुस् - vapus**

Body, form, essential nature.

Thus:

**मन्त्रवपुस् - mantravapus**

Having the form or body of mantra.

The nominative-accusative neuter singular remains *mantravapus*.

Now:

**असाङ्केतिकं मन्त्रवपुः - asāṅketikaṃ mantravapuḥ**

The nonconventional mantra-body.

### Anyonyavicitra - अन्योन्यविचित्र

**अन्योन्य - anyonya**

One another, mutually.

**विचित्र - vicitra**

Variegated, diverse, remarkable.

Thus:

**अन्योन्यविचित्र - anyonyavicitra**

Mutually differentiated.

The supreme conscious power can manifest a multitude of mutually distinguishable forms.

But those differences don't establish separate ultimate substances.

### Saṅketopāya - सङ्केतोपाय

**सङ्केत - saṅketa**

Convention.

**उपाय - upāya**

Means, method.

Thus:

**सङ्केतोपाय - saṅketopāya**

A means involving convention or conventional designation.

And:

### Upāsyatā - उपास्यता

The condition of being an object of worship or contemplation.

From:

**उपास्य - upāsya**

To be worshipped or contemplated.

And:

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract condition.

Now the apparent paradox becomes clear.

**The nonconventional mantra can be taught through conventional means.**

That doesn't turn its ultimate nature into a mere convention.

It means conventional speech can be used to direct attention toward what is prior to and underlying convention.

### The philosophical equivalent of using a map

Imagine a map of a mountain.

The map is made of symbols.

Those symbols have conventional meanings.

Contours.

Labels.

Paths.

Distances.

You learn the conventions.

Then you use the map to reach the mountain.

But the mountain isn't produced by the map's symbols.

And the map's usefulness doesn't require the mountain itself to be made of printed ink.

Now the analogy is limited, because Abhinavagupta's supreme consciousness isn't an external physical mountain.

But it captures the distinction between **a conventionally communicated teaching** and **the reality the teaching is intended to disclose**.

You can teach someone the word *vimarśa*.

They can learn its Sanskrit morphology.

Its philosophical definitions.

Its historical usage.

But the actual self-revealing nature of consciousness is not created by memorizing the word.

That's why Abhinavagupta distinguishes nonconventional mantra from conventional word-meaning associations.

And this is also why he can defend the usefulness of Sanskrit study without reducing realization to Sanskrit proficiency.

You needn't believe that the dictionary causes the Absolute to exist.

### A significant editorial interruption

The Kashmir Series places a long square-bracketed passage at the transition from p. 193 to p. 194.

The main prose is interrupted between the syllables of **उपदिश्यते - upadiśyate**, "is taught."

The bracketed verses begin with Śiva addressing the Goddess and proceed through the alphabet from **अ - a** to **क्ष - kṣa**.

They describe Mātṛkā, the three Bindus, the fiftyfold phonemic arrangement and the transcendent state beyond enumerated sound.

This material is thematically appropriate.

But it is **not a continuous passage of Abhinavagupta's own prose at that point**.

The editor has inserted a scriptural illustration into the middle of the sentence.

We therefore resume the main argument after the closing bracket, not as though Abhinavagupta had suddenly stopped to recite an entire separate alphabetic hymn.

That's especially important here because the argument is about to leave enumeration behind. ; \]

And now comes the crucial distinction.

**Conventional words don't acquire their ultimate capacity to signify from a convention that exists entirely outside consciousness.**

Let's look at the Sanskrit.


## 8. मायीयाः सङ्केताः - The conventions of the differentiated world

Abhinavagupta says:

**मायीयाः सङ्केताः पतन्ति।**

*Māyīyāḥ saṅketāḥ patanti.*

The conventions belonging to the domain of Māyā enter or fall into that nonconventional radiance of speech.

Now this is compact, but extraordinarily important.

### Māyīya - मायीय

Pertaining to Māyā.

From:

**माया - māyā**

Māyā, the principle associated with differentiation and limitation in the Śaiva system.

The adjective:

**मायीय - māyīya**

Belonging to Māyā.

Masculine nominative plural:

**मायीयाः - māyīyāḥ**.

Agreeing with:

**सङ्केताः - saṅketāḥ**.

Now both are masculine nominative plural.

The grammar is straightforward.

The conventions are characterized as Māyic.

But here's the crucial philosophical point.

That doesn't mean conventions are completely nonexistent.

Nor that they have no function.

Their function belongs to the differentiated world.

### Patanti - पतन्ति

They fall, enter, come to rest.

From √**पत् - pat**, to fall or move downward.

Third-person plural present:

**पतन्ति - patanti**.

The conventions enter the supreme radiance of speech.

And the text explains that they come to participate in the non-Māyic mantra-reality.

### Why does a convention need a ground?

Imagine two people agreeing that a particular sound means cow.

They make the agreement.

Then others learn it.

A linguistic community develops.

The convention becomes established.

All perfectly intelligible.

But now ask:

**What allowed the people to understand the agreement in the first place?**

They had to apprehend the sound.

Recognize one another.

Distinguish the cow from other things.

Remember the association.

Understand that the sound was being used as a sign.

And identify the intended referent.

So the possibility of making a convention presupposes cognitive capacities.

That is the point.

Abhinavagupta isn't denying that word-meaning relations are conventionally established at the ordinary linguistic level.

He's denying that convention is the ultimate source of awareness itself.

### The infinite dictionary problem

Imagine somebody constructing a language from nothing.

They announce:

"This sound means cow."

The student asks:

"What does 'means' mean?"

The teacher replies:

"Look it up."

"Where?"

"In the dictionary."

"What's a dictionary?"

"Look that up too."

Now the teacher is in trouble.

Because every attempted explanation relies upon some prior understanding.

That doesn't mean language learning is literally impossible.

Children do learn languages.

But the very possibility of learning and establishing conventions presupposes the capacity to apprehend distinctions.

**The convention can't be the sole explanation of the conscious capacity that makes conventions intelligible.**

This is the regress that Abhinavagupta is about to expose explicitly.

And he does it by asking what it means for a word to be a *vācaka*.

A signifier.

Now we're moving into the technical theory of language.


## 9. वाचकता - What makes a sound signify anything?

The main Sanskrit says:

**स्वरूपप्रतिपत्तिरेव हि तेषां वाचकताभावः।**

*Svarūpapratipattir eva hi teṣāṃ vācakatābhāvaḥ.*

The establishment or recognition of their own nature is what constitutes their signifying character.

That's a compact paraphrase of a difficult philosophical claim.

Let's understand the vocabulary.

### Vācaka - वाचक

Expressing, indicating, signifying.

From √**वच् - vac**, to speak.

The adjective:

**वाचक - vācaka**

Signifying.

And the noun:

**वाचकः - vācakaḥ**

A signifier.

### Vācya - वाच्य

That which is expressed or signified.

Also from √**वच् - vac**.

The gerundive:

**वाच्य - vācya**

To be spoken, expressed or designated.

Now compare:

**वाचक - vācaka**

The signifier.

**वाच्य - vācya**

The signified.

These are fundamental terms in Indian philosophies of language.

### Vācakatā - वाचकता

Signifying capacity.

The abstract suffix:

**-ता - -tā**.

Thus:

**वाचकता - vācakatā**

The condition of being a signifier.

And:

### Svarūpapratipatti - स्वरूपप्रतिपत्ति

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**रूप - rūpa**

Nature, form.

**प्रतिपत्ति - pratipatti**

Apprehension, acquisition, understanding, realization.

Related to *prati-√pad*, to arrive at, obtain or understand.

Thus:

**स्वरूपप्रतिपत्ति - svarūpapratipatti**

Apprehension of one's own nature.

Now here's the philosophical claim.

At the level of ordinary convention, the word *go* signifies a cow because of an established linguistic association.

But Abhinavagupta is explaining a deeper condition.

Signification itself belongs to a conscious manifestation in which the signifier and signified become apprehensible.

Their ultimate ground is not outside the supreme expressive power.

### The difference between convention and identity

This is important because Abhinavagupta isn't simply claiming that every word possesses one eternal, unchangeable conventional meaning in all languages.

If that were his argument, linguistic diversity would become unintelligible.

But we've just spent an entire installment showing that he accommodates an astonishing range of sonic variation.

His deeper claim concerns the relation between signifier and signified within consciousness.

The distinct conventional relation is one manifestation of that broader unity.

### A useful analogy

Imagine a word printed on a page.

The ink marks are physically present.

But merely having the ink marks does not guarantee that a reader understands them.

A person who doesn't know Sanskrit might see:

**गौः - gauḥ**

And recognize only a strange shape.

A Sanskrit reader can recognize a word.

The difference involves learned capacities.

But even learning the word requires the prior capacity to apprehend patterns, retain associations and distinguish referents.

Abhinavagupta's claim is that those capacities ultimately belong to consciousness.

Not to the ink as an independently self-explanatory object.

### The editor's gloss

Immediately after the statement about *vācakatā*, the Kashmir Series inserts a bracketed note asking how the signifier could differ from the signified if their respective realities weren't properly understood.

That gloss makes an interpretive point.

But it is not the next uninterrupted sentence of Abhinavagupta's argument.

The actual prose moves straight into a concrete example.

And it's the most important word of the installment.

**गो - go.**

Cow.

The entire metaphysics of language is about to be tested on a cow.

Not a sacred cosmic cow.

Not the Goddess in bovine form.

Just the ordinary Sanskrit word for cow.

A surprisingly dangerous animal for philosophers.


## 10. गो - Go: The world's most philosophically inconvenient cow

Abhinavagupta now asks us to consider the long-established association between a word and its referent.

His example involves:

**गोशब्दपरामर्शः**

*Gośabdaparāmarśaḥ.*

The apprehension or recollection of the word *go*.

And:

**अभ्यासवशात् - abhyāsavaśāt**

Through the force of repeated use or practice.

Now this is an exceptionally sophisticated example.

Because the meaning of a familiar word often appears immediately.

When you hear "cow," you don't need to consciously reconstruct the original linguistic agreement.

The word is simply understood.

The association has become habitual.

And Abhinavagupta uses that experience to illustrate the relation between convention and the deeper power of apprehension.

### Go - गो

Cow.

Now the word is fascinating in Sanskrit because its stem belongs to the *go* declension.

Let's learn some forms.

**गौः - gauḥ**

Cow, nominative singular.

**गाम् - gām**

Cow, accusative singular.

**गोः - goḥ**

Of the cow, genitive singular, or from the cow, ablative singular.

**गवि - gavi**

In the cow, locative singular.

Now these are not generated by simply attaching ordinary *-a*-stem endings to *go*.

The stem has its own inflectional pattern.

And that is worth learning.

### Śabda - शब्द

Word, sound.

Thus:

**गोशब्द - gośabda**

The word *go*.

### Parāmarśa - परामर्श

Apprehension, reflective reference, recollection.

From the *parā-√mṛś* family.

We've encountered this repeatedly.

Now:

**गोशब्दपरामर्श - gośabdaparāmarśa**

The apprehension of the word *go*.

### Abhyāsa - अभ्यास

Repetition, practice, habituation.

From:

**अभि - abhi**

Toward, intensively.

**√अस् - as**

In the verbal formation associated with repeated practice, through the established noun *abhyāsa*.

And:

**वश - vaśa**

Control, influence, power.

The ablative:

**वशात् - vaśāt**

Through the force or influence of.

Thus:

**अभ्यासवशात् - abhyāsavaśāt**

Through habituation.

Now the philosophical observation.

When a word has been used repeatedly for a long time, its meaning can become immediately available.

The learned association no longer requires a conscious act of recalling the original agreement.

The conventional relationship has become effortless.

### The cow in your head

Imagine somebody says:

"Go."

You know Sanskrit.

You immediately apprehend the meaning "cow."

You don't think:

"Excellent. I heard a voiced velar consonant followed by a long vowel, then consulted my internal lexical database, then reconstructed a historical agreement between ancient speakers."

No.

You simply understand the word.

Now here's the fascinating thing.

The fact that the association has become automatic does not establish that it was never conventional.

It was learned.

But the ease with which it is now apprehended reveals something about the deeper capacities of awareness and memory.

And Abhinavagupta uses that to examine the relationship between conventional signification and nonconventional consciousness.

### A genuine textual variant

Here the Sanskrit witnesses differ.

The Kashmir Series has a reading indicating that the apprehension of the long-established word has **attained a nonconventional condition** through habituation.

The GRETIL transcription, by contrast, transmits:

**साङ्केतिकतामापन्नः - sāṅketikatām āpannaḥ**

Having acquired conventionality.

Those aren't identical propositions.

The first emphasizes how a learned association comes to operate immediately, almost as though its convention had disappeared.

The second emphasizes the establishment of the conventional association through practice.

Both are philosophically intelligible within the surrounding discussion.

But the difference matters, and I won't conceal it. ; \]

### The philosophical conclusion is deeper than either reading

Whether the immediate emphasis falls on the acquisition of convention or its apparently effortless operation, the next point is secure.

The act of apprehending the word already occurs within consciousness.

And even the original moment of establishing its meaning depended upon a prior capacity for apprehension.

The word's conventional meaning is not a substitute for consciousness.

It's one manifestation of its expressive power.

And this is where Abhinavagupta makes the argument explicit.

What about the very first time somebody learns the word?

What allows the learner to recognize the convention?

And if the answer is "another convention," haven't we merely postponed the problem?

Now the child returns.

And with it, the infinite regress.


## 11. The first child who learns “cow” - How does convention begin?

Now comes the decisive argument on Kashmir Series p. 195.

Abhinavagupta says that even in the case of a child, the nonconventional nature of consciousness must already be present.

The key phrase is:

**आदौ स्थितैवासाङ्केतिकी सत्ता।**

*Ādau sthitaivāsāṅketikī sattā.*

The nonconventional reality is already present at the beginning.

And then:

**अन्यथानवस्थानात्।**

*Anyathānavasthānāt.*

Otherwise there would be an infinite regress.

Now we're getting somewhere.

The entire question of language acquisition has led Abhinavagupta directly into a classic philosophical problem.

### Bāla - बाल

Child.

We've encountered this before.

But now the child is being used to investigate the original possibility of linguistic understanding.

### Ādau - आदौ

At the beginning.

From:

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning.

Locative singular:

**आदौ - ādau**.

Now remember the *-i* stem declension.

The locative singular can end in **-au**.

### Sattā - सत्ता

Existence, being, the condition of reality.

Related to:

**सत् - sat**

Being, existent.

And the abstract formation:

**सत्ता - sattā**.

Here the term refers to the conscious reality already present before any particular convention is learned.

### Anavasthā - अनवस्था

Infinite regress, failure to arrive at a stable foundation.

Let's break it down.

**अन् - an-**

Negative prefix.

**अवस्था - avasthā**

Position, state, condition, establishment.

Thus:

**अनवस्था - anavasthā**

Nonestablishment.

In philosophical argument, it can designate an infinite regress.

Now the ablative:

**अनवस्थानात् - anavasthānāt**

Because of regress or nonestablishment.

### The infinite regress argument

Imagine teaching a child the word "cow."

You point at a cow.

You say:

"Cow."

The child begins to associate the sound with the animal.

But how does the child understand that you are trying to indicate something?

How do they recognize the pointing gesture?

How do they distinguish the cow from the grass?

How do they retain the sound?

How do they connect the remembered sound with subsequent encounters?

The answer cannot simply be:

"Because they already knew the word cow."

That would presuppose what you're trying to explain.

Nor can every aspect of understanding be explained through an earlier convention without eventually raising the same question again.

Now the argument isn't that children require a fully formed philosophy of consciousness before learning words.

Obviously not.

It concerns the logical priority of certain capacities.

**Learning a convention presupposes an ability to apprehend and relate things that cannot itself depend entirely upon that very convention.**

That's the central claim.

And for Abhinavagupta, the ultimate reality of that capacity is consciousness.

### The reference to previous births

The Sanskrit also mentions:

**जन्मान्तरानुसरणेऽपि**

*Janmāntarānusaraṇe 'pi.*

Even when considering continuity with another birth.

Now that's important.

The text doesn't make the argument depend merely upon the assumption that a child begins the current life with no prior conditioning.

It explicitly invokes the possibility of continuity across births, which belongs to its traditional religious framework.

But even that doesn't eliminate the philosophical question.

If one convention was acquired in an earlier life, what made its acquisition possible?

Another earlier convention?

And another?

And another?

The regress remains unless some underlying capacity for apprehension is acknowledged.

Now, this is a **philosophical argument within the Śaiva framework**, not scientific evidence for recollection across lives.

The important point is the regress argument, which can be understood independently of accepting rebirth.

And that is why Abhinavagupta says the nonconventional nature of consciousness must already be present.

### Saṅketagrahaṇa - सङ्केतग्रहण

Now the decisive compound.

**सङ्केत - saṅketa**

Convention.

**ग्रहण - grahaṇa**

Grasping, apprehension.

Thus:

**सङ्केतग्रहण - saṅketagrahaṇa**

The apprehension of a convention.

And:

**उपपत्ति - upapatti**

Establishment, intelligibility, possibility, coherent explanation.

The commentary says:

**एवमेव खलु सङ्केतग्रहणोपपत्तिः।**

*Evam eva khalu saṅketagrahaṇopapattiḥ.*

Only in this way is the acquisition of convention intelligible.

Now the grammatical and philosophical structure is clear.

The convention is apprehended.

But the capacity to apprehend isn't produced by an infinite chain of conventions.

It is grounded in consciousness.

### The child and the world's worst dictionary

Imagine a teacher addressing a child.

"That is a cow."

The child looks puzzled.

"What's a cow?"

"It's what the word cow means."

"What's a word?"

"A sound with a meaning."

"What's meaning?"

"Look it up."

The teacher opens a dictionary.

The child eats the dictionary.

A sensible response, frankly.

Because the entire explanation has become circular.

At some point, the explanation must appeal to the actual ability to understand, attend, distinguish and learn.

That's what Abhinavagupta wants to establish.

And now he invokes his great predecessor.

**Utpaladeva.**


## 12. Utpaladeva - Recognition must be prior to the convention it recognizes

Abhinavagupta attributes the preceding conclusion to the explanation of Utpaladeva in his commentary on the *Īśvarapratyabhijñā*.

The important thing is that Abhinavagupta does **not** provide a fresh, complete metrical quotation from Utpaladeva at this point.

He identifies the authority for the argument.

The conclusion is that conventional understanding requires a more fundamental capacity for consciousness and recognition.

This is entirely appropriate to Pratyabhijñā philosophy.

Because the tradition doesn't treat cognition as merely a sequence of disconnected impressions.

It emphasizes the relation between manifestation, self-awareness, memory and recognition.

### Pratyabhijñā - प्रत्यभिज्ञा

Now let's revisit this wonderful word.

**प्रति - prati**

Back, toward, in relation to.

**अभि - abhi**

Toward, directly.

**√ज्ञा - jñā**

To know.

The noun:

**प्रत्यभिज्ञा - pratyabhijñā**

Recognition.

Now compare:

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

And:

**अभिज्ञान - abhijñāna**

Recognition, identification, recollection.

The technical meaning of *pratyabhijñā* involves recognizing an identity that is not exhausted by disconnected presentations.

For example:

You see somebody today.

You remember them from years ago.

You recognize:

"This is the same person."

The present perception and past memory are distinguishable.

Yet recognition relates them.

### Why convention requires recognition

Now return to the cow.

You hear the word *go* today.

You heard it yesterday.

You encounter another cow tomorrow.

The physical sounds differ.

The particular animals differ.

The moments differ.

Yet the learned association can be retained and applied.

That requires a capacity for relating presentations.

And Abhinavagupta's argument is that such apprehension cannot be exhaustively explained by another convention without regress.

### The crucial philosophical distinction

This doesn't establish that every conventional word possesses a timeless, innate, fixed meaning.

The word *go* is still a Sanskrit lexical convention.

Other languages use other expressions.

And meanings can change historically.

The deeper claim concerns **the conscious capacity that makes any such convention intelligible**.

That capacity isn't simply another item in the dictionary.

Now the Pratyabhijñā connection becomes clear.

The recognition of the word, its referent and its continued identity across occasions depends upon consciousness.

And the supreme form of that consciousness is not fundamentally separate from the reality the tradition calls Śiva.

Thus:

**Conventional linguistic knowledge is a manifestation of awareness, not an ultimate replacement for it.**

Abhinavagupta is making a powerful metaphysical claim.

It shouldn't be confused with a complete modern developmental theory of language acquisition.

But it is an important philosophical explanation of why mere conventional designation cannot serve as the ultimate foundation of cognition.

And now comes the next authority.

Śrīkaṇṭha.

With a verse that sounds like a description of somebody having a completely disastrous ADHD afternoon.

Because apparently you can look at one thing, hear another, say a third, do a fourth, think about something else and eat at the same time.

And that, according to the quoted teaching, is precisely where the nonconventional conscious ground becomes relevant.


## 13. Śrīkaṇṭha's verse - Seeing one thing, hearing another, saying something else

Now we arrive at one of the most delightful quotations in the entire commentary.

VERSE ATTRIBUTED TO ŚRĪKAṆṬHA · KASHMIR SERIES P. 195 · FOLLOWING THE REFERENCE TO UTPALADEVA

**पश्यत्यन्यच्छृणोत्यन्यत्करोत्यन्यच्च जल्पति।**

**चिन्तयत्यन्यदा भुङ्क्ते तत्रासाङ्केतिकी स्थितिः॥**

*Paśyaty anyac chṛṇoty anyat karoty anyac ca jalpati.*

*Cintayaty anyadā bhuṅkte tatrāsāṅketikī sthitiḥ.*

One sees something different, hears something different, does something else and speaks; one thinks and eats at another time or under another aspect. There is the nonconventional condition.

Now, the verse is transmitted with significant differences in its final half. The alternative GRETIL segmentation is *cintayaty anyad ābhuṅkte*, yielding the sense "thinks of one thing and consumes or enjoys another." Both are relevant to the theme of simultaneous or differently directed activities.

More importantly, the Kashmir Series prints **साङ्केतिकी - sāṅketikī**, "conventional," at the end, whereas the GRETIL and independent parallel transcription support **असाङ्केतिकी - asāṅketikī**, "nonconventional." I've displayed the latter while keeping the disagreement explicit. ; ; \]

Why does this matter?

Because the two readings change the verse's philosophical emphasis.

With *asāṅketikī*, the plurality of distinguishable activities illustrates an underlying condition not itself dependent upon convention.

With *sāṅketikī*, the verse describes a condition of conventional differentiation.

The surrounding prose is concerned with the nonconventional ground of conventional expression, which makes the first reading especially intelligible.

But the actual manuscript question deserves its own critical examination.

Now let's learn the verbs.

### Paśyati - पश्यति

He or she sees.

From √**पश् - paś**, to see.

Third-person singular present.

Now:

**पश्यति अन्यत् → पश्यत्यन्यत्**

*paśyati anyat → paśyaty anyat.*

Why?

Because final **i** becomes the semivowel **y** before a following vowel.

Now the written form:

**पश्यत्यन्यत् - paśyaty anyat**

Sees something else.

### Śṛṇoti - शृणोति

Hears.

From √**श्रु - śru**, to hear.

Third-person singular present.

Now the sandhi:

*śṛṇoti + anyat → śṛṇoty anyat.*

Again:

**i + a → ya** in this environment.

And the actual phrase:

**शृणोत्यन्यत् - śṛṇoty anyat**.

### Karoti - करोति

Does, makes, performs.

From √**कृ - kṛ**, to do.

Third-person singular present.

Now:

*karoti + anyat → karoty anyat.*

**करोत्यन्यत् - karoty anyat**.

### Jalpati - जल्पति

Speaks, talks, chatters.

From √**जल्प् - jalp**, to speak.

Third-person singular present.

The joined phrase is:

**अन्यच्च जल्पति - anyac ca jalpati**.

Now why *anyac ca*?

Underlying:

**अन्यत् + च - anyat + ca**.

The final *t* assimilates before *c*.

Thus:

**अन्यच्च - anyac ca**.

A useful consonant-sandhi example.

### Cintayati - चिन्तयति

Thinks, considers.

From √**चिन्त् - cint**, to think or reflect, in the relevant verbal formation.

Third-person singular.

### Bhuṅkte - भुङ्क्ते

Eats, consumes, enjoys.

From √**भुज् - bhuj**, to enjoy, consume or partake.

Third-person singular present middle voice.

Now notice the consonants.

**ङ्क्त = ङ् + क् + त**.

The word:

**भुङ्क्ते - bhuṅkte**.

This is not an ordinary *-a*-stem noun.

It's an actual finite verb with its own present formation.

### Anyat - अन्यत्

Something else, another thing.

Neuter nominative or accusative singular.

Now notice the repeated word.

**अन्यत् - anyat**

Another.

Another.

Another.

The verse is deliberately emphasizing differentiated content.

The eye encounters one object.

The ear another.

Speech has its own content.

Action is directed somewhere else.

And thought or eating may involve another object or occasion.

Yet the entire experiential situation is not constituted by a collection of independently ultimate consciousnesses.

### The spectacular everyday example

Imagine you're sitting in a café.

You see a gecko on the wall.

You hear somebody speaking Khmer.

You're typing a Sanskrit translation.

You're thinking about Śiva.

You're drinking tea.

And somewhere in the background you're trying to remember whether the word *go* declines as *gauḥ* or *goḥ*.

Several different operations.

Different contents.

Different sensory and conceptual relations.

Now imagine a philosopher insisting that each requires a separate ultimate consciousness.

One for sight.

One for hearing.

One for thought.

One for speech.

One for bodily action.

And yet another specifically assigned to the bloody gecko.

Abhinavagupta's system says no.

The differentiation belongs to manifestation.

But the conscious ground isn't thereby multiplied into unrelated substances.

That's precisely the kind of structure the verse illustrates.

### What the verse does not establish

It does not prove that every sensory and cognitive process occurs simultaneously in the modern neuroscientific sense.

Nor that attention can fully process unlimited independent tasks without interference.

The philosophical issue concerns the possibility of differentiated presentations within a common conscious ground.

And that is much more interesting than the crude claim that the human mind can multitask perfectly.

### The attribution

The *Īśvarapratyabhijñāṭīkā* is cited immediately before this verse for the preceding discussion of convention.

The verse itself is followed by the attribution to **Śrīkaṇṭha**.

We should therefore not present it as an independently verified metrical quotation from Utpaladeva.

The textual sequence distinguishes the reference to Utpaladeva's explanation from the Śrīkaṇṭha verse.

And now Abhinavagupta adds another brief line from the same authority.

It concerns the mind and the eyes.


## 14. मनोऽप्यन्यत्र - The mind goes one way, the eyes another

The next quoted fragment begins:

**मनोऽप्यन्यत्र निक्षिप्तं चक्षुरन्यत्र पातितम्।**

*Mano 'py anyatra nikṣiptaṃ cakṣur anyatra pātitam.*

Even the mind is placed elsewhere, while the eye is directed somewhere else.

Now this is a particularly good continuation.

Because it distinguishes the locus of mental attention from the visual orientation.

### Manas - मनस्

Mind.

The neuter nominative singular:

**मनः - manaḥ**.

Now:

**मनः + अपि → मनोऽपि**

*manaḥ + api → mano 'pi.*

The result includes the avagraha:

**ऽ**

And:

**अपि - api**

Even, also.

Thus:

**मनोऽपि - mano 'pi**

Even the mind.

### Anyatra - अन्यत्र

Elsewhere.

An indeclinable locative adverb.

Related to:

**अन्य - anya**

Other.

And the adverbial formation:

**-त्र - -tra**.

Thus:

**अन्यत्र - anyatra**

Elsewhere.

### Nikṣiptam - निक्षिप्तम्

Placed, deposited, cast.

From:

**नि - ni**

Down, into.

**√क्षिप् - kṣip**

To throw, cast or place.

Past participle:

**निक्षिप्त - nikṣipta**.

Neuter nominative singular:

**निक्षिप्तम् - nikṣiptam**.

Agreeing with:

**मनः - manaḥ**.

Now:

### Cakṣus - चक्षुस्

Eye, sight.

The nominative-accusative singular neuter:

**चक्षुः - cakṣuḥ**.

In connected speech, the form appears as:

**चक्षुरन्यत्र - cakṣur anyatra**.

Now:

### Pātitam - पातितम्

Caused to fall, cast, directed.

Related to the causative of √**पत् - pat**, to fall.

The past participle:

**पातित - pātita**.

Neuter nominative singular:

**पातितम् - pātitam**.

So:

**चक्षुरन्यत्र पातितम् - cakṣur anyatra pātitam**

The eye directed elsewhere.

### The actual experiential point

You may look at a page while thinking about something else.

Your visual field contains the page.

Your thoughts concern yesterday's conversation.

You hear a noise.

You shift attention.

The contents differ.

And the relation between awareness and its different objects becomes an important subject of philosophical inquiry.

Now, again, don't turn this into the assertion that all forms of sensory processing are identical or that attention never has limitations.

The point is that **the differentiated cognitive functions do not establish independent ultimate consciousnesses**.

And this brings us to the end of the discussion of convention.

Because Abhinavagupta now says that even conventional mantra ultimately returns to its own source.

The Sanskrit expression is magnificent.

**स्वबीजमनुधावत् - svabījam anudhāvat.**

Running after its own seed.

The word follows itself home.

And the destination is Anuttara.


## 15. स्वबीजमनुधावत् - Every word runs back toward its own seed

Now Abhinavagupta says that the mantra-form follows its own source and ultimately terminates in Anuttara.

The key phrase is:

**स्वबीजमनुधावदनुत्तरपदपर्यवसायि भवति।**

*Svabījam anudhāvad anuttarapadaparyavasāyi bhavati.*

Following its own seed, it comes to rest or culminate in the state of Anuttara.

Now that is a genuinely beautiful image.

The word runs after the source from which its power arises.

And the source is not merely another conventional word.

It's the supreme nondual awareness.

### Svabīja - स्वबीज

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

Thus:

**स्वबीज - svabīja**

One's own seed or source.

Now:

**स्वबीजम् - svabījam**

Accusative singular.

The object toward which the movement is directed.

### Anudhāvat - अनुधावत्

Following, running after, pursuing.

From:

**अनु - anu**

After, along, following.

**√धाव् - dhāv**

To run.

The present participle:

**अनुधावत् - anudhāvat**

Running after.

Now this is the perfect verb.

Not merely resting somewhere.

Not simply being associated with a source in a static classification.

The expression describes a movement toward one's own originating principle.

### Anuttarapada - अनुत्तरपद

**अनुत्तर - anuttara**

Unsurpassable.

**पद - pada**

State, station, locus.

Thus:

**अनुत्तरपद - anuttarapada**

The unsurpassable state.

### Paryavasāyin - पर्यवसायिन्

Terminating in, culminating in, arriving at a conclusion.

Related to the *pari-ava-√so* family.

Thus:

**पर्यवसायि - paryavasāyi**

That which culminates or comes to rest in.

Now the philosophical meaning.

A conventional word has a particular learned meaning.

But the power of awareness through which that word is apprehended isn't exhausted by that meaning.

If one traces the occurrence of word, meaning, recognition and reflective apprehension to its ultimate ground, Abhinavagupta says that ground is Anuttara.

### The word isn't literally running

Now, obviously, the word *go* doesn't grow little legs and sprint toward Śiva.

The movement is philosophical and contemplative.

It means that the apparently differentiated expression can be traced back to the conscious reality that makes it intelligible.

The phoneme.

The word.

The meaning.

The recognition.

The awareness in which the recognition occurs.

The structure can be investigated inwardly.

And this is why Abhinavagupta has been insisting on *anupraveśa*, entry into consciousness.

The method doesn't require destroying the word.

It requires understanding its ground.

### The conventionality variant returns

There's another textual disagreement here.

The Kashmir Series and GRETIL have a reading corresponding to **asāṅketikamantravapuḥ**, the nonconventional mantra-form.

The separate parallel Sanskrit presentation gives **sāṅketikamantravapuḥ**, the conventional mantra-form.

The difference is philosophically significant.

If the first reading is accepted, the sentence describes the already nonconventional mantra returning to its source.

If the second is accepted, it describes even the conventional mantra returning to its source.

The broader argument supports the applicability of the same movement to conventional expression, but we should keep the precise lexical variation visible. ; \]

Now the essential conclusion is that differentiated language does not establish a second ultimate consciousness.

The word's convention can be meaningful.

The word can designate a particular object.

But the nondual conscious ground is not confined by that convention.

And now comes the really extraordinary next step.

Anuttara isn't merely the final destination into which everything disappears.

It is also the source of the power through which everything manifests.

**The destination is also the origin of emission.**

Now we're back to Visarga.


## 16. Anuttara is not a cosmic storage cupboard - The return is also an emission

The commentary continues by describing Anuttara through:

**विसर्गदृष्ट्या प्रसरत्**

*Visargadṛṣṭyā prasarat.*

Expanding from the standpoint of Visarga, or emission.

Now that's crucial.

Because the movement toward Anuttara does not terminate in a permanently inert blank.

The supreme conscious ground includes the power of manifestation.

The same reality that receives differentiated expressions can also manifest them.

### Visarga - विसर्ग

Emission, release, sending forth.

From:

**वि - vi**

Outward, apart.

**√सृज् - sṛj**

To emit, release or create.

Thus:

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission.

And remember the written sign:

**ः**

The ordinary Sanskrit Visarga sign.

But in the Tantric system, the term receives a much more extensive metaphysical interpretation.

It represents an expression of Śakti's generative freedom.

### Dṛṣṭi - दृष्टि

Sight, vision, perspective.

From √**दृश् - dṛś**, to see.

Instrumental singular:

**दृष्ट्या - dṛṣṭyā**.

Thus:

**विसर्गदृष्ट्या - visargadṛṣṭyā**

From the standpoint of emission.

Now the phrase is important because Abhinavagupta is distinguishing **ways of considering the same reality**.

From the standpoint of reabsorption, differentiated manifestation returns to Anuttara.

From the standpoint of emission, the supreme freedom expands into an infinite diversity of forms.

The two movements are not independently ultimate processes imposed upon consciousness.

They're modes of Śakti's self-expression.

### Ananta - अनन्त

Endless, without end.

From:

**अन् - an-**

Negative prefix.

**अन्त - anta**

End.

Thus:

**अनन्त - ananta**

Endless.

### Samudāya - समुदाय

Collection, multitude, aggregation.

### Vaicitrya - वैचित्र्य

Variety, diversity, variegation.

Related to:

**विचित्र - vicitra**

Variegated.

### Saṃrambha - संरम्भ

Energetic undertaking, exertion, impulse toward manifestation.

We've encountered it in the account of *kriyāśakti*.

Now combine the concepts.

Anuttara is not merely a static condition considered apart from manifestation.

Its freedom includes the expression of innumerable differentiated forms.

And that active expressive power is described through Visarga.

### The library that writes itself

Imagine an enormous library.

Every possible book belongs to the collection.

Now imagine that the library isn't merely storing books.

It's also the power through which books can be written.

Terrible analogy if taken literally.

But it captures something important.

Anuttara isn't an inert repository of finished objects.

Its own freedom includes the possibility of manifesting differentiated forms.

And therefore the return of convention to Anuttara doesn't abolish the possibility of language.

It discloses the source through which language can manifest.

That's why the passage moves immediately from Anuttara to Visarga.

And then to the power named through the letter **ह - ha**.

Now the alphabet has come full circle.

The first vowel.

The power of emission.

The final breath-like sound.

And the entire consonantal universe.

But before the consonants appear again, the commentary introduces a subtle name.

**Śaktikuṇḍalinī.**


## 17. हकाराख्यशक्तिकुण्डलिनी - The power named through *ha*

At Kashmir Series pp. 195-196, Abhinavagupta says the power of Visarga extends into the *ha*-phase.

The relevant expression includes:

**हकाराख्यशक्तिकुण्डलिनी**

*Hakārākhyā śaktikuṇḍalinī*, in its compounded grammatical context.

Śaktikuṇḍalinī, named through the phoneme *ha*.

Now the precise reading is not completely uniform across the consulted witnesses.

The Kashmir Series and GRETIL have a form associated with **hakāra**, the *ha* sound.

The independent Sanskrit presentation includes **hakārārdha**, half the *ha* sound, at the corresponding point.

Both belong to the subtle phonemic treatment of Visarga, but they should not be silently treated as identical. ; ; \]

### Ha - ह

The consonant *ha*.

In Sanskrit grammatical classification, its articulation is associated with the laryngeal or throat region.

But in this Tantric context, it also represents a divine phonemic power.

### Hakāra - हकार

The *ha* sound or letter.

**ह - ha**

**कार - kāra**

A conventional phonemic naming element.

Thus:

**हकार - hakāra**.

### Ākhyā - आख्या

Named, designated.

Related to:

**आ - ā**

Toward, forth.

**√ख्या - khyā**

To name, announce or declare.

Thus:

**हकाराख्य - hakārākhya**

Named *ha*.

### Śakti - शक्ति

Power.

### Kuṇḍalinī - कुण्डलिनी

The coiled or spiralling Goddess-power.

Related to:

**कुण्डल - kuṇḍala**

Coil, ring, earring.

And the feminine formation:

**कुण्डलिनी - kuṇḍalinī**.

Now here's an important distinction.

The passage is not suddenly introducing a complete modern chakra diagram or a physiological exercise for forcing energy up the spine.

It's interpreting the phonemic power of Visarga through Śaktikuṇḍalinī.

The relation is metaphysical and esoteric.

We should follow the text, not import an unrelated practical manual.

### The bracketed Pañcastavī citation

At p. 196, the Kashmir Series inserts a substantial square-bracketed quotation from a Goddess hymn identified as the **Pañcastavī**.

It describes the divine power associated with the first seed of speech and Śaktikuṇḍalinī as engaged in the production of the universe.

The accompanying editorial gloss interprets the Goddess's power and the liberating significance of recognizing her nature.

This material is **not part of Abhinavagupta's uninterrupted sentence**.

The main prose runs from the discussion of the *ha*-power into the return through Bindu.

The hymn is a useful traditional illustration, but its placement belongs to the edition's explanatory apparatus.

Now, that's especially important because the bracket is long enough to make it seem as though the commentary has started a new independent discussion.

It hasn't.

The argument is still about the same movement.

Anuttara.

Visarga.

The *ha*-power.

Bindu.

And return to Anuttara.

Which brings us to the next profound statement.

**The differentiated power rests in its own nature through Bindu.**


## 18. Bindu - The word returns to the point of non-difference

Now we reach the next stage.

Abhinavagupta describes the *ha*-power returning through:

**बिन्दु - bindu**

The point.

And the relationship is explicitly one of return to Anuttara.

Let's recall what Bindu means in this context.

### Bindu - बिन्दु

Point, drop, dot.

In Sanskrit writing, a dot-like sign appears in the notation of nasal sounds.

But Tantric Bindu isn't exhausted by the orthographic mark.

It can express the concentrated condition of divine conscious power.

Here the relevant structure is the **reabsorption of differentiation into its nondual source**.

### Abheda - अभेद

Non-difference.

From:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**भेद - bheda**

Division, difference.

Thus:

**अभेद - abheda**

Non-difference.

Now here's the philosophical point.

The differentiated phonemic power is not destroyed as though it had never belonged to consciousness.

Its apparent independence is relinquished.

And the underlying identity becomes central.

### Saṅkrama - संक्रम

Transition, passage, transfer.

Related to:

**सम् - sam**

Together.

**√क्रम् - kram**

To step, proceed or move.

Thus:

**संक्रम - saṅkrama**

Passage or transition.

Now the locative:

**अनुत्तरपदे - anuttarapade**

In the state of Anuttara.

And:

**विश्रान्ति - viśrānti**

Repose.

We've encountered this repeatedly.

The differentiated power returns to repose in its own nature.

### Why Bindu isn't annihilation

Imagine somebody saying:

"The word returns to its source. Therefore all language disappears into nothingness."

No.

That would be exactly the wrong interpretation.

The supreme ground is self-revealing consciousness.

Not the complete absence of existence.

Not an inert blank.

Not an independent void outside awareness.

So the return through Bindu does not establish metaphysical annihilation.

It expresses the repose of differentiation in the nondual conscious reality.

### The difference between stopping speech and recognizing its ground

You can stop talking.

You can stop internally rehearsing sentences.

You can enter a relatively quiet mental condition.

But those facts alone don't establish the realization being discussed.

The philosophical point concerns the nature of awareness through which speech appears and disappears.

**The nondual ground is not manufactured by silence.**

Rather, the differentiated speech is manifest within that ground.

And this is why the return through Bindu is significant.

It symbolizes the recognition that the expressive power has never become fundamentally independent of Anuttara.

### The great reversal

Now think about the whole movement.

The nonconventional conscious power expresses conventional words.

Those words acquire meanings through learned associations.

Their apprehension depends upon awareness.

The words can therefore be traced back to the nondual ground.

And that ground includes the freedom of emission through which linguistic expression is possible again.

This is a circle in the symbolic exposition.

But it doesn't establish that the supreme consciousness must literally perform a temporal loop to remain complete.

Which is why Abhinavagupta now introduces a beautiful phrase.

**एकाक्षरसंविद् - ekākṣarasaṃvid.**

The consciousness of one imperishable syllable.

And we're back to the very first vowel.


## 19. एकाक्षरसंविद् - The one imperishable consciousness

Now comes the phrase:

**एकाक्षरसंविद्**

*Ekākṣarasaṃvid.*

The one-syllable or imperishable consciousness.

This is a marvellous Sanskrit expression because *akṣara* carries two relevant associations.

### Akṣara - अक्षर

A syllable, letter or phonemic unit.

But also:

Imperishable, undecaying.

The second sense can be analysed through:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**क्षर - kṣara**

Perishing, flowing away, dissolving.

Related to √**क्षर् - kṣar**, to flow or perish.

Thus:

**अक्षर - akṣara**

Imperishable.

And the established nominal sense is also a letter or syllable.

Now, we mustn't assume the word historically acquired both senses through exactly one uncomplicated derivation.

But the double significance is doctrinally useful.

**The phonemic unit and the imperishable conscious principle can be related through the same Sanskrit word.**

### Eka - एक

One.

### Saṃvid - संविद्

Consciousness.

Thus:

**एकाक्षरसंविद् - ekākṣarasaṃvid**

The consciousness associated with the one imperishable syllable.

Within the phonemic interpretation we've been studying, this points toward the supreme *a*-principle, Anuttara.

But don't interpret it as a claim that writing the character **अ** automatically produces realization.

The written sign is a representation.

The supreme conscious reality is the doctrinal referent.

### Why is this consciousness beyond dependence upon time and place?

Now Abhinavagupta describes its intrinsic freedom through an enormous compound involving:

**देश - deśa**

Place.

**काल - kāla**

Time.

**कलना - kalanā**

Articulation or differentiating power.

**उपादान - upādāna**

Material basis or contributing cause, in the relevant philosophical context.

And:

**नैरपेक्ष्य - nairapekṣya**

Independence, nondependence.

Now that last word is worth learning carefully.

### Apekṣā - अपेक्षा

Dependence, expectation, requirement.

From the *apa-√īkṣ* family of looking toward or expecting.

Now:

**निरपेक्ष - nirapekṣa**

Independent of something, without dependence.

And the abstract noun:

**नैरपेक्ष्य - nairapekṣya**

The condition of independence.

Instrumental singular:

**नैरपेक्ष्येण - nairapekṣyeṇa**.

Thus the sentence expresses independence from spatial, temporal and causal limitations of the relevant kind.

### The crucial distinction

Now, don't reduce this to:

"Śiva exists at an extremely early time."

That's not the point.

If Anuttara were merely the first event on a timeline, it would already be subject to temporal relations.

But Abhinavagupta's argument is that time and differentiation belong to the manifestation of consciousness.

The supreme reality isn't simply another event within that manifestation.

And that's why he can say that the one *akṣara*-consciousness possesses its fullness intrinsically.

It doesn't acquire completeness by adding events.

Nor does it cease to be complete because differentiated events appear.

### The remarkable word jhagiti

Now comes a delightful Sanskrit adverb:

**झगिति - jhagiti**

Suddenly, instantly, in a flash.

Abhinavagupta says that the conscious power rushes into the domain of Visarga in this manner.

But here's the subtle philosophical point.

The word is used to express the absence of a laborious external sequence.

Not to establish that the supreme reality has a measurable reaction time.

We're not timing Bhairava with a stopwatch.

The expression emphasizes immediacy.

And this brings us back to the distinction between *krama* and *akrama*.

The teaching can describe the phases of manifestation in sequence.

The supreme conscious freedom isn't constituted by the sequence.

That's why the movement from Anuttara to Visarga can be described as immediate.

Now we're approaching the final paragraph of Part XVIII.

The entire vowel-series is about to reappear.

And then the consonantal universe.

All within one continuous conscious power.


## 20. The vowel-powers emerge again - But they were never absent

At Kashmir Series p. 197, Abhinavagupta returns to the powers we've been studying since Part XII.

The Sanskrit says:

**विसर्गभूमिश्लेष एव ... आकारादीनां स्थितिः।**

*Visargabhūmiśleṣa eva ... ākārādīnāṃ sthitiḥ.*

The vowel-powers beginning with *ā* are established through their relation to the domain of Visarga.

Now here's the crucial point.

**We're not starting the vowel exposition over because Abhinavagupta has forgotten it.**

He's returning to it at a deeper level.

Earlier, we learned the powers analytically.

Now we understand how they belong to the same movement of manifestation and return.

### Visargabhūmi - विसर्गभूमि

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission.

**भूमि - bhūmi**

Ground, level, domain.

Thus:

**विसर्गभूमि - visargabhūmi**

The domain of emission.

### Śleṣa - श्लेष

Contact, connection, joining or close union.

Related to √**श्लिष् - śliṣ**, to cling or embrace.

Thus:

**विसर्गभूमिश्लेष - visargabhūmiśleṣa**

The conjunction with the domain of Visarga.

And:

### Sthiti - स्थिति

Abiding, establishment, maintenance.

From √**स्था - sthā**, to stand.

So the vowel-powers are described as having their establishment within this relation.

Now let's recall the sequence.

**आनन्द - ānanda**

Bliss.

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will.

**ईशन - īśana**

Sovereign orientation.

**उन्मेष - unmeṣa**

Opening or emergence.

Then the further powers of expansion, differentiation and action.

These expressions aren't independent substances.

They're differentiated aspects of Śakti.

And the point of the passage is that their manifestation belongs to Visarga without requiring the supreme reality to abandon its nondual nature.

### The difference between emergence and production from nothing

Imagine the vowel-powers as different aspects of one expressive capacity.

They become distinguishable.

But that doesn't mean the conscious ground itself has been destroyed and rebuilt.

Nor that Śiva acquires will only after the appearance of one particular written vowel.

The sequence is a sacred exposition of powers already intrinsic to consciousness.

And now here's the striking consequence.

The Visarga-power, expanding in its fullness, becomes the *ha*-phase.

And the *ha*-phase becomes the basis for the consonantal multitude.

The whole alphabet emerges.

Again.

But now we can see why.


## 21. हकला - The *ha*-power becomes the differentiated universe

The commentary says:

**हकलामयः सम्पद्यते।**

*Hakalāmayaḥ sampadyate.*

It becomes constituted by the *ha*-power.

And then:

**कादिसत्तानन्ततत्त्वजालस्थितिः।**

*Kādisattānantatattvajālasthitiḥ.*

The establishment of the infinite network of principles beginning with *ka*.

Now that is the return to Part XV.

The same philosophical architecture.

Vowel-powers.

Their condensation.

Consonantal matrices.

The thirty-six tattvas.

The entire manifested universe.

But the emphasis has changed.

We've now investigated conventional language.

And discovered that its ultimate ground is not merely another convention.

So the movement into the consonantal universe is no longer just a phonemic classification.

It's the expression of the same nonconventional conscious freedom that makes meaningful speech possible.

### Hakalā - हकला

**ह - ha**

The phoneme.

**कला - kalā**

Power, aspect, phase.

Thus:

**हकला - hakalā**

The *ha*-power.

Now this isn't simply the ordinary consonant as pronounced in a classroom.

It is a symbolic phonemic designation within the Śaiva cosmology.

### Kādi - कादि

Beginning with *ka*.

**क - ka**

The first consonant in the ordinary consonantal order.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning.

Through sandhi:

*ka + ādi → kādi.*

Thus:

**कादि - kādi**

Beginning with *ka*.

### Anantatattvajāla - अनन्ततत्त्वजाल

Now here's a magnificent compound.

**अनन्त - ananta**

Endless.

**तत्त्व - tattva**

Principle, reality.

**जाल - jāla**

Net, network, web.

Thus:

**अनन्ततत्त्वजाल - anantatattvajāla**

The endless network of principles.

And:

**स्थिति - sthiti**

Establishment, abiding.

So the full compound concerns the establishment of the differentiated universe through an immense structure of principles.

Now notice the movement.

The one conscious freedom does not need to create a second ultimate reality to manifest plurality.

It possesses the power of differentiation.

That power is expressed through the divine phonemic system.

And the result is the manifested world.

### The universe as a differentiated sentence

Imagine the entire universe as a sentence.

Different words.

Different meanings.

Different grammatical relations.

Some nouns.

Some verbs.

Some adjectives.

Different possibilities of combination.

The sentence has a definite structure.

But the existence of that structure doesn't require every word to be produced by a different ultimate author.

Now the metaphor is imperfect.

The universe isn't literally a written Sanskrit sentence.

And ordinary language has its own historical and social conditions.

But the analogy captures the structural point.

**Manifest differentiation does not require independent ultimate plurality.**

The one power can articulate many forms.

And this is why the entire consonantal universe can be understood through the *ha*-phase of Visarga.

Now, at this exact point, the commentary makes its final move.

The *ha*-power returns into Bindu.

And Bindu returns to Anuttara.

The entire universe is gathered home.

Not destroyed.

Not denied.

But understood through its nondual ground.

That's the culmination.


## 22. हकलैव पुनरपि बिन्दौ - Even the last power returns to Bindu

Now Abhinavagupta says:

**हकलैव च पुनरपि बिन्दावनुप्रविशन्ती।**

*Hakalaiva ca punar api bindāv anupraviśantī.*

And the *ha*-power itself, entering Bindu once again...

Then:

**अनुत्तरपद एव पर्यवस्यति।**

*Anuttarapada eva paryavasyati.*

...comes to rest in the state of Anuttara.

Now that's a beautiful ending to the argument.

Because we've followed the entire movement.

From fullness.

To differentiated cognition.

To convention.

To language.

To the word *go*.

To the original capacity for understanding.

To the supreme conscious ground.

Then outward again through Visarga.

Through the vowel-powers.

Through the *ha*-power.

Through the consonantal universe.

And finally back to Bindu and Anuttara.

The same reality.

Different manifestations.

No independent second Absolute.

### Bindau - बिन्दौ

Now let's learn the grammar.

**बिन्दु - bindu**

Point.

A masculine *-u* stem.

Locative singular:

**बिन्दौ - bindau**

In Bindu.

Notice **-au**.

We've encountered this pattern before.

Compare:

**गुरु - guru**

Teacher.

**गुरौ - gurau**

In or with reference to the teacher, locative singular.

So:

**बिन्दौ - bindau**

In Bindu.

### Anupraviśantī - अनुप्रविशन्ती

Entering.

From:

**अनु - anu**

Along, into.

**प्र - pra**

Forth.

**√विश् - viś**

To enter.

The feminine present participle:

**अनुप्रविशन्ती - anupraviśantī**.

Why feminine?

Because it describes:

**कला - kalā**.

The divine power.

### Paryavasyati - पर्यवस्यति

Terminates, culminates, comes to rest.

From the *pari-ava-√so* family.

Third-person singular present:

**पर्यवस्यति - paryavasyati**.

And:

### Anuttarapada - अनुत्तरपद

The unsurpassable state.

Now this is the central conclusion.

The differentiated manifestation doesn't establish an independently existing reality outside consciousness.

Its ultimate nature is grounded in Anuttara.

### The cosmic return

Imagine the entire alphabet having emerged.

Fifty letters.

Sixty-four letters in the expanded classification.

Every word.

Every sentence.

Every convention.

Every semantic relation.

Every object of consciousness.

Now the whole structure is traced inward.

Not physically erased.

But recognized through the conscious power that made its manifestation possible.

The multiplicity doesn't cease to have occurred.

Its ultimate independence is denied.

And the ground is recognized.

That's the purpose of the return through Bindu.

### The crucial distinction about liberation

It would be a mistake to interpret this merely as:

"Everything disappears, therefore liberation."

We've already distinguished the supreme reality from an unconscious blank.

The point is recognition of the self-revealing nature of consciousness.

The return through Bindu is a phonemic expression of that nondual recognition.

Not simply the destruction of experience.

And that brings us to the last sentence of the present movement.

One of the most beautiful statements in the whole commentary.


## 23. एकैवाद्वयपरिपूर्णरूपा - The Goddess remains one, nondual and full

Abhinavagupta concludes:

**एकैवाद्वयपरिपूर्णरूपा संवेदनसत्ताभट्टारिकेयं परा भगवती परमेश्वरी।**

*Ekaivādvayaparipūrṇarūpā saṃvedanasattābhaṭṭārikeyaṃ parā bhagavatī parameśvarī.*

This venerable supreme Goddess, the reality of consciousness, is one alone, nondual and completely full.

And then he adds:

**न त्वत्र क्रमादियोगः कश्चित्।**

*Na tv atra kramādiyogaḥ kaś cit.*

There is no ultimate involvement of sequence and similar determinations here.

Now that is the conclusion we've been building toward since Part XI.

The movement from Anuttara to manifestation isn't a process imposed upon the Absolute by an external temporal law.

The differentiated sequence belongs to the exposition and manifestation.

The supreme reality remains nondual.

Let's learn the compounds.

### Ekā - एका

One.

Feminine nominative singular.

And:

**एव - eva**

Alone, indeed, precisely.

Thus:

**एकैव - ekaiva**

One alone.

The sandhi:

*ekā + eva → ekaiva.*

Because:

**ā + e → ai**.

### Advaya - अद्वय

Nondual.

From:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**द्वय - dvaya**

Pair, duality.

Thus:

**अद्वय - advaya**

Without a second.

### Paripūrṇa - परिपूर्ण

Completely full.

From:

**परि - pari**

All around, completely.

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa**

Full.

### Rūpā - रूपा

Having the nature or form of.

Thus:

**अद्वयपरिपूर्णरूपा - advayaparipūrṇarūpā**

Having a nature that is nondual and completely full.

### Saṃvedana - संवेदन

Cognition, conscious apprehension.

### Sattā - सत्ता

Being, reality.

### Bhaṭṭārikā - भट्टारिका

Venerable Lady, exalted Goddess.

Thus:

**संवेदनसत्ताभट्टारिका - saṃvedanasattābhaṭṭārikā**

The venerable Goddess whose reality is conscious apprehension.

And:

**परा - parā**

Supreme.

**भगवती - bhagavatī**

Venerable, blessed Goddess.

**परमेश्वरी - parameśvarī**

Supreme sovereign Goddess.

Now the philosophical meaning is stunningly direct.

All differentiated manifestations belong to the one conscious reality.

The vowels.

The consonants.

The tattvas.

The conventions.

The words.

The objects.

The processes of knowing.

All may be distinguished.

But the supreme Goddess remains one, nondual and full.

### The final warning against chronological literalism

The commentary explicitly rejects the application of *krama*, sequence, at the ultimate level here.

And that matters enormously.

Because we've spent many installments discussing stages.

First *a*.

Then *ā*.

Then *i*.

Then knowledge.

Then action.

Then Bindu.

Then Visarga.

Then consonants.

Then differentiated language.

Then return.

But the sequence is an explanatory articulation.

It's not a claim that the supreme consciousness was incomplete at the beginning, passed through a literal series of transformations, and finally became complete once more.

The completeness was never absent.

The manifestations are expressions of freedom.

**The universe is not the Absolute trying to become whole. It is the manifestation of a fullness that was never divided.**

Now that is the philosophical conclusion of Part XVIII.

And it's the perfect place to stop.

Because the next sentence begins an entirely new explanation.

Not of convention.

Not of the relation between signifier and signified.

But of the most important word in the entire Pratyabhijñā tradition.

**अहम् - aham.**

I.

And the sequence of phonemes that composes it.

Which is why Part XIX is going to be absolutely extraordinary.

**Convention isn't meaningless.** Words acquire meanings through learned associations.

**The sequence of manifestation isn't an external law imposed upon Śiva.** It expresses the power of his own Śakti.

And therefore:

**Linguistic convention is neither the creator of consciousness nor something fundamentally outside consciousness.**

That's the extraordinary middle position Abhinavagupta has established.

### Four especially useful writing exercises

**1. The two kinds of linguistic signification**

## साङ्केतिक - असाङ्केतिक

*Sāṅketika - asāṅketika*

The negative initial **अ** changes conventional into nonconventional. Learn to identify the prefix before trying to interpret the full compound.


**2. The cow's irregular declension**

## गो - गौः - गाम् - गोः - गवि

*Go - gauḥ - gām - goḥ - gavi*

Stem, nominative, accusative, genitive/ablative and locative singular.


**3. The Śrīkaṇṭha sandhi**

## पश्यति + अन्यत् → पश्यत्यन्यत्

*Paśyati + anyat → paśyaty anyat*

Remember the glide **इ → य्** before a following vowel.


**4. The return to the supreme ground**

## बिन्दौ - अनुत्तरपदे - विश्रान्तिः

*Bindau - anuttarapade - viśrāntiḥ*

In Bindu, in Anuttara, repose. Notice the locative endings of *bindu* and *pada*.


## 26. The final insight - The meaning of “cow” doesn't create consciousness

Now we can finally answer the question that began this installment.

**Who decided that *go* means cow?**

At the conventional linguistic level, the meaning belongs to the historical practices of a speech community.

The association is learned.

Maintained.

Remembered.

Repeated.

And used.

That part of the explanation is perfectly intelligible.

But Abhinavagupta asks a deeper question.

What makes it possible for a conscious being to learn and use such a convention?

How can the word be apprehended?

How can the object be recognized?

How can the sound be retained and applied again?

How can the original agreement be understood if every act of understanding requires an earlier agreement?

His answer is that convention presupposes a more fundamental capacity of consciousness.

And in Trika, that capacity ultimately belongs to the supreme self-revealing reality.

**The word doesn't create the awareness that understands it.**

The awareness makes the meaningful use of the word possible.

Now this doesn't mean the conventional meaning of *go* is innate.

Nor that Sanskrit is the only possible language.

Nor that every animal cry is secretly a grammatically complete Sanskrit sentence.

It means that the capacity for conscious apprehension cannot be exhaustively reduced to the conventions formed within it.

And that's a genuinely profound distinction.

Because it changes how we understand the relationship between sacred speech and realization.

The word *Śiva* isn't the supreme reality merely because somebody has written four Sanskrit letters.

The word *vimarśa* doesn't manufacture self-awareness.

And memorizing the definition of *Anuttara* doesn't create the unsurpassable conscious ground.

Those words can serve as means of understanding.

But the conscious reality through which they're understood is the very subject of the teaching.

**The teaching points through language toward the nature of awareness itself.**

And now Abhinavagupta completes the circle.

The conventional word returns to its seed.

The seed belongs to Anuttara.

Anuttara expresses the freedom of Visarga.

Visarga manifests the vowels and consonants.

The differentiated universe appears.

And that manifestation returns to Bindu and Anuttara.

No independently existing second consciousness.

No ultimate fragmentation.

No need to turn every convention into a cosmic absolute.

The Goddess remains:

**एकैवाद्वयपरिपूर्णरूपा।**

*Ekaivādvayaparipūrṇarūpā.*

One alone, nondual, completely full.

That is the heart of Part XVIII.

And now, after all this business with the cow, the child and the dictionary, Abhinavagupta arrives at the most important Sanskrit word of all.

**अहम् - aham.**

I.

Not merely the finite ego.

But the supreme self-revealing **Aham** of Bhairava.

The next installment will explain how the first phoneme **अ - a**, the power **ह - ha**, and Bindu are understood through the mystery of divine I-consciousness.

And then the entire process reverses.

**अहम् becomes मह...**

Manifestation and reabsorption.

One consciousness, two directions of exposition.

After eighteen installments of the Sanskrit alphabet, we've finally reached the word that knows it is speaking.
