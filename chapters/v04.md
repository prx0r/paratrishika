# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART IV - The Great Secret of the Heart: Kaulinī, the Mistress of Kula

*Abhinavagupta explains why pleasure is metaphysically interesting, why the Absolute is simultaneously hidden and completely obvious, why the word “mine” is a philosophical disaster, and why the Goddess dwelling in your Heart is secretly running the entire universe.*


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

The Goddess asked how recognition of Anuttara grants immediate Kaulika perfection and equality with Khecarī.

Abhinavagupta responded by explaining sixteen meanings of the Absolute, the structure of language, the nature of time, the whole family of divine powers, and how your mind can get so entangled in its own productions that Śiva essentially forgets he's Śiva.

One question.

An entire metaphysical civilization constructed in response.

And now we arrive at the last movement of his commentary on verse one.

But there's a surprise.

**Abhinavagupta isn't finished with the body, the senses, or even sexual pleasure.**

Then the Goddess will ask about the Great Secret, and the commentary will introduce Kaulinī, the Power dwelling in the Heart.

We're following the Sanskrit of the Kashmir Series edition (pp. 45-64), cross-checked against the GRETIL transcription and a separate Devanāgarī transcription.

An important textual detail: the editions don't always divide the verses identically. The commentary treats *etad guhyaṃ mahāguhyam* as the continuation of the Goddess's first question, then numbers the *hṛdayasthā* passage as verse 2. Some standalone editions count the *etad guhyaṃ* line as part of verse 2 and carry the following line into verse 3. There are also variants such as *kaulikī/kaulinī* and *labhāmi/vrajāmi*. We'll preserve those differences rather than blending them into an imaginary uniform original. ; ; \]

Right.

Back into the Heart.


## 1. Khecarī's last secret: the senses are not your enemies

We ended Part III with the recognition that the various powers of cognition are manifestations of the one Khecarī Śakti.

The fourfold differentiations associated with Vyomacarī, Gocarī, Dikcarī and Bhūcarī don't establish four independently existing consciousnesses.

They are expressions of divine power.

And Abhinavagupta concludes:

**तदेवं खेचरीसाम्यमेव मोक्षः।**

*Tad evaṃ khecarīsāmyam eva mokṣaḥ.*

Thus, equality with Khecarī is itself liberation.

Notice the word:

**साम्य - sāmya**

Equality, similarity, sameness.

Compare:

**सम - sama**: equal, even.

**समता - samatā**: equality.

**साम्य - sāmya**: the condition of likeness or equality.

The words are related, but they aren't identical grammatical formations.

Now the philosophical point.

Liberation isn't achieved by destroying the senses.

It isn't achieved by abolishing the powers of cognition.

It concerns recognition of their ultimate ground.

And Abhinavagupta immediately moves into some very concrete descriptions of embodied experience.

### ओजस् - Ojas

**ओजस् - ojas**

Vigour, strength, vital potency.

Notice the final **स् - s** in the stem.

This isn't simply *oja*.

The nominative singular is often **ओजः - ojaḥ**, while the dictionary stem is *ojas*.

This is another little Sanskrit lesson: dictionary stems and inflected forms aren't always identical.

Abhinavagupta associates vital potency with the central channel, the senses, the bodily energies and the process through which sensory stimulation can awaken desire.

The eye encounters form.

The ear encounters sound.

A remembered experience may awaken excitement without the original external stimulus being present.

Now, he isn't presenting a modern experimentally validated model of neuroscience or reproductive physiology. He's interpreting experience through the subtle physiology and Kaula symbolism of his tradition.

But the observation that interests him is straightforward:

**Even remembered or imagined content can affect the body.**

You hear a melody.

Later, merely remembering it can revive an emotional response.

You see someone you're attracted to.

Later, the image can reappear in memory and affect your bodily state.

Abhinavagupta takes this relationship between sensory cognition, memory, vital power and pleasure as a window into the way the conscious powers are interconnected.

### The Sanskrit: क्षोभ

**क्षोभ - kṣobha**

Agitation, disturbance, stirring, excitation.

From √*kṣubh*, to become disturbed or agitated.

Look at the consonant cluster:

**क्ष = क् + ष**

The first letter क loses its inherent *a* before joining ष.

So **क्षो** contains the joined consonants *kṣ* followed by the vowel **o**, represented by **ो**.

And the final भ retains its inherent *a*.

Hence:

*kṣo-bha*.

Now the important distinction.

*Kṣobha* isn't automatically psychological suffering.

It can mean stirring, excitation, or movement.

In Kaula contexts, the term can participate in discussions of creative energy and bliss.

Which brings us to an unexpectedly provocative part of the commentary.

## 2. Yes, Abhinavagupta actually discusses erotic pleasure

Not vaguely.

Not through a modern metaphor we've invented.

He explicitly discusses erotic excitement, bodily contact, the stirring of sexual energy, and the experience of bliss.

The commentary also considers how memory can evoke pleasure even in the absence of a partner.

This is entirely consistent with the larger Kaula interest in **visarga**, emission, and the symbolism of Śiva-Śakti union.

### The Sanskrit: सङ्घट्ट

**सङ्घट्ट - saṅghaṭṭa**

Conjunction, contact, coming together, collision.

Now look carefully at the letters.

**सङ् + घट्ट**

The **ङ् - ṅ** is a velar nasal.

It belongs to the same articulation family as *k* and *g*.

And **ट्ट - ṭṭa** is a doubled retroflex consonant.

This is not the same as **त्त - tta**, the dental combination in *anuttara*.

So:

- **त्त - tta**: dental.
- **ट्ट - ṭṭa**: retroflex.

Once you begin hearing the distinction, Sanskrit pronunciation gets considerably more precise.

In the philosophical account, *saṅghaṭṭa* can signify the union of Śiva and Śakti, as well as forms of experiential conjunction through which their symbolism is expressed.

Now, what is the point of bringing sex into a discussion of the Absolute?

It's certainly not that sexual pleasure automatically produces enlightenment.

Nor is the argument that the strongest possible physical sensation is identical to stable liberation.

Abhinavagupta is exploring how experiences of intense pleasure, self-awareness and creative power can reveal aspects of consciousness otherwise obscured in ordinary differentiated experience.

The movement of bliss may occur through an embodied event.

But the awareness in which it is experienced isn't ultimately a foreign object produced by the event.

This is a crucial distinction between **an occasion that discloses something** and **the ultimate source of what is disclosed**.

### चमत्कार - Camatkāra

One of our favourite Trika words returns.

**चमत्कार - camatkāra**

Wonder, astonishment, delighted apprehension.

In this context, the word participates in Abhinavagupta's discussion of the experiential intensity of conscious manifestation.

And he makes a surprising observation.

The same deep capacity for affective intensity isn't confined to what is conventionally called pleasure.

Even grief can involve an intense engagement of the Heart.

He mentions the emotional impact of a beloved person or child.

The point isn't that grief secretly feels pleasant, or that tragedy should be enjoyed.

It's that the underlying conscious capacity for intense, reflexive experience is not exhausted by the ordinary distinction between pleasure and pain.

In both, consciousness can be deeply stirred.

The particular emotional tone differs.

The manifestation of awareness remains.

This is subtle and easy to misunderstand.

Abhinavagupta isn't denying suffering. He's distinguishing the fundamental nature of conscious manifestation from the particular forms through which it is experienced.

### An old debate: Should one worship the external liṅga?

The commentary then takes an interesting detour through different ritual orientations.

Some Śaiva traditions emphasize worship of a consecrated external liṅga.

Other Kaula perspectives favour the immediately accessible body as the location of the full universe of principles.

And Abhinavagupta's own Trika argument doesn't simply require that one side be declared absolutely correct and the other utterly worthless.

Both prescribed practice and its rejection can become further conceptual restrictions if one loses sight of the nondual reality.

The body can be contemplated as the manifestation of the complete order of consciousness.

An external ritual object can also function as a support for recognition.

But neither makes Śiva present where he was metaphysically absent.

This is rather devastating for the person who imagines the supreme reality can only be reached by performing the correct external ceremony.

It's equally devastating for the person proudly announcing that he's too enlightened to use any ceremony.

Two egos.

One worships a stone.

The other worships his own refusal to worship a stone.

Abhinavagupta is interested in the consciousness underlying both.

### The final point of this passage

He interprets the union of conscious powers through images of bodily creativity, vital excitation, and the bliss of Śiva-Śakti.

And then explicitly connects this to the Goddess's original question:

Anuttara grants Kaulika perfection, and through its recognition one reaches equality with Khecarī.

Thus the discussion of embodied experience closes the exposition of verse one.

It also prepares us for the great question that follows.

Because the supreme reality can manifest itself in everything.

But it is not thereby universally recognized.

Which means we need to ask:

**How can something be absolutely present and yet remain secret?**

And that's exactly where the Goddess takes us next. ; ; \]


## 3. एतद्गुह्यं महागुह्यं - The Great Secret that isn't actually hidden

The Goddess continues:

## एतद्गुह्यं महागुह्यं कथयस्व मम प्रभो।

*Etad guhyaṃ mahāguhyaṃ kathayasva mama prabho.*

"Tell me this secret, this exceedingly great secret, my Lord."

Now we're in the territory of *guhya*, secrecy.

And the first thing Abhinavagupta does is ruin our ordinary understanding of what secrecy means.

Because we're imagining something hidden.

A secret teaching.

Some forbidden knowledge.

A dusty manuscript concealed in a Himalayan cave.

A secret mantra that only the grandmaster knows, and he'll reveal it when you've completed seventeen initiations and donated a suitable amount to the monastery roof fund.

But Abhinavagupta's point is much stranger.

**The supreme reality is hidden precisely while being the ground of every experience.**

### The Sanskrit laboratory: Guhya

**गुह्य - guhya**

Secret, concealed, hidden.

Related to √**guh**, to conceal, hide or cover.

Now compare:

**गुहा - guhā**

Cave, cavern, secret place.

**गुह्य - guhya**

Something concealed.

**रहस्य - rahasya**

Secret, mystery, confidential teaching.

And the compound:

**महागुह्य - mahāguhya**

A great secret.

Break it down:

- **mahā-** - great, large, mighty.
- **guhya** - secret.

The base is related to **महत् - mahat**, great, with *mahā-* appearing as the compound form.

Now here's the clever grammar.

The Goddess says:

**एतद्गुह्यम् - etad guhyam**

*Etad* \= this.

*Guhyam* \= secret.

Then:

**महागुह्यम् - mahāguhyam**

The great secret.

Both *guhyam* and *mahāguhyam* are neuter accusative singular forms.

The object to be explained is the secret itself.

But what sort of secret?

### The Great Cave of Māyā

Abhinavagupta introduces:

**माया - māyā**

The divine power associated with differentiated manifestation, which at the level of limited experience permits the obscuration of one's true nature.

Now, don't immediately translate this as "illusion" and announce that nothing exists.

Abhinavagupta's account is more nuanced.

He says the secret remains unrecognized because it is concealed in the cave of Māyā, associated with nonrecognition of one's own essential nature.

And yet the reality being concealed never becomes fundamentally non-luminous.

That's the paradox.

The Absolute isn't hidden in the ordinary sense that a missing object is hidden behind a cupboard.

Its concealment concerns the limited subject's failure to recognize the consciousness through which experience occurs.

Imagine looking everywhere for the light illuminating your room.

You inspect the furniture.

You search under the bed.

You rummage through the wardrobe.

But all your searching depends upon the illumination whose source you're trying to discover.

Now the analogy isn't perfect: physical light is an object of empirical investigation, while Trika's *prakāśa* is the self-revelatory character of consciousness.

Nevertheless, it helps us understand the structure.

**The means through which the search proceeds already depends on the reality being sought.**

### Hidden and unhidden at once

Abhinavagupta then makes a deliberate interpretive reversal.

The word *mahāguhya* is explored as something not merely hidden, but in another sense greatly unhidden.

The esoteric reading invokes **mahat aguhyam** - greatly unhidden.

Let's learn the negative formation:

**गुह्य - guhya**

Secret.

**अगुह्य - aguhya**

Not secret, unconcealed.

The initial *a-* negates the following adjective.

And this is where the apparently contradictory interpretations make sense.

From the standpoint of the contracted individual, reality is secret.

From the standpoint of consciousness's constant self-manifestation, it is not concealed at all.

You're looking for consciousness.

Through consciousness.

In consciousness.

While assuming consciousness is somewhere else.

The philosophical absurdity is spectacular.

And Abhinavagupta is having an absolute field day.

### Why Māyā is not simply an evil imposter

Now the commentary makes a remarkably important move.

It discusses Śuddhavidyā, the pure power of knowledge, appearing through the structures of differentiation.

These structures include:

**मातृ - mātṛ**

The knower.

**मान - māna**

The measure or means of knowledge.

**मेय - meya**

The measured or known object.

Notice the common epistemological family.

We've encountered related forms already:

*pramātṛ*, *pramāṇa*, and *prameya*.

But here Abhinavagupta is focusing on a triadic structure of manifestation itself.

The knower.

The means or act of knowing.

The known.

And he connects their differentiation with the triangular symbolism of Trika.

### त्रिकोण - Trikoṇa

**त्रि - tri**

Three.

**कोण - koṇa**

Angle, corner.

Together:

**त्रिकोण - trikoṇa**

Triangle, three-cornered.

Now here is what makes the interpretation subtle.

The pure consciousness does not become an entirely separate metaphysical substance when it appears through the differentiated structures of cognition.

Nor is Māyā simply an independent force capable of attacking Śiva from outside.

The power through which differentiation arises remains grounded in the divine consciousness.

**Māyā is binding insofar as its differentiated manifestation is misrecognized, not because it constitutes an independently existing ultimate reality.**

Abhinavagupta therefore considers the very same manifesting power through the language of *vidyā*, knowledge.

This doesn't mean the technical tattvas of Śuddhavidyā and Māyā should always be treated as interchangeable.

Rather, he's examining their relation through the nondual ground of manifestation.

The divine power that permits apparent limitation isn't absolutely alien to the power that reveals liberation.

And then the commentary moves into the genuinely magnificent phonemic interpretation.


## 4. The secret hidden in मह - The alphabet becomes a cosmological machine

Abhinavagupta examines the sounds of the words themselves.

And now you need to remember what we learned about Sanskrit phonemes.

The most important sound in the tradition's symbolic alphabet is:

**अ - a**

In this context, it represents the unsurpassable consciousness, Anuttara.

Then:

**ह - ha**

Associated in his phonemic symbolism with the power of emission, *visarga*.

And:

**म - ma**

Connected with the contracted individual and the completion of universal I-consciousness.

Together, these participate in the symbolism of:

# अहम्

## aham

I - interpreted as universal self-awareness

Ordinary Sanskrit *aham* is the first-person singular pronoun.

"I."

But the Trika interpretation treats its phonemic structure as a symbol of the entire universe gathered within universal I-consciousness.

A crucial distinction: this is not a normal etymological derivation of the pronoun *aham*. It is Abhinavagupta's sacred phonemic exegesis.

### The movement from A to Ha to Ma

At the level of the symbolism:

**अ - a**

The unsurpassable foundation.

**ह - ha**

The power of manifesting and emitting.

**म - ma**

The completion through the manifestation of the subject.

And the nasal completion of *aham* becomes significant in the associated doctrine of *bindu*.

### Bindu - बिन्दु

**बिन्दु - bindu**

A drop, point or dot.

In Tantric phonemic interpretation, it may represent the concentrated unity of powers and their relationship to the individual subject.

But we must distinguish written phonetics from esoteric symbolism.

In ordinary orthography, **अहं** is commonly written using the anusvāra **ं**, while **अहम्** writes a final म्.

The representation and pronunciation are governed by linguistic conventions.

The Tantric interpretation adds another level of meaning; it doesn't eliminate the grammar.

Now here's the extraordinary twist.

Abhinavagupta also explores a movement of reabsorption:

**ma → ha → a**

The reverse of the symbolic expansion through *a*, *ha*, and *ma*.

The movement from differentiation back into Anuttara.

And he finds a phonemic indication of it in the language of *mahāguhya*.

Now, do not mistake this for an ordinary grammatical sandhi rule.

Sanskrit grammar does not state that *mahā* is historically derived by reversing the cosmic alphabet.

Abhinavagupta is making an intentional esoteric resegmentation.

And it's worth understanding precisely because it reveals the methodology of the commentary.

He is not merely explaining what the text says at the level of conventional language.

He is showing how the sounds of the text can be interpreted as embodiments of the doctrines they express.

### The Sanskrit: प्रत्युपसंहार

**प्रत्युपसंहार - pratyupasaṃhāra**

Withdrawal, bringing back, reabsorption.

Break it into:

*prati + upa + saṃ + hāra*

The last element is related to √*hṛ*, to take, carry or remove.

The combined expression conveys drawing things back into a prior or underlying unity.

In the cosmological interpretation, the differentiated manifestations of consciousness are understood as returning into recognition of their own ground.

Not necessarily through literal physical destruction.

But through reabsorption of the apparent distinction into the unity of the Heart.

### Ahaṃ and the great inversion

Think about an ordinary cognition:

"I see that."

The structure involves:

**अहम् - aham**

I.

And:

**इदम् - idam**

This.

The perceiving subject and perceived object.

But Abhinavagupta's nondual ontology asks whether *idam* can ever truly exist as something independent of the universal *aham*.

The point is not that your personal ego literally creates every object by thinking about it.

That would turn Trika into an extraordinarily arrogant form of individual idealism.

Rather, universal I-consciousness is the ultimate ground of both limited subject and object.

The differentiated experience of "this" is not outside the reality of consciousness.

And the symbolic reversal of the phonemes illustrates the movement from manifest distinction back into self-recognition.

The word *mahāguhya* thus becomes a little map of manifestation and reabsorption.

The secret isn't merely a statement hidden behind the words.

**The very sounds are interpreted as participating in the metaphysical process described.** ; ; \]


## 5. Kathayasva - Why the Goddess asks Śiva to speak

Now we reach one of those moments where Abhinavagupta finds a second universe hiding in a perfectly ordinary verb.

**कथयस्व - kathayasva**

Tell, explain, speak.

This is a second-person singular imperative form used to address the Lord.

The meaning in context is:

"Tell me."

But Abhinavagupta introduces another reading.

He takes:

**कथय स्व - kathaya sva**

Treating *sva* separately.

And *sva* means one's own, the self, what belongs to oneself.

This is not the ordinary grammatical segmentation of *kathayasva*.

It's a deliberate interpretive redivision.

Now here's the philosophy.

The Goddess is asking Śiva to articulate what is fundamentally undivided.

The reality of Parā Vāk is not a succession of distinct sentences.

But teaching requires the appearance of differentiated speech.

Words.

Syllables.

A sequence.

A teacher.

A disciple.

So the Goddess's request can be read as:

Express in differentiated speech the reality that is undivided at the supreme level.

Now that's an extraordinarily important point.

Because the whole commentary is an example of exactly that process.

Abhinavagupta is using words to reveal what cannot be reduced to words.

He is arranging a sequence of concepts to point toward a reality that is not fundamentally constituted by conceptual succession.

Imagine trying to explain the taste of a mango.

You can describe sweetness.

Texture.

Aroma.

Ripeness.

But the description isn't identical to the experience of tasting it.

And yet language can guide someone toward the experience.

That's the function of the teaching.

Not to manufacture a new supreme reality.

But to clarify what must be recognized.

### Guru and disciple

Abhinavagupta explicitly connects this with the manifestation of the guru-disciple relationship.

The one consciousness appears through both positions.

The teacher expresses the teaching.

The disciple receives it.

But at the ultimate level, the reality enabling both isn't divided.

This is a subtle balance.

You should not conclude that all teachers are equally qualified, that distinctions in knowledge are illusory in everyday life, or that no one needs instruction.

The practical roles remain meaningful.

The ontological claim is that the ultimate ground of both roles is the same consciousness.

Thus the Goddess's request for explanation is itself a manifestation of the very power being explained.

The cosmos is using speech to explain the source of speech.

You couldn't make this up.

Well, Abhinavagupta could.

But he's doing it with remarkable philosophical consistency.


## 6. मम - Mama: The entire metaphysics of “mine”

Now things get even more interesting.

The Goddess says:

**मम - mama**

My, mine, of me.

In ordinary Sanskrit, *mama* is the genitive singular of the first-person pronoun.

Compare:

| Sanskrit | Meaning |
|--------|--------|
| **अहम् - aham** | I |
| **माम् - mām** | Me, accusative |
| **मया - mayā** | By me |
| **मह्यम् - mahyam** | To or for me |
| **मम - mama** | My, of me |
| **मयि - mayi** | In me |

Now Abhinavagupta starts playing with the implications of possessiveness.

Think about what ordinary "mine" means.

"My phone."

"My book."

"My body."

"My experience."

The word establishes a relationship between the possessor and something apparently possessed.

And ordinarily the two are distinguishable.

But what happens when universal consciousness says:

"Mine"?

What could possibly exist outside it as a second independent reality to be possessed?

### The grammatical problem

Sanskrit uses the genitive case, **षष्ठी विभक्ति - ṣaṣṭhī vibhakti**, for relationships such as possession.

*Ṣaṣṭhī* literally means sixth.

The genitive is traditionally the sixth case in the Sanskrit grammatical ordering.

Now the philosophical problem.

Possessiveness ordinarily implies a relational distinction.

The possessor is one thing.

The possessed is another.

But the supreme consciousness cannot be defined through ownership of an independently existing universe.

So Abhinavagupta interprets the language in a more radical way.

The universe belongs to Śiva not as a foreign object but as his own self-manifestation.

**The supreme “mine” does not require an independently existing “other.”**

### His extraordinary statement from a hymn

Abhinavagupta cites a verse of his own contrasting the helpless sense that nothing belongs to the individual with the recognition that the universal reality is one's own nature.

Its central declaration is:

**अस्मि सर्वम् - asmi sarvam**

"I am all."

Now the grammar.

**अस्मि - asmi**

I am.

From √*as*, to be.

First-person singular, present tense.

**सर्वम् - sarvam**

All, everything.

This is not the ordinary claim that your empirical personality is identical to every other personality.

It's the universal I-consciousness recognizing the totality as its own manifestation.

A limited person saying "I own the entire universe" is making an astonishingly bad property claim.

Universal consciousness recognizing all manifestation as its own nature is something quite different.

And now the word *mama* becomes philosophically explosive.

The apparent relation of possession can be reinterpreted through the identity of universal consciousness and the world.

### Sva-mama and the nested compound

Abhinavagupta explores additional esoteric segmentations involving **sva** and **mama**.

One possibility treats the expression in a way suggesting the absence of an independently separate "mine."

Another treats the sound sequence as containing elements associated with auspiciousness, knowledge and ascertainment.

He invokes the grammatical category:

**बहुव्रीहि - bahuvrīhi**

A possessive or exocentric compound.

Let's learn this properly.

The conventional example is:

**बहुव्रीहिः - bahuvrīhiḥ**

Literally, having much rice.

The word historically combines:

*bahu* - much.

*vrīhi* - rice.

But a bahuvrīhi compound typically denotes an entity characterized by the quality expressed by the compound, not merely the rice itself.

For example, a phrase meaning "one whose eyes are like lotuses" describes a person, not a collection of lotus-eyes.

And Abhinavagupta even discusses a **bahuvrīhi within a bahuvrīhi**.

The man has reached nested metaphysical compounds.

The Sanskrit equivalent of recursion.

We've gone from:

"Tell me a secret."

To:

"First, let us construct a recursive possessive compound explaining why the Absolute cannot possess the universe as something external to itself."

Completely reasonable.

But the philosophical result is important.

Even within ordinary relations of knowing and possessing, Śiva remains inseparable from Śakti.

The relative structures do not create a second ultimate reality.

### The most beautiful implication

Your everyday experience contains:

"I see."

"I think."

"I want."

"I remember."

But Trika asks you to distinguish the limited individual who appropriates those experiences from the fundamental awareness that makes them possible.

The sense of "mine" can reinforce contraction.

Yet the same grammatical relation, when interpreted through universal I-consciousness, can point beyond separation.

The words don't liberate automatically.

But their meanings can be made to disclose the structure of bondage and recognition.

And then Abhinavagupta finishes the first question with a remarkable theological observation.

Even the Goddess's act of addressing the Lord is, ultimately, consciousness addressing itself.

The entire dialogue remains an expression of the nondual reality.

The teacher and disciple are different within the manifestation.

But the foundation of their capacity to know and speak is one.

And at last-

**The second verse begins.** ; ; \]


## 7. The Goddess's second verse - Kaulinī, Mistress of the Heart

PARĀTRĪŚIKĀ - VERSE 2, AS ARRANGED IN ABHINAVAGUPTA'S COMMENTARY

## हृदयस्था तु या शक्तिः कौलिकी कुलनायिका।

## तां मे कथय देवेश येन तृप्तिं लभाम्यहम्॥२॥

*Hṛdayasthā tu yā śaktiḥ kaulikī kulanāyikā.*

*Tāṃ me kathaya deveśa yena tṛptiṃ labhāmy aham.*

**Literal translation:**

"Tell me, O Lord of the gods, about that Śakti who dwells in the Heart, Kaulikī, the Mistress of Kula, by which I may obtain complete satisfaction."

Notice that this is not merely:

"Where is the Goddess?"

Or:

"What is the correct mantra?"

She asks about the Power dwelling in the Heart.

The Power governing the totality.

And the attainment of **tṛpti**, fulfilment.

Abhinavagupta explicitly presents this verse as a gathering together of the questions raised in the preceding one and a half verses.

This is not an unrelated new topic.

It is a concentrated restatement of everything we've just been discussing.

### Every word, grammatically

| Sanskrit | Grammatical role | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **हृदयस्था - hṛdayasthā** | Feminine nominative singular compound | Dwelling in the Heart |
| **तु - tu** | Particle | But, now, indeed |
| **या - yā** | Feminine relative pronoun | Who, which |
| **शक्तिः - śaktiḥ** | Feminine nominative singular | Power |
| **कौलिकी - kaulikī** | Feminine nominative singular | Kaulikī |
| **कुलनायिका - kulanāyikā** | Feminine nominative singular | Mistress of Kula |
| **ताम् - tām** | Feminine accusative singular | Her, that one |
| **मे - me** | Enclitic pronoun | To me, for me |
| **कथय - kathaya** | Imperative, second-person singular | Tell, explain |
| **देवेश - deveśa** | Masculine vocative singular | O Lord of gods |
| **येन - yena** | Instrumental relative pronoun | By which |
| **तृप्तिम् - tṛptim** | Feminine accusative singular | Satisfaction |
| **लभामि - labhāmi** | Present, first-person singular | I obtain |
| **अहम् - aham** | First-person pronoun | I |

And here's something beautifully grammatical.

The four expressions **hṛdayasthā**, **yā śaktiḥ**, **kaulikī**, and **kulanāyikā** all describe the same feminine subject.

The Goddess isn't introducing four separate women.

She's identifying one Śakti through four different descriptions.

Meanwhile **tām** is the accusative pronoun referring back to that Śakti.

So the grammatical structure is:

**She who is the Power dwelling in the Heart, Kaulikī, Mistress of Kula - tell me about her.**

That distinction between nominative and accusative is something you should start noticing whenever you read Sanskrit verses.

The endings are doing the syntactic work.


## 8. हृदयस्था - Hṛdayasthā: Where does she actually live?

Ordinary analysis first.

**हृदय - hṛdaya**

Heart.

**स्था - sthā**

Standing, abiding, dwelling; in this compound, a feminine form associated with √*sthā*, to stand or remain.

So:

**हृदयस्था - hṛdayasthā**

She who dwells in the Heart.

Now look at the writing.

# हृदयस्था

hṛ-da-ya-sthā

हृ

द

य

स्था

The final cluster:

**स्था = स् + थ + ा**

Notice the aspirated dental **थ - tha**.

Not the unaspirated **त - ta**.

And the long-*ā* vowel sign belongs to the joined consonantal syllable.

Now here's the Abhinavaguptan madness.

He refuses to leave *hṛdayasthā* as a straightforward compound.

He divides it differently.

### Hṛt + Aya + Sthā

**हृत् + अया + स्था**

*Hṛt + ayā + sthā.*

This is an esoteric interpretive segmentation, not the standard morphological analysis of *hṛdayasthā*.

First:

**हृत् - hṛt**

Heart, interpreted here as supreme consciousness.

Abhinavagupta defines this Heart as the ultimate foundation of both outwardly presented and inwardly experienced phenomena.

Colour.

Pleasure.

Body.

Breath.

Intellect.

And everything else.

The Heart is not a hidden object situated somewhere among those experiences.

It is the conscious ground of their manifestation.

Now comes:

**अया - ayā**

Abhinavagupta relates this expression to manifold cognitions produced through the freedom of consciousness.

Examples include cognitions of pots and other objects.

And later he invokes Somānanda's explanation of *aya* in terms of going or knowledge.

The Sanskrit statement he quotes is:

**हृदि अयो गमनं ज्ञानम्।**

*Hṛdi ayo gamanaṃ jñānam.*

In the Heart, *aya* is interpreted as movement, namely knowledge.

Don't treat this as a universal dictionary rule that *aya* always means knowledge.

It's a contextual explanatory derivation.

Now the beautiful idea.

The Heart is universal consciousness.

The movements of knowledge arise within it.

And **Śakti is that which abides in this Heart and manifests these movements**.

She isn't sitting in your chest like a miniature deity occupying a tiny celestial bedsit.

She is the power by which the Heart manifests cognition.

The ordinary subject knows a pot.

A colour.

A pleasure.

A memory.

Yet none of these cognitions independently illuminates itself outside consciousness.

This is why the Goddess can be called *hṛdayasthā*.

The Heart is the ground.

Her power is its living manifestation.

### Sphuraṇa - स्फुरण

Abhinavagupta describes her as:

**स्फुरणमयी - sphuraṇamayī**

Consisting of throbbing manifestation, pulsation or vibrant appearing.

Break it down:

**स्फुरण - sphuraṇa**

Flashing, throbbing, vibrating, becoming manifest.

Related to √*sphur*, to quiver, flash or manifest.

And:

**-मयी - -mayī**

A feminine formation meaning composed of, consisting of, or pervaded by.

So *sphuraṇamayī* refers to a Power characterized by living manifestation.

Look at the initial consonants:

**स् + फ = स्फ**

*s + pha*

The **फ - pha** is aspirated.

In Sanskrit, *pha* is not inherently the English *f*, although modern pronunciations sometimes approximate it.

Now add:

**रण - raṇa**

And we get *sphuraṇa*.

The metaphysical point is that consciousness isn't an inert background.

Its very nature includes the capacity to manifest.

And Śakti is the name for this expressive freedom.

If Śiva were merely a blank, unconscious substance incapable of manifesting anything, we would have a serious problem explaining the actual presence of experience.

Abhinavagupta's answer is that **prakāśa**, luminosity, and **vimarśa**, reflexive self-awareness, are inseparable.

The Heart is not dead.

It pulses with its own capacity for manifestation.

And the Goddess is that power.

; \]


## 9. कुलनायिका - Kulanāyikā: Mistress of the entire manifestation

Now the second title.

**कुलनायिका - kulanāyikā**

Let's dissect it properly.

**कुल - kula**

Family, collection, totality.

**नायिका - nāyikā**

Female leader, mistress, guide, heroine.

Related to the verbal root √*nī*, to lead or conduct.

A *nāyaka* is a leader or hero.

A *nāyikā* is the corresponding feminine form.

So:

**कुलनायिका - kulanāyikā**

The Mistress or Leader of Kula.

And Abhinavagupta explains exactly why she deserves that name.

She gives manifest vitality to the body, breath and experiences of pleasure.

She is the operative power of the circle of deities headed by Brāhmī.

She is the innermost centre of the network of senses and subtle channels.

And he even relates her to the generative symbolism of the female and male sexual organs.

Now this is not an accidental piece of erotic decoration.

It's part of the Kaula doctrine of manifestation through Śiva-Śakti union.

### The circle of deities

**देवताचक्र - devatācakra**

- **devatā** - deity, divine power.
- **cakra** - circle, wheel, organized collection.

The goddess powers have differentiated functions.

But their operative vitality isn't independent of the supreme Śakti.

This is why Abhinavagupta describes Kaulikī in terms of their **vīrya**, potency.

**वीर्य - vīrya**

Energy, vigour, potency, strength, and in some contexts semen.

Don't flatten that word into only one English meaning.

Its precise significance depends on whether we're discussing spiritual power, ritual symbolism, embodiment or sexual generation.

Here it participates in a complex metaphysical and generative symbolism.

### The centre of centres

He describes her in relation to the whole circle of sensory organs and subtle channels as the innermost centre.

**अक्ष - akṣa**

Among its meanings, a sense organ.

**नाडी - nāḍī**

A subtle channel, tube or conduit.

**चक्र - cakra**

Circle, wheel or organized system.

And:

**मध्य - madhya**

Middle, centre.

The repetition of *madhya* emphasizes her centrality within the centre.

Now think about it.

Your sensory faculties are differentiated.

Vision isn't hearing.

Hearing isn't touch.

The senses appear to operate through distinct organs and processes.

But for Trika, their manifestation as conscious experience depends on the same underlying awareness.

Kaulikī is called their centre because their living manifestation is grounded in her power.

She's not another individual sense organ competing for attention.

She's the power through which the entire sensory complex manifests.

### The generative symbolism

Abhinavagupta also identifies her through the terms:

**कर्णिका - karṇikā**

The central portion of a flower, or in this context part of the symbolism associated with the female generative organ.

**लिङ्ग - liṅga**

Mark, sign, emblem, and in this context the male generative organ.

The passage connects these with the place of generation.

This is explicitly embodied and sexual imagery.

The feminine and masculine generative principles symbolize the capacity through which manifestation arises.

And in Kaula metaphysics, that symbolism extends beyond merely biological reproduction.

The coming together of Śiva and Śakti represents the inseparable relationship of luminous consciousness and its power of manifestation.

The physical symbols aren't irrelevant.

But the metaphysical doctrine isn't exhausted by their physical form.

You can understand the symbols without assuming that performing a particular sexual act automatically yields enlightenment.

**The generative power of consciousness is the ultimate point.**

And now we reach Kaulikī herself.


## 10. कौलिकी - Kaulikī: She belongs to Kula without being imprisoned by it

Now we need to be very precise.

The commentary's principal reading is:

**कौलिकी - kaulikī**

Some other recensions of the root text have:

**कौलिनी - kaulinī**

Both forms are important to textual history, but they should not be silently substituted for one another.

*Kaulikī* is the feminine form related to *kaulika*, that which pertains to Kula or Kaula.

*Kaulinī* belongs to a related derivative formation and can be understood as a feminine designation connected with the Kaula family or tradition.

The philosophical focus here is the power underlying *kula*.

And Abhinavagupta gives two distinct interpretations.

### First interpretation: She is in Kula, yet has the nature of Akula

He writes, in effect:

**कुले भवा अकुलरूपा कौलिकी।**

*Kule bhavā akularūpā kaulikī.*

Kaulikī is she who is present within Kula while possessing the nature of Akula.

Now this is beautiful.

First:

**कुले - kule**

In Kula.

Locative singular.

Then:

**अकुल - akula**

The term *kula* negated by the prefix *a-*.

In the doctrinal contrast, Akula denotes the transcendent reality of Śiva, not reducible to the differentiated collective of manifestation.

Then:

**रूप - rūpa**

Form, nature, characteristic.

So:

**अकुलरूपा - akularūpā**

She whose nature is Akula.

Now the paradox.

Kaulikī manifests within Kula.

Yet she is not fundamentally limited by the differentiated totality.

She possesses the nature of the transcendent reality.

In other words:

**She is present as the whole manifestation without being exhausted by any particular manifested form.**

Now imagine somebody asking:

"Is the Goddess inside the universe or beyond the universe?"

Abhinavagupta's answer makes the ordinary opposition inadequate.

She isn't merely a finite object situated inside the universe.

But neither is she an external deity separated from it.

The universe is her manifestation.

And her reality is inseparable from the supreme consciousness.

Trying to force her into one side of the inside/outside distinction is like asking whether awareness is located on the left side or right side of the experience in which left and right become meaningful.

The question is badly framed.

### Second interpretation: She contains Kaula as her own identity

Abhinavagupta then introduces another analysis.

Here *Kaula* is understood through the relationship of Kula and Akula.

The reality of Akula is present in Kula, and that Kaula reality exists inseparably within her.

The crucial term is:

**तादात्म्य - tādātmya**

Identity.

Not a casual resemblance.

Not merely association.

But identity of nature.

The doctrine concerns the inseparability of consciousness and manifestation.

Kula cannot exist as an independent reality detached from Akula.

Its manifest nature is grounded in Akula's light.

And Śakti is the power through which that manifestation occurs.

Therefore Kaulikī is not a second substance mediating between two unrelated worlds.

She is the inseparable power of their unity.

### Sanskrit laboratory: Prakāśa

**प्रकाश - prakāśa**

Illumination, manifestation, luminosity.

Related to *pra-* and √*kāś*, to shine or appear.

Now compare:

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness.

The traditional distinction is not that Śiva is an unconscious light and Śakti is an unrelated consciousness added later.

Rather, their inseparability is fundamental.

Prakāśa without vimarśa would fail to capture the living self-revelation that Abhinavagupta regards as intrinsic to consciousness.

And Śakti without the luminous ground of Śiva would become an independent principle, violating the nondual ontology.

**Kula and Akula are distinguishable in the teaching, but not ultimately independent.**

This is what the two interpretations of Kaulikī are designed to disclose.


## 11. The Spanda connection - Even mantras depend upon the Self's power

Now Abhinavagupta quotes the Spanda tradition.

And this is a wonderful connection to the Śiva Sūtras you've already studied.

He invokes the power of the Self and then cites:

SPANDAKĀRIKĀ 2.1, QUOTED IN THE COMMENTARY

**तदाक्रम्य बलं मन्त्राः सर्वज्ञबलशालिनः।**

**प्रवर्तन्तेऽधिकाराय करणानीव देहिनाम्॥**

*Tad ākramya balaṃ mantrāḥ sarvajñabalaśālinaḥ.*

*Pravartante 'dhikārāya karaṇānīva dehinām.*

A close rendering:

Having taken hold of that power, mantras endowed with the power of omniscience proceed to their respective functions, just as the faculties of embodied beings do.

Now the philosophical claim.

The power of a mantra isn't interpreted as an independently existing magical object floating around outside consciousness.

Mantra operates through the power of the Self.

And the comparison with the senses is crucial.

Your eyes perform their function.

Your ears perform theirs.

But neither organ possesses an independently self-luminous awareness.

Similarly, the mantra powers depend upon the divine potency underlying their manifestation.

### The grammar

**बल - bala**

Strength, power, force.

**मन्त्राः - mantrāḥ**

Mantras, nominative plural.

Notice the ending **-āḥ**.

The singular is:

**मन्त्रः - mantraḥ**

The plural:

**मन्त्राः - mantrāḥ**

Now:

**प्रवर्तन्ते - pravartante**

They proceed, operate, become active.

From *pra-* and √*vṛt*, to turn, occur or function.

Third-person plural, present middle voice.

And:

**करणानि - karaṇāni**

Instruments or faculties.

Neuter nominative or accusative plural.

From *karaṇa*, instrument.

Abhinavagupta is showing that the faculties and mantras have differentiated operations while drawing their actual power from the same underlying conscious reality.

The senses do not illuminate themselves by themselves.

The mantras do not possess a supreme consciousness independent of Bhairava.

And Kaulikī is the operative Śakti through which these powers function.

Thus the relationship between the Goddess and the totality is not merely symbolic.

Within the system, it is the ontological explanation for their very manifestation.

; \]


## 12. तां मे कथय - Tell me about her

And now Abhinavagupta returns to the ordinary words of the verse.

**तां मे कथय - tāṃ me kathaya**

Tell me about her.

The pronoun **tām** refers back to the feminine Śakti.

But the commentary records an important textual alternative.

Somānanda reads:

**तन्मे कथय - tan me kathaya**

And this can be interpreted differently.

Rather than "tell me about her," it may mean "therefore, tell me," or "tell me that," depending on the grammatical and interpretive treatment.

This is not trivial.

It changes the precise relation between the question and its object.

Let's understand the difference.

## तां मे

*tāṃ me*

About her, to me

## तन्मे

*tan me*

That / therefore, to me

The first has **ताम् - tām**, feminine accusative.

The second involves **तत् - tat**, neuter demonstrative, with a sandhi form before *me*.

And Abhinavagupta actually records Somānanda's variant.

This is precisely why we cannot pretend that every Sanskrit manuscript presents one perfectly identical text.

The tradition itself was aware of variant readings and commented upon them.

Now another word:

**देवेश - deveśa**

O Lord of gods.

Break it into:

*deva + īśa*

Ordinary vowel sandhi gives:

**a + ī → e**

So:

**deva + īśa → deveśa**

Another little rule you've now learned.

The vowel *e* here isn't an arbitrary substitution.

It results from the combination of two vowels.

And *deveśa* is vocative singular.

The Goddess is addressing Bhairava.

Abhinavagupta glosses him as the Lord of gods such as Brahmā, Viṣṇu and Rudra.

But remember the deeper interpretation established earlier.

Śakti is not ultimately separate from the Śiva whom she addresses.

The dialogue is one consciousness manifesting a relationship through which its own nature is disclosed.

Again, the language becomes an expression of the metaphysics.


## 13. तृप्ति - Tṛpti: What does the Goddess actually want?

Now we reach the final purpose of her question.

**तृप्तिं लभाम्यहम् - tṛptiṃ labhāmy aham**

I obtain satisfaction.

And this is not ordinary satisfaction in the sense of having eaten an excellent meal.

Although, as you now know, the meal can certainly become an occasion for contemplating consciousness.

### Tṛpti

**तृप्ति - tṛpti**

Satisfaction, satiation, fulfilment.

From √**tṛp**, to be satisfied or satiated.

Now watch the written form.

**तृ = त + ृ**

The vowel *ṛ* is attached below त.

Then:

**प् + त = प्त**

A conjunct cluster.

Then the vowel *i* follows the t.

Thus:

**तृप्ति - tṛpti**

And with the accusative singular ending:

**तृप्तिम् - tṛptim**

The Goddess asks to obtain fulfilment.

Now what does Abhinavagupta say that fulfilment means?

He interprets it as supreme bliss and the savour of freedom associated with ultimate nondual repose.

The important terms include:

**परमानन्द - paramānanda**

Supreme bliss.

**निर्वृति - nirvṛti**

Satisfaction, repose, completion.

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Freedom, independence.

**रस - rasa**

Essence, sap, juice, savour, aesthetic flavour.

This last word is particularly beautiful.

Because *rasa* isn't merely something intellectually understood.

It suggests something savoured.

And Abhinavagupta's philosophical language repeatedly connects consciousness with experiential fullness and wonder.

The Goddess wants more than a definition.

She wants the fulfilment of recognizing her own real nature.

### The second verbal reading

The commentary records:

**व्रजामि - vrajāmi**

I go, proceed, attain.

As an alternative to:

**लभामि - labhāmi**

I obtain.

The meanings differ slightly, but both can express the attainment of satisfaction in this context.

The important thing is that Abhinavagupta notes the variant.

One reading emphasizes obtaining the fulfilment.

Another expresses coming into or attaining that state.

Neither is a request for a new material possession.

### Labhāmy aham - the sandhi

Now learn this one:

**लभामि + अहम्**

*labhāmi + aham*

The final *i* before the following vowel *a* becomes the corresponding glide *y* in external sandhi:

**लभाम्यहम् - labhāmy aham**

Thus:

*i + a → ya*

This is another extremely common Sanskrit sandhi pattern.

And it's particularly worth recognizing because it appears repeatedly in connected verse.

You can read the original and reverse the sandhi:

*labhāmy aham* → *labhāmi aham*.

Now the final word:

**अहम् - aham**

I.

And Abhinavagupta refuses to treat this merely as the personal ego saying:

"I want to feel better."

He interprets *aham* through the universal consciousness that is the life of all cognizing subjects.

This is the same great "I" we've encountered in the phonemic symbolism.

The fulfilment is therefore the recognition of one's own lordly nature.

Not that the individual ego becomes a supreme monarch.

But that the finite experiencer recognizes the universal consciousness underlying its own existence.

And because that consciousness is fundamentally complete, the fulfilment is expressed as **paripūrṇabhāva**.

### Paripūrṇa - परिपूर्ण

Completely full.

Break it down:

**परि - pari**

Around, entirely, completely.

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa**

Full, complete.

Related to √*pṝ*, to fill.

Together:

**परिपूर्ण - paripūrṇa**

Completely fulfilled, perfectly full.

Now that's the real *tṛpti*.

Not satisfaction because you've finally acquired enough objects.

But fullness in which the fundamental assumption of metaphysical incompleteness has been overturned.

And this takes us directly back to the three *malas*.

Remember the fundamental contraction of āṇava mala?

The sense of incompleteness.

"I am merely this limited being."

"I lack."

"I must become something else."

Now the Goddess asks for fulfilment through recognition.

And Abhinavagupta interprets that fulfilment as the establishment of one's own real nature.

**The very incompleteness that perpetuates bondage is answered by recognition of the fullness of consciousness.**

The Goddess's request has become a complete statement of the Trika soteriological project.

; ; \]


## 14. Abhinavagupta's own final verse - Tear open the knot of the Heart

And now the commentary reaches one of its most striking moments.

Abhinavagupta composes a closing stanza for this long exposition of the Goddess's questions.

He speaks of those whose minds thirst to drink the essence of Śiva, and of cutting apart the knot in the sky of the Heart.

The concluding Sanskrit reads:

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S CONCLUDING STANZA, FINAL HALF

**हृदयगगनग्रन्थिं तेषां विदारयितुं हठाद्।**

**अभिनव इमां प्रश्नव्याख्यां व्यधात्त्रिकतत्त्वगाम्॥**

*Hṛdayagaganagranthiṃ teṣāṃ vidārayituṃ haṭhād.*

*Abhinava imāṃ praśnavyākhyāṃ vyadhāt trikatattvagām.*

The sense is that Abhinavagupta has composed this exposition of the Goddess's questions, concerning the truth of Trika, to forcefully cut apart the knot in the sky of the Heart.

And what a description.

After all the philosophical analysis, all the grammar, all the extraordinary interpretations of phonemes, he tells you what he's actually trying to do.

**Untie the knot.**

Not entertain you with sophisticated metaphysical terminology.

Not win a competition for the most terrifying Sanskrit compound.

Not establish an academic system that can be discussed indefinitely without changing anything about how you understand experience.

He wants the teaching to penetrate the very structure of misrecognition.

### The Sanskrit: Hṛdaya-gagana-granthi

Let's break this magnificent compound down.

**हृदय - hṛdaya**

Heart.

**गगन - gagana**

Sky, space, firmament.

**ग्रन्थि - granthi**

Knot.

Thus:

**हृदयगगनग्रन्थि - hṛdayagaganagranthi**

The knot in the sky of the Heart.

Now think about that image.

The Heart is compared with the sky.

Open.

Vast.

Unconfined.

And yet within that very openness appears a knot.

The knot is not a rope tied around the physical organ.

It's the obstruction of recognition.

The contraction through which universal consciousness appears as limited subjectivity.

And Abhinavagupta's philosophical exposition is intended to cut it apart.

### Vidārayitum

**विदारयितुम् - vidārayitum**

To split, tear apart, rend.

The infinitive of the causative verb *vidārayati*, related to *vi-* and √*dṝ*, to split.

Now this is wonderfully forceful language.

Not:

"Gently consider loosening the knot when convenient."

No.

He wants to tear the bastard open.

The image conveys the force of liberating understanding.

The insight is not merely an additional belief.

It removes the basis of a mistaken identification.

And then:

**व्यधात् - vyadhāt**

He made, composed, established.

From *vi-* and √*dhā*, to place or put.

Abhinavagupta has composed this exposition for the sake of that recognition.

That's his declared purpose.

And it's a fitting conclusion to the first great movement of the commentary.




## 15. The doorway opens - Bhairava is finally going to answer

And now, at last:

**श्रीभैरव उवाच**

*Śrībhairava uvāca.*

The venerable Bhairava said.

After the Goddess's questions and Abhinavagupta's enormous philosophical analysis, we enter the formal reply.

And the next verse begins:

**शृणु देवि महाभागे उत्तरस्याप्यनुत्तरम्।**

*Śṛṇu devi mahābhāge uttarasyāpy anuttaram.*

"Listen, O greatly fortunate Goddess, to that which is unsurpassable even beyond the higher."

Now there's a delicious little grammatical connection.

**शृणु - śṛṇu**

Listen!

Second-person singular imperative of √*śru*, to hear.

And:

**उत्तरस्य - uttarasya**

Of the higher, of what is further.

Genitive singular.

Then:

**अपि - api**

Even, also.

And:

**अनुत्तरम् - anuttaram**

The unsurpassable.

There's sandhi here:

**uttarasya + api → uttarasyāpi**

The final *a* combines with the following *a* to give long ā.

So when you read:

**उत्तरस्याप्यनुत्तरम्**

You should recover:

*uttarasya api anuttaram*.

The text is preparing us for the next revelation.

And this time Bhairava is going to introduce a method residing in the space of his Heart.

**हृद्व्योमन् - hṛdvyoman**

The sky or space of the Heart.

And the phonemic system of the Tantra is about to move to the centre of the stage.

Which means we're approaching the doctrine in which vowels, consonants, the lunar digits, solar powers, tattvas and mantra are understood as manifestations of consciousness.

The language of the universe itself.

Exactly the territory the Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa is famous for.

But that is where Part V begins.

| **तृप्ति - tṛpti** | Fulfilment, satisfaction |
| **परिपूर्ण - paripūrṇa** | Complete fullness |
| **हृदयगगनग्रन्थि - hṛdayagaganagranthi** | The knot in the sky of the Heart |

And I think the most important insight is this:

**The Great Secret isn't hidden because consciousness has concealed itself behind an inaccessible object. It is hidden because the limited subject mistakes the very manifestations of consciousness for something independent of their source.**

The world appears.

Thoughts appear.

Sensations arise.

The distinction between "I" and "this" becomes established.

And universal consciousness is apprehended only through its contracted forms.

The Goddess asks for the Power dwelling in the Heart because that Power is what makes all these manifestations possible.

Kaulikī is not somewhere else.

She is the very power of their manifestation.

And recognition is the transformation by which their underlying nonduality becomes apparent.

This is why Abhinavagupta can discuss the senses, pleasure, mantras, cognition, the Heart, the universe and liberation within the same philosophical argument.

They're not unrelated topics.

They're different aspects of the single reality of Śiva and Śakti.

And the apparent knot connecting them is exactly what he wants to undo.


## Your Sanskrit writing practice

Try these three, progressively.

# हृदयस्था

hṛdayasthā - she who dwells in the Heart


**हृ + द + य + स्था**

Pay attention to the vocalic ṛ and the final *sthā* conjunct.


# कुलनायिका

kulanāyikā - Mistress of Kula


**कुल + नायिका**

Notice the long ā in *nā* and *kā*, and the *i* sign attached to य in *yi*.


# हृदयगगनग्रन्थि

hṛdayagaganagranthi - knot in the sky of the Heart


**हृदय + गगन + ग्रन्थि**

Notice the two conjuncts in *granthi*: ग्र and न्थ.

And one especially useful sandhi pattern to remember:

**देव + ईश = देवेश**

*deva + īśa → deveśa*

That's the same vowel combination you'll meet in many compounds expressing a lord or ruler, and it will make future scripture much easier to read.
