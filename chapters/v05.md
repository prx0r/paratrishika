# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART V - Bhairava Speaks: The Secret of the Heart-Space

*Abhinavagupta discovers that Sanskrit grammar has been quietly explaining the entire universe, that your pronouns are doing metaphysical gymnastics, and that Bhairava's ultimate secret is hidden in the very act of speaking.*

Abhinavagupta's commentary on Parātrīśikā 3-4 · Kashmir Series of Texts and Studies, pp. 63-97


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

The Goddess has finally finished asking her question.

And what a question.

What is Anuttara? How does it grant immediate Kaulika perfection? What is the secret Power residing in the Heart? How does recognition lead to the fullness of Khecarī?

Abhinavagupta has already spent an outrageous amount of time demonstrating that the Supreme is neither a higher object, nor a distant destination, nor a particular mental state you acquire after sufficiently aggressive meditation.

We've met Kaulinī, the Mistress of Kula.

We've untied sixteen interpretations of the Heart-knot.

And we've established that consciousness doesn't somehow cease being consciousness when it manifests a world full of apparently separate things.

Now Bhairava is finally going to answer.

And naturally, instead of immediately explaining the method, Abhinavagupta stops to discuss what the word *Bhairava* means.

Because of course he bloody does.

The poor Goddess has waited through an entire treatise, and we're about to interrogate the name of the man answering her.

Except this isn't an irrelevant digression.

The name *Bhairava*, the distinction between question and answer, and the relationship between the three Sanskrit grammatical persons are about to become one continuous philosophical argument.

The structure is extraordinary:

**Speech unfolds the universe. Grammar expresses distinctions within that unfolding. And the ultimate speaker is the consciousness in which all these distinctions arise.**

Right.

The Lord has the floor.


## 1. The verses - Bhairava's answer begins

PARĀTRĪŚIKĀ, VERSES 3-4

## शृणु देवि महाभागे उत्तरस्याप्यनुत्तरम्॥३॥

*Śṛṇu devi mahābhāge uttarasyāpy anuttaram.*


## कौलिकोऽयं विधिर्देवि मम हृद्व्योम्न्यवस्थितः।

## कथयामि सुरेशानि सद्यः कौलिकसिद्धिदम्॥४॥

*Kauliko 'yaṃ vidhir devi mama hṛdvyomny avasthitaḥ.*

*Kathayāmi sureśāni sadyaḥ kaulikasiddhidam.*

### Close translation

"Listen, O greatly blessed Goddess, to that which is unsurpassable even in relation to what is higher.

"This Kaulika method, O Goddess, is established in the space of my Heart. I shall explain, O Mistress of the gods, that which immediately bestows Kaulika perfection."



Now the tempting thing is to say:

Fantastic.

Bhairava has an extraordinary method hidden in the Heart.

Presumably a mantra, a ritual, or some secret yogic technique.

Let's learn the technique.

But Abhinavagupta refuses to let you assume that *vidhi*, method, necessarily means an external procedure.

Because even the distinction between the speaker and the listener must first be understood.

So, before we begin, learn the grammatical skeleton.

| Sanskrit | Grammar | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **शृणु - śṛṇu** | Second-person singular imperative | Listen! |
| **देवि - devi** | Feminine vocative singular | O Goddess |
| **महाभागे - mahābhāge** | Feminine vocative singular | O greatly blessed one |
| **उत्तरस्य - uttarasya** | Genitive singular | Of the higher, or of the subsequent |
| **अपि - api** | Particle | Even, also |
| **अनुत्तरम् - anuttaram** | Neuter accusative singular | The unsurpassable |
| **कौलिकः - kaulikaḥ** | Masculine nominative singular | Kaulika |
| **अयम् - ayam** | Masculine nominative singular | This |
| **विधिः - vidhiḥ** | Masculine nominative singular | Method, procedure, ordinance |
| **मम - mama** | First-person genitive singular | My, of me |
| **हृद्व्योम्नि - hṛdvyomni** | Locative singular compound | In the Heart-space |
| **अवस्थितः - avasthitaḥ** | Masculine nominative singular | Established, situated |
| **कथयामि - kathayāmi** | First-person singular present | I explain, I tell |
| **सुरेशानि - sureśāni** | Feminine vocative singular | O Mistress of the gods |
| **सद्यः - sadyaḥ** | Adverb | Immediately |
| **कौलिकसिद्धिदम् - kaulikasiddhidam** | Neuter accusative singular compound | Bestowing Kaulika perfection |

Notice the grammar of verse four.

**Kaulikaḥ**, **ayam**, **vidhiḥ**, and **avasthitaḥ** all agree in masculine nominative singular.

They're describing the method.

The final *kaulikasiddhidam*, however, is a neuter compound in the accusative. Abhinavagupta's interpretation relates it back to the unsurpassable reality being explained.

The sentence therefore doesn't need to be flattened into a list of disconnected English words.

Its inflections are establishing the relationships.

And we'll see why that matters very shortly.


## 2. Śrībhairava uvāca - What does Bhairava mean?

The text introduces the response:

**श्रीभैरव उवाच**

*Śrībhairava uvāca.*

The venerable Bhairava said.

Abhinavagupta immediately remarks that he has already explained the name earlier. There is no need to repeat everything.

Which is a hilarious display of restraint from a man who has spent dozens of pages on the Goddess's first question.

But he briefly recalls two fundamental interpretations.

### First: Bhairava sustains the universe

The relevant term is:

**भरण - bharaṇa**

Supporting, sustaining, nourishing, bearing.

From √**bhṛ**, to bear, support or carry.

Now look at the root.

**भृ - bhṛ**

This is भ with the vowel sign **ृ**, representing vocalic *ṛ*.

Compare:

**भर - bhara**

A form built with the ordinary *a* vowel.

And:

**भरण - bharaṇa**

Supporting or sustaining.

The difference between *bhṛ* and *bhara* isn't arbitrary. Sanskrit roots undergo vowel changes in derivation.

For example, the present-tense verb:

**बिभर्ति - bibharti**

He or it bears, supports, sustains.

Also belongs to √*bhṛ*.

And Abhinavagupta's interpretation concerns the sustaining of the universe.

But not in the crude sense of a deity physically holding up the planet like an overworked celestial weightlifter.

Bhairava sustains all manifestations **within his own consciousness**.

That's the central claim.

Imagine a world appearing within awareness.

Colours.

Sounds.

Thoughts.

Memories.

The distinction between yourself and other beings.

The entire manifest field.

Nothing within that field is independently self-luminous in the sense that universal consciousness is.

Its manifestation rests upon consciousness.

The name Bhairava thus suggests the one who sustains the entire universe in his own being.

### Second: Bhairava as the great roar of mantra

Now he connects Bhairava with:

**महामन्त्ररव - mahāmantrarava**

The great resonance or roar of mantra.

Break it apart.

- **mahā** - great.
- **mantra** - sacred utterance, formula, instrument of contemplation.
- **rava** - sound, roar, noise, resonance.

The verbal root √*ru*, to cry or sound, is associated with *rava*.

This is an esoteric explanation of the divine name, not a standard historical etymology demonstrating that *bhairava* originated as an abbreviation of the compound.

The philosophical meaning is that Bhairava is the ultimate reality expressed through the supreme power of mantra.

Not merely a deity who happens to enjoy chanting.

He is the consciousness whose expressive freedom is the ground of all sacred speech.

Now here's the subtle bit.

Bhairava is associated with **saṃhāra**, reabsorption or withdrawal.

And in this context Abhinavagupta emphasizes the predominance of **śaktimat**, the possessor of Śakti, over Śakti's outwardly differentiated expansion.

### Śakti versus Śaktimat

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power, capacity, divine energy.

**शक्तिमत् - śaktimat**

Possessing power.

The suffix **-mat** expresses possession.

Just as:

**श्रीमत् - śrīmat**

Possessing splendour or prosperity.

The two-power and possessor of power-are conceptually distinguishable.

But they are not ultimately separable substances.

When Śakti expands as differentiated manifestation, we can speak of her expressive prominence.

When those manifestations are gathered back into undivided unity, we can speak of the predominance of Śaktimat.

But neither movement produces a genuine separation between Śiva and Śakti.

And Abhinavagupta connects the process of reabsorption with the symbolic phonemic sequence:

**म - ह - अ**

*ma - ha - a*

The manifest individual and the powers of emission return into the unsurpassable *a*.

This is precisely the symbolic reversal encountered in Part IV.

Again, ordinary Sanskrit grammar does not generate this cosmology automatically.

This is a deliberately Tantric interpretation of phonemes.

So the Lord who speaks is the same consciousness whose power manifests speech.

And the apparent reply will be the reabsorption of the question into recognition of its ground.

That's an extraordinary beginning.


## 3. The seven verses explaining why consciousness asks itself a question

Now Abhinavagupta does something rather beautiful.

Before commenting on *śṛṇu*, he composes a seven-stanza summary of the relationship between the Goddess's question and Bhairava's answer.

These are **Abhinavagupta's explanatory verses**, not additional verses of the Parātrīśikā Tantra.

Their sequence is important, so let's follow the argument.

### First movement - Parā expands through her own nature

The Goddess is supreme consciousness itself, manifesting through her own power.

She is the supreme power of will, **parecchāśakti**, inseparable from Bhairava.

The Sanskrit components:

**परा - parā**

Supreme, transcendent.

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will, desire, intention.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

Together:

**परेच्छाशक्ति - parecchāśakti**

Supreme will-power.

Notice the vowel sandhi.

**parā + icchā → parecchā**

The combination **ā + i** gives **e**.

So *parecchā* is not missing letters.

The two vowels have fused according to sandhi.

Now imagine consciousness possessing the freedom to manifest.

It isn't compelled by an external cause.

Nor does it create because it is fundamentally deficient.

Its creative power belongs to its very nature.

### Second movement - Will unfolds into knowledge, action and speech

The supreme Śakti's expansive nature includes her manifestation as knowledge and action.

And Abhinavagupta connects this with differentiated forms such as Parāparā, Aparā and the levels beginning with Paśyantī.

The underlying structure is:

**इच्छा - icchā**: will.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**: knowledge.

**क्रिया - kriyā**: activity.

And speech increasingly differentiated into recognizable forms.

In Parā, the expressive power is not yet divided into an independently existing speaker, spoken word and object.

In Paśyantī, the potential for differentiation begins to become manifest.

In Madhyamā, the distinction of signifier and signified becomes mentally articulated.

In Vaikharī, speech appears in outward phonetic expression.

These are related manifestations of one power, not four independent machines.

### Third movement - The Goddess's question is the expansion itself

Now comes the twist.

The question isn't ultimately a separate entity called "the Goddess" requesting information from a separate entity called "Bhairava."

The Goddess's question symbolizes **the expansion and self-apprehension of consciousness as differentiated manifestation**.

In other words, the very emergence of a question already requires distinctions.

The questioner.

The questioned.

The anticipated answer.

The act of asking.

All are articulations of consciousness.

That differentiating movement is Śakti.

And Abhinavagupta calls her the maker of the question because the question is constituted by her own power.

### Fourth movement - The answer is consciousness recognizing its own expansion

The answer is the reflexive apprehension of what has unfolded.

The power manifests differentiation.

Then that manifested differentiation is gathered into recognition of its own ground.

But note the subtlety.

This doesn't mean consciousness was previously ignorant in the manner of a confused human being and suddenly obtained an answer it didn't know.

It means that universal consciousness possesses the freedom to manifest the relationship of question and answer.

The apparent ignorance and the revelation both occur within that freedom.

### Fifth movement - The return from differentiated consciousness into supreme bliss

The answer is described through an inward movement from a more differentiated condition toward the dense bliss of supreme consciousness.

The term to remember is:

**संहार - saṃhāra**

Reabsorption, withdrawal, gathering back.

The prefix *saṃ-* carries a sense of coming together.

The verbal element is connected with √*hṛ*, to take or carry.

So *saṃhāra* is a gathering-in.

Within this teaching, it signifies the return of differentiated manifestations to recognition of their own undivided basis.

### Sixth movement - Bhairava manifests as the answering speaker

The Bhairava-form incorporates the entirety of the manifested expansion within itself.

And it appears as the respondent.

Now the Goddess is the questioning power.

Bhairava is the answering power.

Yet both express the same ultimate reality.

So the apparent dialogue is the Absolute's own movement of manifestation and recognition.

Imagine an immense ocean forming a wave that asks what water is.

Then the ocean answers through another wave.

The two waves have different functions in the story.

But neither has escaped the reality of water.

That's the basic analogy.

Except Trika attributes to supreme consciousness a reflexive freedom that a literal body of water does not possess.

### Seventh movement - Question and answer are not separated by ultimate time

Finally, Abhinavagupta makes the astonishing claim that expansion and reabsorption are not ultimately constituted by ordinary temporal succession.

From the standpoint of differentiated experience, the question comes before the answer.

From the standpoint of supreme consciousness, both belong to one undivided reality.

The apparent difference is meaningful within manifestation.

But the ultimate identity is not produced at the end of the sequence.

**The answer is not a second reality added to the question. It is the same consciousness recognized in its undivided nature.**

And Abhinavagupta says that his teachers had previously expressed this essential doctrine of the **Ṣaḍardha**, the sixfold Trika revelation.

This is the inheritance he is unfolding.

Not a private philosophical invention detached from the lineage.

The teaching comes through the gurus.

But its content concerns the consciousness in which guru, disciple and teaching all appear.

### An important textual aside

At this point the Sanskrit witnesses also preserve an Apabhraṃśa passage associated with the teachers' explanation. It develops the relationship between expansion, Bhairava and the reversal of that movement. One commonly circulated GRETIL text explicitly lacks the Apabhraṃśa passages, while the annotated Kashmir Series transcription preserves them in this location.

So we shouldn't pretend the surviving text is simply uninterrupted classical Sanskrit.

Abhinavagupta's world includes learned Sanskrit, earlier scriptural citations, and vernacular or literary Prakrit-family material.

The linguistic diversity is part of the historical text itself.

And immediately after this passage, Bhairava's first two verses are presented.

Now Abhinavagupta starts examining their individual words.


## 4. महाभागे - Mahābhāge: O Goddess of tremendous fortune

Now Abhinavagupta examines Bhairava's first proper address:

**महाभागे - mahābhāge**

O greatly blessed one.

And he isn't satisfied with that one meaning.

Naturally.

### The ordinary grammar

Start with:

**महत् - mahat**

Great, large, mighty.

Then:

**भाग - bhāga**

Share, portion, division, fortune.

The compound:

**महाभागा - mahābhāgā**

She who possesses a great share or great fortune.

And the feminine vocative singular:

**महाभागे - mahābhāge**

O greatly blessed one!

Now notice the ending.

A feminine noun in long **-ā**, such as *devatā*, has vocative singular **devate**.

Similarly:

*mahābhāgā → mahābhāge*.

This is not because the Goddess has suddenly changed gender.

The case ending has changed because she's being directly addressed.

Compare:

| Grammatical case | Sanskrit | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Nominative | **महाभागा - mahābhāgā** | The greatly blessed Goddess |
| Accusative | **महाभागाम् - mahābhāgām** | The greatly blessed Goddess, as object |
| Vocative | **महाभागे - mahābhāge** | O greatly blessed Goddess! |

And because she's directly addressed, we use the vocative.

Now, why such a grand title?

Abhinavagupta develops several possibilities.

### First - She possesses great fortune

At the ordinary level, the compound is entirely understandable.

A *mahābhāgā* is someone exceptionally fortunate or blessed.

But that raises an interesting question.

What could the supreme Śakti possibly be fortunate to possess?

Does the Goddess have unusually successful investments?

A lovely property portfolio?

A very comfortable apartment in Kailāsa?

No.

Her greatness concerns her relation to supreme consciousness itself.

### Second - Worship of her bestows the Great Power

Abhinavagupta connects the word with:

**भज् - √bhaj**

To share, partake, worship, serve or honour.

This is important because *bhāga* and *bhaj* belong to a connected verbal family.

Now:

**भज्यमाना - bhajyamānā**

Being worshipped, served or honoured.

Break it down.

The root √*bhaj* undergoes a passive present participial formation.

The feminine ending makes it refer to the Goddess.

Thus, the Goddess as the one who is worshipped.

But the commentary gives this a specific significance.

She is worshipped through continued engagement with the teachings already delivered and those yet to come.

Not merely by setting up an image and performing gestures.

The contemplation, assimilation and practice of the teaching constitute forms of service.

And she grants the supreme divine power.

So here's the delicious implication.

When a student investigates the nature of consciousness through authentic instruction, that intellectual and contemplative effort can itself be understood as worship of Śakti.

The Goddess isn't confined to a shrine.

She is the power through which understanding occurs.

Your grammar lesson has become devotional practice.

If you've been lovingly examining Devanāgarī consonant clusters for hours, congratulations: in this interpretive framework, even the business of understanding sacred language participates in the divine power of knowledge.

Though learning the conjunct **क्ष** does not, by itself, confer liberation.

Let's not get carried away.

### Third - The supreme Śiva is her great portion

Now Abhinavagupta interprets *mahān bhāgaḥ*, the great portion, in relation to **Anāśritaśiva**.

This is a particularly subtle designation.

**अनाश्रित - anāśrita**

Not dependent upon, not resting upon another.

It comes from the negative prefix *an-* and *āśrita*, a past participial form associated with √*śri*, to resort to, rely upon or take refuge in.

So the term indicates what does not depend on an external support.

In the present context, it expresses an exceedingly high divine level within the cosmological account.

The Goddess contains the supreme Śiva as her own inseparable reality.

This doesn't make Śiva a little piece of material hidden inside Śakti.

Nor does it establish Śakti as one substance larger than Śiva.

The division is conceptual and exegetical.

Their fundamental reality remains inseparable.

And Abhinavagupta connects this with the complete field of the thirty-six tattvas and the further levels of divine manifestation.

The Great Goddess isn't one more object inserted into the tattva hierarchy.

Her power is implicated in the manifestation of the hierarchy itself.

### Fourth - Even differentiated intelligence belongs to her

Now here's the extraordinary reversal.

Having discussed the supreme divine reality, Abhinavagupta interprets *mahābhāgā* through **buddhi**.

Yes.

The intellect.

The very faculty that makes distinctions.

The thing you're using to understand this commentary.

**बुद्धि - buddhi**

Intellect, discernment, determinative cognition.

From √**budh**, to awaken, perceive, understand.

Look at the Devanāgarī.

# बुद्धि

buddhi

बु

द्धि

द् + ध + ि forms the second syllabic unit.

This word teaches an important phonetic difference.

**द - da**

Unaspirated dental.

**ध - dha**

Aspirated dental.

And the conjunct:

**द्ध - ddha**

A voiced dental stop followed by its aspirated counterpart.

Do not read it as two independent consonants separated by an inherent *a*.

It's *bud-dhi*, not *bu-da-dhi*.

Now the philosophy.

Buddhi distinguishes.

It determines one object as this rather than that.

It can recognize something as pleasant while excluding other possibilities.

For example:

"This is a blue flower."

The intellect identifies the flower.

It distinguishes blue from red.

Flower from stone.

This from that.

All perfectly useful.

But Abhinavagupta asks whether the *power of determining* can be independent of consciousness.

No.

Even differentiated intelligence is a manifestation of Śakti.

### Enter Somānanda

He invokes Somānanda to distinguish the ordinary intellect from the intrinsic awareness of Śiva.

This is a serious philosophical argument.

An opponent might say:

"Knowledge requires buddhi. Buddhi is a product of Prakṛti. Therefore how could Śiva possess knowledge without depending upon the intellect?"

Now the answer is fascinating.

Ordinary determinate cognition does indeed involve the intellect.

But Śiva's fundamental awareness is not a mental modification produced by Prakṛti.

It is intrinsic to his nature.

**The intellect can differentiate knowledge; it does not manufacture the original luminosity of consciousness.**

This is one of those distinctions you should memorize.

Otherwise it's easy to confuse pure awareness with ordinary intellectual operations.

Abhinavagupta is not saying that your reasoning mind is completely useless.

He is saying that conscious manifestation cannot ultimately be explained by a mind treated as something independently self-illuminating.

The Goddess is therefore great not only because of her transcendent nature, but because the apparently lower faculties also belong to her expressive power.

### Fifth - Every limited pleasure is a portion of supreme bliss

Now we arrive at one of the most beautiful transitions.

Abhinavagupta explains that particular pleasures can be understood as limited manifestations of the supreme bliss associated with consciousness.

**आनन्द - ānanda**

Bliss, joy.

**महानन्द - mahānanda**

Great bliss.

**जगदानन्द - jagadānanda**

World-bliss, the bliss associated with the totality of manifestation.

Now be precise.

He is not saying that the psychological sensation of enjoying a mango is quantitatively identical to the full realization of Śiva.

Nor that every pleasant sensation automatically liberates you.

He is tracing the metaphysical dependence of limited pleasure upon the greater freedom and fullness of consciousness.

The differentiated pleasure is restricted.

It has an object.

A duration.

A particular intensity.

But consciousness itself is not fundamentally restricted by those divisions.

The commentary's transmitted annotations also expand this relation through several contemplative conditions of bliss; those explanatory additions shouldn't be confused with a fresh numbered list supplied by Abhinavagupta in the main prose.

Abhinavagupta invokes Bhaṭṭa Nārāyaṇa's image of the Lord as an ocean of bliss, of which all the joy found in the three worlds is but a drop.

Picture that.

Every ordinary happiness.

Every moment of aesthetic delight.

The pleasure of music.

The relief of understanding something difficult.

The joy of affection.

All are treated as limited disclosures of a reality whose intrinsic fullness is not exhausted by them.

This is a strikingly affirmative view of experience.

And now we understand another interpretation of *mahābhāgā*.

The Goddess can be described through the portions or manifestations of bliss because the whole field of differentiated experience belongs to her power.

Now the word **śṛṇu**, listen, takes centre stage.

And Abhinavagupta is about to explain why hearing is much stranger than your ears would have you believe. ; ; \]


## 5. शृणु - Śṛṇu: What does it actually mean to hear?

Bhairava says:

**शृणु - śṛṇu**

Listen!

And Abhinavagupta asks what hearing really is.

Now, before you roll your eyes and think, surely the man knows what ears do, remember the distinction we've been developing.

Physical sensory activity and conscious apprehension are not identical concepts.

Sound can occur without being understood.

Phonetic stimulation is not automatically meaningful cognition.

And this is where Abhinavagupta becomes unexpectedly relevant to the philosophy of language.

### The Sanskrit verb

**श्रु - √śru**

To hear.

Its imperative second-person singular is:

**शृणु - śṛṇu**

Now observe the vowel change.

The dictionary root √*śru* contains *u*.

But the present-system forms include:

**शृणोति - śṛṇoti**

He or she hears.

**शृणोषि - śṛṇoṣi**

You hear.

**शृणु - śṛṇu**

Hear! Listen!

This isn't an ordinary case of adding a suffix to an unchanged root. The present stem has a particular formation.

It's worth learning as a paradigm.

And don't confuse it with:

**श्रवण - śravaṇa**

Hearing, listening.

Also derived from the root-family of √*śru*, but through a different nominal formation.

### The noisy-room problem

Abhinavagupta gives an example involving indistinct sound, described as *kalakala*.

**कलकल - kalakala**

A clatter, confused noise, indistinct hubbub.

Imagine sitting in a crowded room.

People are speaking.

You hear voices.

There is a constant mixture of sound.

But perhaps you're not attending to any specific words.

You say:

"I can hear noise, but I can't hear what they're saying."

Now something changes.

You focus.

Distinct syllables become recognizable.

Words emerge.

You understand the sentence.

What happened?

It isn't simply that the ears abruptly acquired a new capacity to receive vibrations.

The sounds were already present.

But conscious apprehension has gathered and differentiated the auditory content.

Abhinavagupta describes this through the power of **saṅkalana**.

### Saṅkalana - सङ्कलन

Gathering, collecting, bringing together.

This is an extremely useful word.

From *saṃ-* plus a formation associated with √*kal*, to count, arrange or collect.

Now compare:

**संकल्प - saṅkalpa**

Intention, determination, conception.

And:

**सङ्कलन - saṅkalana**

Collecting, gathering.

They're related in form, but you shouldn't mechanically translate them as interchangeable.

Here *saṅkalana* means the act of gathering or integrating what is heard.

And Abhinavagupta argues that this integration depends upon the Goddess's power.

### The astounding implication

Hearing isn't merely the passive reception of a physical signal.

It includes the manifestation of meaningful unity.

You hear a sentence as a sentence.

Not simply as an unstructured pile of phonemes.

And the very power through which sound is assembled into intelligible expression is a manifestation of consciousness.

This allows him to connect the discussion with the Sanskrit grammarians' debate about **sphoṭa**.

### Sphoṭa - स्फोट

A bursting forth, disclosure, or linguistic unit apprehended as meaningful.

The term is associated with √*sphuṭ*, to burst, become clear or manifest.

Certain grammatical theories distinguish the underlying unit of meaning from the succession of individually articulated sounds.

Abhinavagupta mentions such explanations, but he does not simply abandon his own argument for an unqualified adoption of every version of sphoṭa theory.

His emphasis is on the power that makes meaningful apprehension possible.

Here's a memorable analogy.

Imagine somebody slowly spelling a word:

S.

H.

I.

V.

A.

You hear five distinct letters.

But you also apprehend them as a unified word.

The unity of the meaningful word isn't identical to any isolated letter.

And the moment of understanding can have a character different from the chronological sequence through which the sounds were presented.

Abhinavagupta is interested in the conscious power enabling that unity.

The phonemes are differentiated.

Yet meaningful apprehension brings them together.

**And the Goddess is the power of that apprehension.**

### Then he attacks simplistic accounts of whispered mantra

This is a particularly technical passage.

The Svacchandatantra distinguishes kinds of mantra recitation, including audible and softer forms.

The crucial question is what makes recitation genuinely internal rather than external.

If someone whispers very quietly, another person might still hear the sound.

So mere low volume isn't sufficient to establish the fundamental distinction.

Abhinavagupta pays attention to the articulation of phonemes, the movement of air, the positions of tongue and lips, and the relationship between internal expression and externally audible sound.

A person might move the lips while no fully articulated external sound is produced.

In that situation, the internal verbal form does not automatically become Vaikharī simply because visible bodily movement occurs.

Conversely, a very soft but fully articulated sound remains externally manifested speech, even if it's difficult to hear.

The distinction matters because **Madhyamā and Vaikharī are not simply different settings on a volume control.**

Madhyamā concerns internal verbal articulation.

Vaikharī concerns outwardly manifested speech.

Now that's an excellent philosophical point.

Imagine a monk whispering a mantra at approximately the volume of a disappointed mosquito.

Another person standing close by hears him.

The monk declares:

"My mantra is entirely internal because I whispered it extremely quietly."

Abhinavagupta would ask whether the utterance has actually crossed into external phonetic manifestation.

The metaphysics doesn't care about your personal definition of whispering.

It cares about the nature of the manifestation.

### Why this matters for Parā Vāk

The levels of speech are not independently self-conscious.

Their reality depends upon the supreme expressive power.

And the Goddess is invoked precisely because she is that power.

Thus Bhairava's command:

**Śṛṇu - Listen**

is not just an ordinary instruction directed at an independent listener.

It becomes a teaching about the underlying reality of hearing itself.

The Goddess is the one through whom hearing occurs.

And the Lord tells her to listen.

The irony is absolutely sublime.

Consciousness tells its own power of hearing to hear the reality of consciousness.

And Abhinavagupta proceeds to explain the whole thing through Sanskrit grammatical personhood.

Because apparently things weren't complicated enough. ; \]


## 6. The greatest Sanskrit grammar lesson in the whole bloody commentary

Here we arrive at an extraordinary argument.

And I especially want you to learn this one, because it brings together Sanskrit grammar and the Trika triad in an exceptionally memorable way.

The three grammatical persons:

**प्रथमपुरुष - prathama-puruṣa**

The third person.

**मध्यमपुरुष - madhyama-puruṣa**

The second person.

**उत्तमपुरुष - uttama-puruṣa**

The first person.

Yes, you read that correctly.

In traditional Sanskrit grammatical terminology, **prathama**, meaning first, names what we call the *third person* in English grammar.

The *uttama*, literally highest or foremost, names the *first person*.

So the traditional ordering is:

**सः**

saḥ

He

**Prathama-puruṣa**

Third person - associated here with **Nara**.

**त्वम्**

tvam

You

**Madhyama-puruṣa**

Second person - associated here with **Śakti**.

**अहम्**

aham

I

**Uttama-puruṣa**

First person - associated here with **Śiva**.

Now.

Before anybody decides that Sanskrit grammatical terminology proves Trika metaphysics, understand what Abhinavagupta is doing.

The person categories are ordinary grammatical facts.

**Their identification with Nara, Śakti and Śiva is his philosophical interpretation.**

He is using a feature of language to reveal the relationship between differentiated objecthood, relational awareness and universal subjectivity.

And it's genuinely fascinating.

### Nara - The third person, the apparently separate object

Consider:

**सः तिष्ठति - saḥ tiṣṭhati**

He stands.

This is a pedagogical sentence; the words can of course undergo sandhi in connected speech.

Now:

**तिष्ठति - tiṣṭhati**

Third-person singular.

From √*sthā*, to stand or remain.

The subject is something spoken about.

"He."

"She."

"It."

Or even a pot.

The object appears as something distinct from the speaker.

Abhinavagupta associates this mode with **Nara**, the differentiated or limited aspect of the triad.

Now notice: he is not saying every person spoken of in the third person is spiritually inferior.

He is interpreting the grammatical structure of reference.

The third person is typically someone or something not directly constituting the speaking "I" or addressed "you."

This provides an analogy for the differentiated field of objects.

### Śakti - The second person, the one addressed

Now:

**त्वं तिष्ठसि - tvaṃ tiṣṭhasi**

You stand.

Notice the verb.

**तिष्ठसि - tiṣṭhasi**

Second-person singular.

And here the other person isn't merely being discussed.

They're being directly addressed.

The "you" is recognized as another subject capable of hearing, responding and acting.

This is where Abhinavagupta's argument becomes very subtle.

When you address someone, you do not apprehend them merely as an inert object.

You recognize a form of subjectivity.

The other person is distinct from your individual position, yet possesses a capacity corresponding to your own.

Thus the second-person relationship expresses a mixture of difference and sameness.

And Abhinavagupta associates this with **Śakti**, who mediates between differentiation and nonduality.

In the traditional triadic language:

- Nara: differentiation predominates.
- Śakti: difference and non-difference are both manifest.
- Śiva: non-difference predominates.

This is not a classification of three different species of human beings.

It's a metaphysical interpretation of three modes of manifestation.

### Śiva - The first person, universal I-consciousness

Now:

**अहं तिष्ठामि - ahaṃ tiṣṭhāmi**

I stand.

**तिष्ठामि - tiṣṭhāmi**

First-person singular.

And the subject is:

**अहम् - aham**

I.

Here the expression is grounded in the immediate subjectivity of the speaker.

Now this is where the philosophy becomes radical.

Abhinavagupta's ultimate *aham* is not simply the biographical personality who says:

"I am twenty-seven years old, I enjoy Sanskrit, and I've had entirely too much tea."

That is the finite individual.

The universal *aham* is the fundamental subjectivity underlying all limited acts of identification.

The word "I" can be used by every conscious speaker, yet it doesn't function like the name of a fixed external object.

The reference shifts according to who is speaking.

And the immediacy of first-person awareness provides Abhinavagupta with a powerful grammatical analogy for supreme subjectivity.

**The universal I is not one more object presented to consciousness. It is the reality of consciousness itself.**

### The incredible plural examples

Now here's the part I absolutely love.

Suppose we say:

"He and you stand."

Sanskrit can use the second-person dual:

**तिष्ठथः - tiṣṭhathaḥ**

You two stand.

The third-person subject is included within the grammatical scope of the second-person form when the persons are combined.

Now add the speaker.

"He, you, and I stand."

The verb becomes:

**तिष्ठामः - tiṣṭhāmaḥ**

We stand.

First-person plural.

This is exactly the kind of grammatical hierarchy Abhinavagupta invokes.

The third person can be included within the second.

And the second within the first.

Not because the third-person individual has been physically swallowed by the speaker.

But because the grammatical expression of a collective containing the speaker takes first-person form.

The example becomes an analogy for:

**Nara → Śakti → Śiva**

The differentiated is included within a more encompassing mode.

And ultimately the whole structure can be understood within universal I-consciousness.

Now, that is a genuinely stunning way to interpret grammar.

The verb endings aren't manufacturing cosmic reality.

But their ordinary functioning is being used to illustrate the inclusion of apparent difference within a more comprehensive subjectivity.

### The root of tiṣṭhati

Let's learn the forms properly.

| Person | Sanskrit | IAST |
|--------|--------|--------|
| He stands | **तिष्ठति** | *tiṣṭhati* |
| You stand | **तिष्ठसि** | *tiṣṭhasi* |
| I stand | **तिष्ठामि** | *tiṣṭhāmi* |
| You two stand | **तिष्ठथः** | *tiṣṭhathaḥ* |
| We stand | **तिष्ठामः** | *tiṣṭhāmaḥ* |

The root is √**sthā**, to stand.

Its present stem is **tiṣṭha-**.

So don't assume the present stem is simply the dictionary root followed by personal endings.

Sanskrit has different present-stem formations, including reduplicated and modified forms.

Now notice how much metaphysical weight Abhinavagupta places on a grammatical distinction that most students would otherwise learn and forget.

The language itself becomes a mirror of the doctrine.

### But what about “I” referring to the body?

Abhinavagupta anticipates the objection.

When an ordinary person says "I stand," aren't they referring to the body?

Of course they often are.

But the body is a differentiated, conditioned object within experience.

The self-revealing character of the subject cannot be reduced without further argument to that bodily object.

Thus the sentence can simultaneously involve a conditioned bodily activity and the immediate first-person awareness through which it is apprehended.

The philosophical question is whether the limited bodily identification exhausts the nature of the subject.

For Abhinavagupta, it doesn't.

The universal reality is not excluded merely because the ordinary expression refers to the body.

### Even the categories can shift

He also discusses how grammatical personhood can be modified by the situation of speaking.

An apparently distinct object may be addressed.

A "you" may be described in third-person terms.

A person may even address themselves or speak about themselves as though they were someone else.

The forms can change.

Their relationships are not rigid substances.

This is important because the original triadic distinction might otherwise look like three independently existing things.

But Abhinavagupta insists that all three are manifestations of one reality.

And his reflections on ordinary speech show how fluidly their apparent forms can be redistributed.

This is another way of undermining the assumption that grammatical differentiation proves metaphysical separation.

**The categories have different functions, but their conscious ground remains one.**

### A deliciously practical example

Imagine yourself watching a lizard.

You say:

"He is looking at me."

Third person.

Then you address the lizard:

"You little bastard, I know you're watching me."

Second person.

Then you reflect:

"I am watching him watching me."

First person.

The scene has involved three grammatical relations.

The lizard hasn't magically travelled through three cosmic realms.

But the modes of apprehension have shifted.

Abhinavagupta's argument is about how the very possibility of those shifts depends upon conscious manifestation.

And from the Trika standpoint, the ultimate consciousness is not restricted to the isolated personal perspective that appears in the final sentence.

That's the thing worth contemplating.

The ordinary "I" is not yet the full recognition of the universal "I."

But the capacity for first-person awareness provides an exceptionally direct entry into the inquiry.

And then Abhinavagupta makes a beautiful observation.

Even languages and dialects that do not preserve Sanskrit grammatical forms exhibit person distinctions.

He mentions Buddhist, Andhra and Dravidian linguistic contexts.

The philosophical point is not that Sanskrit rules govern every language.

It is that the fundamental relations between speaker, addressee and what is spoken about are wider than the peculiar endings of any one language.

The Sanskrit grammarians have codified an order that, for him, reflects an underlying structure of conscious apprehension.

And that's why he thinks grammar can illuminate Trika.

Not merely because Sanskrit is sacred, but because **language is a manifestation of how consciousness relates to itself and its objects**.

; ; ; ; \]


## 7. उत्तरस्याप्यनुत्तरम् - Even the higher is not outside the Unsurpassable

Now Abhinavagupta returns to the central expression of verse three.

**उत्तरस्याप्यनुत्तरम्**

*Uttarasyāpy anuttaram.*

And after the extraordinary discussion of grammatical persons, we're equipped to understand why this is so important.

First, the grammar.

**उत्तरस्य - uttarasya**

Genitive singular of *uttara*.

Meaning: of the higher, of the subsequent, of the answer, depending on context.

**अपि - api**

Even, also, too.

**अनुत्तरम् - anuttaram**

The unsurpassable.

Now observe the sandhi:

# उत्तरस्य + अपि

# उत्तरस्यापि

uttarasya + api → uttarasyāpi

The two adjacent *a* vowels combine into long *ā*.

Now add *anuttaram*.

**उत्तरस्याप्यनुत्तरम्**

There is another *i + a* boundary, represented by the *y* glide.

So the complete form is:

*uttarasya + api + anuttaram → uttarasyāpy anuttaram*.

Two sandhi operations in one little phrase.

Learn that and a remarkable amount of continuous Sanskrit suddenly becomes readable.

### First interpretation - Even Uttara rests in Anuttara

The Goddess asked how Anuttara grants perfection.

Bhairava replies by describing the Anuttara even of *uttara*.

Abhinavagupta explains that the higher or differentiated manifestation itself depends on Anuttara.

The apparently subsequent reality is not independent of what underlies it.

This is important because we've spent so much time distinguishing the Absolute from hierarchies.

We might mistakenly conclude that manifestation must simply be discarded.

That Anuttara is real, while *uttara* is a worthless error.

But that's not the Trika position.

The differentiated manifestation belongs to supreme freedom.

Śakti is not a metaphysical accident.

The universe is not an unauthorized second substance.

**Even the higher or subsequent expression of consciousness possesses its reality through Anuttara.**

Now imagine a man standing before the ocean, declaring that waves are an unfortunate illusion because water is the only real thing.

Well, yes, the wave depends on water.

But you're not improving the situation by pretending no waves appear.

The point is to understand their reality correctly.

Abhinavagupta's position is not simply the negation of the manifested universe.

It is recognition of the universe in its divine ground.

### Second interpretation - The genitive of disregard

Now comes the grammar that makes the whole passage rather delicious.

Abhinavagupta invokes the possibility of:

**अनादरे षष्ठी - anādare ṣaṣṭhī**

The genitive used to indicate disregard or concession.

Let's learn the terms.

**अनादर - anādara**

Disregard, lack of consideration.

From:

*an-* \+ *ādara*.

*Ādara* means regard, respect, attention.

**षष्ठी - ṣaṣṭhī**

Sixth.

The traditional Sanskrit name for the genitive case.

So the expression refers to a special use of the genitive in which something occurs despite or without regard to another condition.

Abhinavagupta exploits this to explain *uttarasya*.

The higher or differentiated condition is not being established as an independent ultimate reality.

Rather, Anuttara is recognized even without accepting the supposedly separate status of that higher manifestation.

This is subtler than merely saying:

"Anuttara is higher than Uttara."

Because that formulation risks constructing another hierarchy.

It suggests that we have one entity called Uttara and a second, superior entity called Anuttara.

And now we're back in the cosmic ranking business.

Abhinavagupta's point is that the differentiated condition has no independently ultimate existence outside its ground.

### Third interpretation - Even the answering process is grounded in Anuttara

Remember the earlier meaning of *uttara* as an answer?

Now that returns.

The answer Bhairava is giving is itself a manifestation of consciousness.

The act of replying belongs to the differentiated expressive power.

But the reality disclosed by that reply cannot be reduced to the spoken words.

The answer is not an object outside the consciousness through which the answer manifests.

So even Bhairava's teaching is understood as an expression of Anuttara.

And this creates a remarkable recursive structure.

Bhairava speaks about Anuttara.

But the act of speaking depends upon Anuttara.

The Goddess hears the answer.

But the hearing depends upon Anuttara.

The disciple understands the answer.

But the understanding depends upon Anuttara.

And the distinction between speaker, listener and teaching also manifests within that reality.

In other words:

**Consciousness is using its own differentiated powers to reveal the undivided reality of those very powers.**

It's not a logical trick.

It's the fundamental structure of the teaching.

### Fourth interpretation - The Trident and the higher absorption

Abhinavagupta also connects *uttara* with the divine powers invoked in the symbolism of the trident.

**त्रिशूल - triśūla**

Trident.

Break it down:

**त्रि - tri**

Three.

**शूल - śūla**

Spear, spike, pointed weapon.

The trident represents threefold powers whose movement is gathered into a higher state of absorption.

This symbolism connects with the Trika's interest in will, knowledge and action, though its ritual interpretations are more elaborate than a simple three-word correspondence.

Abhinavagupta is asking where the power of that higher absorption ultimately rests.

The answer is still Anuttara.

Even the extraordinary yogic experience associated with the threefold divine power doesn't establish a reality beyond the supreme consciousness.

So we have a remarkable progression:

The manifestation depends on Anuttara.

The grammatical relation can express disregard for the separate status of that manifestation.

The answer itself depends on Anuttara.

And even the highest yogic absorption does not escape Anuttara.

The supreme reality is not threatened by its manifestations.

**Its freedom is precisely what makes them possible.**

Now we've finally reached Bhairava's announcement of the Kaulika method. ; \]


## 8. कौलिकोऽयं विधिः - This Kaulika method

And now the Lord says:

**कौलिकोऽयं विधिः**

*Kauliko 'yaṃ vidhiḥ.*

This Kaulika method.

At first glance, you might imagine we're finally going to receive a specific ritual.

Maybe a sequence of breath retentions.

A secret mantra.

A particular meditation posture.

A terrifyingly complicated arrangement of Sanskrit syllables.

But first Abhinavagupta explains what **vidhi** means here.

### The Sanskrit

**विधि - vidhi**

Method, procedure, prescription, arrangement, ordinance.

Related to *vi-* and √*dhā*, to place, establish or arrange.

The idea is an ordering or establishment of something.

And:

**कौलिक - kaulika**

Pertaining to Kula and the Kaula reality.

Now watch the sandhi:

**कौलिकः + अयम्**

*kaulikaḥ + ayam*

Becomes:

**कौलिकोऽयम्**

*kauliko 'yam*.

This is one of those rules you absolutely should memorize.

When a word ending in **-aḥ** is followed by initial **a**, the visarga commonly changes to **o**, while the following *a* is elided and indicated by the avagraha.

Thus:

**-aḥ + a- → -o '**

So:

*kaulikaḥ ayam → kauliko 'yam*.

Compare your earlier prayer:

*brahma + arpaṇam → brahmārpaṇam*.

That's a different vowel-sandhi operation.

And:

*śramaḥ ayam → śramo 'yam*.

That's the same visarga pattern we're seeing here.

You are now learning to reverse the sandhi while reading rather than treating connected Sanskrit as a string of mysterious new words.

### What is the Kaulika method?

Abhinavagupta identifies it with the great manifestation of the relationship between **Kula and Akula**.

Kula: differentiated manifestation.

Akula: the transcendent reality of Śiva.

Their inseparability: Kaula.

And the method is understood in relation to the emergence of manifestation from the supreme consciousness and its repose within that same reality.

This is not merely a ritual procedure taking place inside an independently existing universe.

The universal act of manifestation itself is the primary reality being explained.

Abhinavagupta describes the countless modes of creation as contained within this single divine freedom.

And he invokes a teaching whose central structure is extraordinarily beautiful:

That in which everything exists.

That from which everything arises.

That which is everything.

That which is present in everything.

The universal conscious reality is simultaneously the ground, source, manifestation and pervading nature of the totality.

### This isn't ordinary causation

Now think about the difference between making a table and the Trika conception of creation.

A carpenter requires wood.

Tools.

Time.

Physical activity.

The table is produced through a chain of dependent causes.

But the supreme consciousness cannot be understood as a carpenter manufacturing the cosmos from an entirely independent material that existed before it.

That would require two ultimate realities.

Consciousness.

And the external stuff from which the universe is made.

Abhinavagupta refuses the division.

The manifestation is grounded in Śiva's own freedom.

And he does not need a second material substance outside himself.

This is why *svātantrya* is so central.

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Sovereign independence.

The freedom not to depend upon an external ultimate cause.

### The thirty-six tattvas return

Abhinavagupta makes clear that the entire manifested order is included.

And this means the thirty-six tattvas.

From the supreme divine principles through Māyā and the limiting conditions, down through intellect, ego-function, mind, senses, subtle elements and gross elements.

The whole shebang.

Now imagine someone announcing:

"I've understood the supreme consciousness, but unfortunately the physical universe is still outside it."

Well, you've misunderstood the claim.

The manifest universe doesn't become independent of consciousness merely because it appears differentiated.

And the power of manifestation is not a foreign addition to Śiva.

Abhinavagupta speaks of the universal reality as involving both the generalized ground and its particularized expansion.

The power and possessor of power.

The manifest and transcendent.

The differentiated and undivided.

These are distinguishable for the purposes of teaching.

But not metaphysically independent.

The Kaulika method is therefore associated with both **prasara**, expansion, and **viśrānti**, repose.

### Prasara - प्रसर

Expansion, spreading forth.

### Viśrānti - विश्रान्ति

Rest, repose, settling into one's own nature.

Related to *vi-* and √*śram*, to rest or become weary, in the formation *viśrānti*.

Now, notice the philosophical pairing.

Manifestation proceeds.

And manifestation rests in its own ground.

This doesn't mean consciousness first ceases to be supreme and then eventually gets around to becoming supreme again.

The movement is one of appearance and recognition.

The expansion remains grounded in the same reality in which it ultimately rests.

And Bhairava says that this method is situated in:

**मम हृद्व्योम्नि - mama hṛdvyomni**

The space of my Heart.

Now things become seriously interesting. ; \]


## 9. मम हृद्व्योम्नि - In the space of my Heart

This is one of the most beautiful expressions in the whole work.

And we're going to understand it from the sounds upward.

**मम - mama**

My, of me.

**हृद् - hṛd**

Heart.

**व्योमन् - vyoman**

Sky, space, expanse.

The locative singular of *vyoman* is:

**व्योम्नि - vyomni**

Thus:

**हृद्व्योम्नि - hṛdvyomni**

In the space of the Heart.

### Why vyomni, not vyomane?

This is the sort of grammatical detail that makes Sanskrit genuinely satisfying.

The noun *vyoman* is a neuter *-man* stem.

Its nominative and accusative singular are:

**व्योम - vyoma**

Space.

But its locative singular is:

**व्योम्नि - vyomni**

In space.

The stem changes according to its inflectional pattern.

You cannot always add an ordinary vowel ending to the form you see in the dictionary.

Now let's look at the conjunct:

**म्न = म् + न**

The consonant म loses its inherent *a* before न.

And the final *i* belongs to the case ending.

Now add:

**अवस्थितः - avasthitaḥ**

Situated, established, abiding.

The result is:

**हृद्व्योम्न्यवस्थितः**

*hṛdvyomny avasthitaḥ*.

Why the *y*?

Because:

**i + a → ya**

The final *i* of *vyomni* becomes the corresponding glide before the initial *a* of *avasthitaḥ*.

So the continuous phrase contains two words:

*hṛdvyomni avasthitaḥ*.

But the written form reflects their phonetic combination.

Excellent.

Now what does *avasthitaḥ* mean?

### Avasthita - अवस्थित

Established, positioned, remaining.

From:

**ava + √sthā**

The verbal root √*sthā* means to stand, remain or be situated.

The participial formation *sthita* means stood, established, situated.

And *ava-sthita* gives *avasthita*.

The masculine nominative singular is *avasthitaḥ*, agreeing with *vidhiḥ*.

Thus the method is established in the Heart-space.

### But whose Heart?

Bhairava says:

**मम - mama**

My.

Now remember Part IV.

We spent an extraordinary amount of time investigating the word *mama*.

And the commentary returns to that meaning here.

The "my" of universal consciousness is not an ego claiming private ownership of a cosmic object.

It is the self-referential nature of universal awareness.

Abhinavagupta considers the way even ordinary objects appear in relation to the experiencer.

A colour is apprehended.

A sensation arises.

An object is known.

And the apparent "mine" belongs to the relation of manifestation within awareness.

But for the universal subject, everything appears within the one conscious field.

The Heart is the ground in which all manifest things have their ultimate establishment.

### The Heart contains every colour

Abhinavagupta explicitly discusses the manifestation of blue and other objects.

Why?

Because the philosophical issue is not confined to extraordinary mystical states.

Even seeing blue involves consciousness.

The blue colour is presented as something differentiated.

But its manifestation as something known depends upon awareness.

And the Heart is not a physical object somewhere behind the colour.

It is the self-luminous ground of the experience.

Thus the Lord's Heart is the reality in which the entire world appears.

Including objects.

Subjects.

Minds.

Senses.

And even the distinctions between them.

### Vyoman isn't empty nothingness

Now a crucial distinction.

**व्योमन् - vyoman**

Space.

But this doesn't mean an empty physical cavity located inside Bhairava's chest.

The metaphor concerns non-obstruction, openness, and the absence of ultimate delimitation.

The Heart-space is not one location among others.

It is the ground of spatial manifestation itself.

And Abhinavagupta describes the differentiated universe as resting within that expanse.

The apparent individuality of the objects does not establish an independent ultimate reality.

They are present within the self-luminous consciousness.

The Heart-space is therefore not an absence of existence.

It is the fullness in which all determinate manifestations arise.

### Now comes the phonemic reversal again

And here's where the commentary becomes delightfully strange.

Abhinavagupta explores *mama hṛdvyomni* through the symbolic reabsorption of:

**म - ma**

**ह - ha**

**अ - a**

The order moves backward.

From differentiated individuality toward the power of emission, and from there into the unsurpassable.

This is an esoteric *nirvacana*, not ordinary Sanskrit morphological analysis.

The word *mama* does not historically derive from a hidden cosmological sequence of *ma-ha-a*.

Rather, Abhinavagupta takes the sounds as meaningful symbols through which the process of manifestation and reabsorption can be contemplated.

The *ma* can signify the limited subject.

The *ha* participates in the symbolism of emission.

The *a* signifies Anuttara, the unsurpassable ground.

And the return is toward the complete self-awareness of the Heart.

Now imagine the whole universe contracting back into recognition of its own source.

Not because all physical things must be annihilated.

But because their ultimate reality is no longer misrecognized as independent of consciousness.

**The Heart-space is both the source of manifestation and the ground of its repose.**

And this is why the Kaulika method can be said to reside there.

It is not installed in the Heart like a little ritual device.

The very principles of manifestation and recognition belong to the Heart's own freedom.

; \]


## 10. कथयामि - Kathayāmi: When Bhairava says “I speak”

Now we reach one of those sentences where the philosophy becomes almost absurdly self-referential.

Bhairava says:

**कथयामि - kathayāmi**

I explain.

I tell.

And Abhinavagupta relates this to the emergence of differentiated speech from the supreme consciousness.

### The ordinary grammar

The root is:

**कथ् - √kath**

To tell, narrate, explain.

The present stem is:

**कथय - kathaya-**

Then add the first-person singular ending:

**-मि - -mi**

The resulting form is:

**कथयामि - kathayāmi**

I tell.

Now compare:

**कथयति - kathayati**

He or she tells.

**कथयसि - kathayasi**

You tell.

**कथयामि - kathayāmi**

I tell.

Notice how the endings express grammatical personhood.

And remember our previous discussion.

Third person.

Second person.

First person.

Nara.

Śakti.

Śiva.

The grammar is once again becoming philosophical.

### The supreme speaker

Bhairava's "I" is not merely the limited personal pronoun of an ordinary individual.

It refers to universal consciousness.

Now, here's the problem.

If the universal consciousness is undivided, how can it produce differentiated speech?

Because ordinary speaking involves sequence.

First syllable.

Second syllable.

Third syllable.

And so forth.

Abhinavagupta explains this through the levels of speech.

The supreme awareness manifests its own power through Parā, Paśyantī, Madhyamā and Vaikharī.

And finally the words are spoken.

Thus Bhairava's statement "I explain" isn't simply a description of one external action.

It indicates the universal expressive power manifesting in a determinate verbal sequence.

### Somānanda's interpretation

Abhinavagupta cites Somānanda's understanding of *kathayāmi* in terms of uttering or expressing through an inner stirring.

The key idea is that speech arises from the internal power of consciousness before becoming externally articulated.

Now imagine you're about to say something.

There is an intention.

An apprehended meaning.

A forming verbal expression.

Then the movements of the organs of speech.

Finally the articulated sound.

Ordinary psychology can analyse these stages through cognitive and physiological processes.

Abhinavagupta is making a metaphysical claim about the conscious ground of the entire sequence.

Not that every stage is scientifically explained by Tantric cosmology.

But that the capacity for manifestation itself is inseparable from the power of consciousness.

### The ultimate speaker isn't speaking from a distant location

When Bhairava says "I speak," the speech itself arises as a manifestation of his power.

The listener is not outside that ground.

The act of listening is not outside it.

The words are not outside it.

And the meaning apprehended through them isn't outside it.

So the entire exchange is one expression of universal consciousness.

Now we're back to the apparent paradox with which we began.

The Goddess asks.

Bhairava answers.

And the truth being communicated is the reality of both questioner and respondent.

**The dialogue is a manifestation of the very nonduality it teaches.**

This is why Abhinavagupta can treat the act of speaking as philosophically significant rather than a merely external vehicle for the doctrine.

The speech isn't ultimately separate from the truth.

But nor is every arbitrary utterance automatically a complete expression of liberation.

The distinction between meaningful instruction and confused speech remains important at the level of manifestation.

The universal power makes both possible.

Recognition concerns its real nature.

### Sureśāni - सुरेशानि

Bhairava also says:

**सुरेशानि - sureśāni**

O Mistress of the gods.

Break it down:

**सुर - sura**

Deity, god.

**ईशानी - īśānī**

Lady, mistress, female sovereign.

The compound undergoes vowel sandhi:

**sura + īśānī → sureśānī**

Because:

**a + ī → e**

The vocative singular of *sureśānī* is:

**sureśāni**.

Notice the shorter final *i* in the vocative.

Abhinavagupta does not devote a large independent etymological discussion to this title at this precise point.

Its role is to address the Goddess as the divine mistress of the powers.

And now we're ready for the final part of the verse.


## 11. सद्यः - Sadyaḥ: The instantaneous reality

Remember:

**सद्यः - sadyaḥ**

Immediately.

We've already studied the ordinary meaning in Part III.

Now Abhinavagupta gives another, considerably more esoteric explanation.

And this is where we must be especially careful not to confuse historical Sanskrit grammar with his symbolic interpretation.

### The ordinary meaning

*Sadyaḥ* means immediately, straightaway, at once.

In the original question, the Goddess asks how realization brings about perfection immediately.

And Bhairava repeats the term.

The answer must therefore explain the nature of that immediacy.

But Abhinavagupta now examines the relation between *sat*, being, and divine activity.

### Sat - सत्

**सत् - sat**

Being, existent, real.

This is related to √**as**, to be.

And it gives us familiar expressions such as:

**सत्यम् - satyam**

Truth, reality, truthfulness.

And:

**सच्चिदानन्द - saccidānanda**

Being-consciousness-bliss.

Though note that *saccidānanda* is a compound with its own history and philosophical uses; we shouldn't automatically identify every occurrence of *sat* with that entire later formulation.

Now Abhinavagupta explores the possibility that the expression indicates the divine reality characterized by the power of action.

The idea is not simply that something happens very quickly.

It is that the ultimate real is inseparable from its own operative freedom.

### The special reading sadyad

The commentary records a variant read by certain teachers:

**सद्यद् - sadyad**

Abhinavagupta explains this through an esoteric derivational treatment involving the real, *sat*, and a verbal sense of striving or activity.

He invokes the technical grammatical suffix **kvip**, a sometimes zero-realized suffix used in Sanskrit derivation.

This is not an ordinary dictionary explanation of the adverb *sadyaḥ*.

The interpretive goal is to relate the word to the active power of supreme existence.

The underlying argument is familiar.

If supreme consciousness were merely inert being, how would manifestation be possible?

But if freedom of action belongs to its very nature, manifestation doesn't require a second independent cause.

Thus the supreme reality is both self-luminous and freely expressive.

### The philosophical meaning of immediacy

Now we can bring together the earlier and later readings.

The ordinary sense of *sadyaḥ* concerns immediacy in time.

The deeper interpretation concerns the reality that makes temporal manifestation possible.

The supreme consciousness isn't a product whose creation requires a temporal sequence.

Recognition may occur within time.

But the reality recognized doesn't become real only at that instant.

This is the crucial distinction.

Imagine a lamp illuminating a room.

You look around.

You notice the objects.

Then suddenly you become aware of the lamp itself.

The recognition of the lamp happens at a particular moment.

But the lamp didn't begin illuminating the room only when you noticed it.

Now the analogy isn't perfect, since the physical lamp is a finite object while universal consciousness is the ground of objectification.

But the basic relation is illuminating.

**The recognition occurs; the reality being recognized was not manufactured by the recognition.**

And this is why Abhinavagupta can speak of immediate Kaulika perfection without reducing liberation to a future object.




## 12. कौलिकसिद्धिदम् - The perfection that makes the whole cosmos intelligible

Now we arrive at the final compound.

**कौलिकसिद्धिदम् - kaulikasiddhidam**

That which grants Kaulika perfection.

Again, the structure:

**कौलिक - kaulika**

Pertaining to Kula and Akula.

**सिद्धि - siddhi**

Accomplishment, perfection.

**द - da**

Giving, granting.

The neuter ending *-am* agrees with the reality being characterized in the verse.

But here Abhinavagupta adds an important emphasis.

*Siddhi* is understood through **firm establishment in the true nature of reality**.

The differentiated world is apprehended as the manifestation of one supreme consciousness.

Not as an independent collection of objects completely detached from their ground.

### The Sanskrit: Tathātva-dārḍhya

**तथात्व - tathātva**

The condition of being thus, suchness or actual nature.

From *tathā*, thus, with the abstract suffix *-tva*.

**दार्ढ्य - dārḍhya**

Firmness, stability.

The expression concerns firm ascertainment of the actual nature of the manifest totality.

Now that's a much richer idea than simply gaining an unusual power.

It's the stable recognition of how things truly are.

And the whole world enters the account.

Because Kaula perfection is not the destruction of Kula.

It is recognition of the totality in its true relation to Akula.

The manifest world is not discarded.

Its ultimate reality is understood.

### The hierarchy of Tantric traditions

Abhinavagupta now cites scriptural materials arranging different traditions in a hierarchy.

The names include Vedic, Śaiva, Vāma, Dakṣiṇa, Kula, Mata and Trika.

And the cited hierarchy places Trika at the highest position.

Now this must be understood historically and philosophically.

Abhinavagupta is writing from within a particular Tantric lineage.

The hierarchy expresses that tradition's own claims to doctrinal and ritual superiority.

It is not an independently demonstrated ranking of all historical Indian religious systems.

But why does he include it here?

Because the passage is establishing the distinctive scope of the Kaulika method.

Trika claims to incorporate and transcend lower distinctions through recognition of the nondual reality underlying them.

This is part of its self-understanding as a comprehensive teaching.

And it provides an interesting contrast with the earlier sixteen meanings of Anuttara.

Within the historical tradition, hierarchies of teaching and initiation matter.

At the level of ultimate reality, no independent hierarchy can surpass Anuttara.

The practical and absolute perspectives are being distinguished.

That's why Abhinavagupta can cite sectarian hierarchies while maintaining that the supreme consciousness is not merely the final item in a series of competing objects.

### Somānanda's conclusion

Abhinavagupta invokes Somānanda again.

The point is that everything, insofar as its true reality is understood, is Anuttara.

This isn't a trivial declaration that all conceptual distinctions are meaningless.

It is the result of the whole preceding argument.

The distinguished forms remain manifest.

But none possesses ultimate independence from the universal consciousness.

And the firm recognition of this is the perfection under discussion.

**The cosmos has not been replaced by something else. Its reality has been recognized.**




## 13. Abhinavagupta gets into an argument with other commentators

And just when you think we've reached the peaceful conclusion-

The man becomes combative.

Of course.

Because after all this meticulous metaphysical analysis, Abhinavagupta turns toward rival interpretations of the passage.

And he is not particularly gentle.

He criticizes interpretations he regards as grammatically and philosophically inadequate.

Now this is a very interesting historical moment.

It means we're not merely reading a philosophical system developed in isolation.

We're watching an exegete arguing over the proper interpretation of an inherited scriptural text.

And the dispute involves the precise relationship between **Nara, Śakti and Śiva**.

### The rival interpretation

One alternative treats the Goddess's initial question as being about Śiva.

The subsequent question concerns Śakti.

And Bhairava's reply is interpreted in a way suggesting that both the higher and unsurpassable are to be heard.

Now Abhinavagupta objects.

If the passage is interpreted according to the Trika triad, where is the corresponding independent third question concerning Nara?

And if it is interpreted instead as a Śiva-Śakti pair, one must be careful not to reify two independent ultimate substances.

The whole point is their inseparability.

So he argues that the alternative reading doesn't properly account for the structure of the teaching.

### The grammatical objection

Abhinavagupta also discusses the relationship between the previous question and the subsequent reply.

The sequence matters.

Certain particles and expressions indicate a relation between what has already been established and what follows.

An interpretation that requires a different structure of questioning and answering must explain why the Sanskrit supports it.

You cannot simply announce that a verse means whatever fits the system you've already decided to defend.

The Sanskrit has grammatical constraints.

Even in a work full of esoteric wordplay.

And this is what makes Abhinavagupta fascinating.

He permits highly symbolic *nirvacana*.

But he also demands rigorous attention to conventional grammar, textual sequence and the argumentative relation between verses.

**Esoteric interpretation isn't supposed to excuse complete philological indifference.**

### The older manuscript reading

He mentions a verse found in certain older manuscripts that expresses prior understanding of the great knowledge called Trika, along with an understanding of the higher teaching through the Lord's grace.

This is important evidence for the textual history.

Abhinavagupta knew that the scriptural material circulated in more than one form.

And he was willing to appeal to manuscript readings in an interpretive dispute.

But that does not mean every variant automatically carries equal authority in his argument.

He explicitly favours the interpretation transmitted through Somānanda and his own teachers.

### The great scholar loses his patience

And then he composes verses describing the sun of his exposition dispersing the darkness of bad interpretation.

It's one of those marvellously self-assured moments.

He is effectively announcing:

"I have now illuminated this teaching so thoroughly that the darkness of incompetent commentary has been annihilated."

My brother in Bhairava.

Your humility is positively incandescent.

But there's a serious reason for the polemical tone.

He considers accurate understanding of this teaching soteriologically important.

A mistaken interpretation isn't merely a small scholarly inconvenience.

It can obscure the recognition toward which the scripture is directed.

He therefore regards the exegetical work itself as part of the liberating enterprise.

This isn't just commentary as intellectual entertainment.

It's commentary as a means of removing misrecognition.




## 14. The end of verses 3-4 - Why Abhinavagupta continues after the answer is already complete

Now comes the perfect conclusion.

Abhinavagupta says, in effect, that the essential truth has already been established.

For those whose understanding is firm enough, the opening question and Bhairava's reply suffice.

The meaning is there.

Anuttara is the source, reality and repose of the entire Kaulika manifestation.

The method is not fundamentally separate from supreme consciousness.

And Kaulika perfection is grounded in recognition of that reality.

So why keep writing?

Because not every student has the same degree of understanding.

Some can recognize the essential point through a concise teaching.

Others require much more extensive explanation.

The text's subsequent development is therefore addressed to those needing a detailed exposition of the relationship between supreme consciousness and differentiated manifestation.

This is one of the most pedagogically important moments in the entire work.

And it connects beautifully with the first benedictory verses.

Remember how Abhinavagupta said he was writing for the understanding of his students and the recollection of those already awakened to the teaching?

Here we see that intention expressed directly in the structure of the commentary.

The teaching can be concise.

But understanding may require elaboration.

### The source of the Kaulika method

The concluding argument can be expressed through three closely related claims.

The Kaulika manifestation arises from Anuttara.

It rests in Anuttara.

And its essential reality is not different from Anuttara.

The source.

The repose.

The identity.

Now this is not a claim that every individual object is indistinguishable from every other object at the level of ordinary experience.

The distinctions remain meaningful.

The claim is about their ultimate metaphysical ground.

And the teaching has moved from the general principle to the necessity of explaining the structure of the manifest universe.

Which is exactly what comes next.

Because verse five will begin introducing the phonemic cosmology.

The sounds.

The vowels.

The lunar digits.

The solar powers.

The grouping of phonemes into different levels of manifestation.

And the relationship between those sounds and the thirty-six tattvas.

Abhinavagupta has spent this entire movement establishing why language is not an arbitrary ornament attached to the cosmos.

Now he can begin explaining the universe through the structure of sacred speech.

The philosophical foundation is complete.

The phonemic exposition is about to begin.

And the Lord's first two verses have done their job.


## 15. The whole argument - Why one simple instruction contains the universe

Now let's gather the entire movement.

Bhairava says:

**Listen, O Goddess.**

But the Goddess is herself the power of consciousness through which hearing becomes possible.

He says:

**I shall speak.**

But the speaking is a manifestation of the very consciousness being described.

He says:

**The method is in the space of my Heart.**

But the Heart is not a physical container.

It is the ultimate ground of all manifestation.

He says:

**The method is Kaulika.**

But Kula and Akula are not independently existing realities.

Their inseparability is the very subject of the teaching.

And he says:

**It grants perfection immediately.**

But the supreme reality is not an object that must first be produced in time.

Recognition is possible because consciousness is already the ground of experience.

The whole argument is therefore recursive.

Every part of the teaching depends upon the reality the teaching is supposed to disclose.

And that, fundamentally, is why Abhinavagupta finds the question-answer structure so important.

The Goddess's question is the expansion of conscious power into differentiation.

Bhairava's answer is the reabsorption of that differentiation into its undivided ground.

But the two movements never establish an ultimate separation.

The whole dialogue is one manifestation of universal freedom.

### Why this isn't just idealism about your private mind

Here's an important philosophical qualification.

It is tempting to take the teaching and say:

"Everything I experience occurs in my awareness, therefore my individual mind creates the whole universe."

But that's not an adequate statement of Trika.

The limited individual mind is itself one manifestation within the larger field of universal consciousness.

The personal ego does not possess the absolute freedom attributed to Śiva.

It experiences constrained knowledge.

Limited agency.

Temporal succession.

And ignorance of its own universal ground.

Trika's universal *aham* is not simply the personality inflated without limit.

It is the ground of the very distinction between finite subject and object.

That is why the doctrine of recognition is necessary.

The limited subject does not become omnipotent by declaring itself Śiva.

Rather, liberation concerns recognition of the universal consciousness underlying limited subjectivity.

And the whole process of manifestation and recognition belongs to Śakti's freedom.

### The connection with the Śiva Sūtras

Remember the opening:

**चैतन्यमात्मा - caitanyam ātmā**

Consciousness is the Self.

And the later teaching:

**ज्ञानं बन्धः - jñānaṃ bandhaḥ**

Knowledge is bondage.

In Kṣemarāja's interpretation, the latter concerns limited or differentiated knowledge, not the intrinsic awareness of Śiva.

Now look at what Abhinavagupta has developed.

The differentiated structure of knowledge appears through the powers of speech and cognition.

Those powers are not ultimately separate from consciousness.

But when their manifestations are apprehended as independent realities and the subject is identified exclusively with limitation, bondage is experienced.

Recognition reveals their actual nature.

This is precisely why the discussion of grammatical persons matters.

"He."

"You."

"I."

Ordinary linguistic distinctions.

But their interpretation through Nara, Śakti and Śiva becomes a way of investigating the structure of experience.

### The connection with Pratyabhijñā

And now return to:

**प्रत्यभिज्ञा - pratyabhijñā**

Recognition.

The philosophical question isn't whether you can accumulate enough correct statements about consciousness.

It is whether the nature of the experiencer is recognized.

The very consciousness through which you investigate the teaching is not fundamentally external to the reality the teaching describes.

Which brings us to the beautiful and slightly ridiculous conclusion.

The student reads a commentary about consciousness.

The student understands some Sanskrit.

The student reflects upon the meaning.

The student's awareness is the condition of each of these acts.

And the philosophical claim is that this conscious reality is not fundamentally different from the universal ground described by the scripture.

The teaching is not pointing toward another object to be acquired.

It is directing inquiry toward the nature of awareness itself.

That's an extraordinary move.

And it is why this particular text is so important for understanding Abhinavagupta.

**The Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa is not simply a commentary on mantras. It is a sustained argument about how consciousness, language, self-awareness and manifestation are inseparable.**

The phonemes matter because speech is a manifestation of consciousness.

The grammar matters because the distinctions of speech express relations within conscious experience.

And the recognition matters because the apparent separation of those relations does not establish an ultimate plurality.

That's the entire philosophical architecture of the opening reply.
