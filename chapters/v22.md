# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XXII - Spanda: The Movement That Goes Nowhere

*Somānanda returns from the philosophical grave carrying an alphabet that is simultaneously transformed and untransformed. Abhinavagupta explains why the Supreme can move without travelling anywhere, introduces a wave that doesn't need an ocean outside consciousness, invokes the mysterious belly of a fish, and then subjects four tiny Sanskrit verses to such aggressive grammatical cross-examination that the entire cosmos turns out to have several legitimate meanings.*

## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

Twenty-one installments.

We've now watched Abhinavagupta explain practically the entire universe through the Sanskrit alphabet.

We've learned why the vowels represent divine powers, why consonants can be understood as generative matrices, why cognition has distinct moments, why space and time are manifestations rather than independent sovereign realities, and why Mount Meru doesn't become the same size as an atom merely because both are grounded in consciousness.

Then came the wonderful lunar-solar swing.

Twelvefold contraction.

Sixteenfold fullness.

The Goddess emitting and swallowing the network of divine powers.

And the whole magnificent drama culminated in:

# अहम्

Aham - the divine I-consciousness

The first vowel at the beginning.

The generative phonemic powers in the middle.

Bindu at the end.

The entire manifestation understood through the supreme self-revealing **I**.

Now comes an extraordinary historical moment.

Abhinavagupta stops his own exposition and invokes his philosophical grandfather, Somānanda.

Not merely because Somānanda was an important predecessor.

But because Somānanda had written his own commentary on the *Parātrīśikā*, and Abhinavagupta is preserving a fragment of it.

This is valuable textual evidence. Somānanda's *Parātrīśikāvivṛti* is not available to us as a complete independent work. Abhinavagupta's citations are therefore particularly important witnesses to his predecessor's interpretation. ; \]

And Somānanda's teaching leads directly to the deepest philosophical question of this installment.

**What exactly is Spanda?**

If Spanda means vibration, what vibrates?

If it means movement, where does consciousness move?

And if consciousness is already everything, what could it possibly move *into*?

A second universe?

A bigger consciousness?

An adjoining field rented from another Absolute?

That would be a colossal metaphysical embarrassment.

So Abhinavagupta gives a wonderfully precise answer.

Spanda is a kind of *kiṃciccalana*-a slight movement.

But not ordinary movement from one independently existing thing to another.

It is the self-expressive upsurge of consciousness within its own nature.

The difference between these two senses of movement will govern the entire reading.

And afterward, the commentator returns to the root Tantra's wording, showing how the same Sanskrit can be understood through different phonemic, grammatical and metaphysical analyses.

We will stop on p. 209, just as the argument turns from pure mantra-powers to their differentiated appearances under Māyā. The full treatment of those differentiated phonemes begins in the next section, making that the appropriate boundary for Part XXIII. ; \]


## 1. तदुक्तं श्रीसोमानन्दपादैः - The grandfather of Pratyabhijñā enters the room

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · KASHMIR SERIES P. 207

**तदुक्तं श्रीसोमानन्दपादैर्निजविवृतौ।**

*Tad uktaṃ śrīsomānandapādair nijavivṛtau.*

"That has been said by the venerable Somānanda in his own commentary."

Now, immediately notice the attribution.

Abhinavagupta is not claiming the next words are from the root Tantra.

He's not introducing a verse from Utpaladeva.

And he's not supplying an editorial note.

He's explicitly referring to **Somānanda's own commentary**.

Let's dismantle the Sanskrit.

### Tad - तद्

That.

The pronoun points back to the preceding account of the divine phonemic manifestation as *Aham*.

### Uktam - उक्तम्

Said, declared, stated.

From √**वच् - vac**, to speak.

The past passive participle:

**उक्त - ukta**.

Neuter singular:

**उक्तम् - uktam**.

The form isn't *vacta*, because Sanskrit uses a different stem in this participial formation.

This is a useful irregular form to memorize.

### Śrīsomānandapādaiḥ - श्रीसोमानन्दपादैः

Let's separate it.

**श्री - śrī**

Venerable, auspicious, honoured.

**सोमानन्द - somānanda**

Somānanda.

**पाद - pāda**

Foot, but here an honorific element in referring to a revered teacher.

The instrumental plural:

**पादैः - pādaiḥ**.

Thus:

**श्रीसोमानन्दपादैः - śrīsomānandapādaiḥ**

By the venerable Somānanda.

The plural is honorific in this context; we don't have several Somānandas contributing different feet to the manuscript.

### Nijavivṛtau - निजविवृतौ

**निज - nija**

Own.

**विवृति - vivṛti**

Exposition, explanation, commentary.

Related to *vi-√vṛ*, to uncover or disclose.

The locative singular:

**विवृतौ - vivṛtau**.

Thus:

**निजविवृतौ - nijavivṛtau**

In his own commentary.

Now why is this important?

Because it locates the next interpretation within a specific textual lineage.

Somānanda is a foundational figure of nondual Śaivism.

His student Utpaladeva developed the systematic Pratyabhijñā philosophy.

Abhinavagupta inherits and elaborates their arguments.

And now, in this very passage, we can watch him engage directly with Somānanda's earlier treatment of the same root Tantra.

Not merely repeat it.

**Interpret it, preserve it and place it within a more extensive philosophical argument.**

The intellectual lineage is visible in the Sanskrit itself.

And the idea being transmitted is extraordinary.

Mātṛkā, the Mother of the letters, is both **transformed and untransformed**.

That sounds contradictory.

Which means we've reached the perfect starting point for another Abhinavagupta discussion.


## 2. विकृताविकृतरूपा मातृका - The alphabet that changes without ceasing to be itself

SHORT FRAGMENT ATTRIBUTED TO SOMĀNANDA'S VIVṚTI

**एषैव विकृताविकृतरूपा मातृका।**

*Eṣaiva vikṛtāvikṛtarūpā mātṛkā.*

"This very Mātṛkā has the nature of both the transformed and the untransformed."

Abhinavagupta introduces the fragment with the phonemic notation **अम् अ**, while the GRETIL transcription represents it as *aṃ a*. The two witnesses therefore differ in how the first element's nasal ending is written. Neither should be silently converted into an invented full Sanskrit verse. ; \]

Let's unpack the central words.

### Vikṛta - विकृत

Transformed, modified, altered.

From:

**वि - vi**

Apart, differently.

**√कृ - kṛ**

To do or make.

The past passive participle:

**विकृत - vikṛta**

Changed or transformed.

### Avikṛta - अविकृत

Untransformed.

The negative prefix:

**अ - a-**.

Thus:

**अविकृत - avikṛta**

Unmodified.

### Vikṛtāvikṛta - विकृताविकृत

Now join them.

*vikṛta + avikṛta → vikṛtāvikṛta.*

The adjacent **a + a** vowels become long **ā**.

The compound expresses both aspects.

Transformed.

Untransformed.

### Rūpā - रूपा

Having the nature or form of.

### Mātṛkā - मातृका

The divine Mother of phonemic manifestation.

Now here's the philosophical issue.

If she is transformed, how can she be untransformed?

Well, we must distinguish **the manifest differentiation** from **its ultimate conscious ground**.

The sounds are differentiated.

*A* is not pronounced identically to *ka*.

A vowel isn't a consonant.

A thought isn't an audible syllable.

One mantra has a different phonemic arrangement from another.

Those transformations belong to manifestation.

But Mātṛkā's ultimate nature isn't an independently divisible material that becomes another Absolute every time a new sound appears.

She remains grounded in the one consciousness.

**Modified as manifest form; unmodified in her supreme nature.**

That's the essential distinction.

### The divine alphabet's identity crisis

Imagine Mātṛkā arriving at a metaphysical identity interview.

"Are you unchanging?"

"Yes."

"Do you manifest changes?"

"Yes."

"Then which one are you?"

"Both, in different respects."

"That's not allowed."

"According to what?"

"The philosophy department's administrative form."

Mātṛkā proceeds to manifest the entire philosophy department.

The form is now part of the problem.

And that is precisely why the compound is powerful.

It refuses to equate manifestation with the production of a second ultimate substance.

### The distinction from ordinary material transformation

Now here's an important qualification.

Imagine melting ice into water.

The physical arrangement changes.

That's a material transformation.

Abhinavagupta isn't simply applying the same model to consciousness.

A physical substance has conditions, interactions, thermodynamic properties and measurable changes.

The supreme conscious reality in Trika isn't treated as another physical substance undergoing external modification.

The transformation is the **manifest articulation of its powers**.

So the distinction between *vikṛta* and *avikṛta* isn't a claim about the physics of ice.

It's a distinction about the relation of conscious manifestation to its nondual ground.

And now Somānanda's interpretation becomes more technical.

Because he offers alternative ways of identifying the first phoneme and Bindu within the root Tantra's description of the lunar days.

That's where the exegesis gets wonderfully aggressive.


## 3. Two ways of reading the first vowel and Bindu

Now Abhinavagupta explains Somānanda's alternatives.

The first reading identifies **अ - a** with Anuttara, and understands the lunar days through the following vowel-series beginning with **आ - ā**.

The second treats **अं - aṃ**, the nasalized phonemic form associated with Bindu, as the distinct starting reference, then interprets the vowel-series and Visarga accordingly.

These aren't two different root verses.

They are two exegetical mappings of the same phonemic wording.

And that distinction is essential.

### The first arrangement

# अ

Anuttara

# आ…

The subsequent vowel-powers as lunar days


# अं

Bindu-designated starting form

# अ…अः

Vowel-powers culminating in Visarga

The distinction is subtle.

In one interpretation, the initial *a* represents the unsurpassable reality prior to the differentiated lunar powers.

In the other, the Bindu-associated form is explicitly identified before the vowel sequence is interpreted through its culminating Visarga.

Both keep the vowel-powers related to the supreme conscious ground.

But they partition the phonemic expression differently.

### Anuttara - अनुत्तर

**अन् - an-**

Negative prefix.

**उत्तर - uttara**

Higher, superior, further.

Thus:

**अनुत्तर - anuttara**

That beyond which nothing is higher.

This is the supreme reality symbolized by *a*.

### Ākārādi - आकारादि

**आकार - ākāra**

The vowel *ā*.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning with.

Thus:

**आकारादि - ākārādi**

Beginning with *ā*.

Now be careful.

**आकार - ākāra**, the phoneme *ā*, must not be confused here with the ordinary noun **आकार - ākāra**, form or shape.

The identical written form has different relevant meanings in different contexts.

### Tithi - तिथि

Lunar day.

We've studied its traditional relation to the vowel-powers.

Now remember that the root Tantra's original verse does not give a numbered table explaining every one of these alternatives.

It presents the phonemic-lunar relationship compactly.

Somānanda is interpreting that relationship.

### Bindu - बिन्दु

Point, drop, concentrated power.

And:

**अङ्कार - aṃkāra** in the relevant phonemic designation.

Now this is important.

The nasalized *aṃ* is not merely an ordinary *a* followed by an arbitrarily inserted consonant.

Within the Tantric scheme, Bindu has a specific symbolic function.

And Somānanda's interpretation uses that function to reorganize the vowel-sequence.

### The deeper point

Now why would Abhinavagupta preserve both readings?

Because the root Tantra's wording is extremely compact.

Its phonemic references admit multiple meaningful arrangements.

And both can articulate the same underlying nondual structure.

But this doesn't mean any random reinterpretation is acceptable.

The alternatives are constrained by the actual Sanskrit phonemes and the established doctrinal system.

Somānanda's reading has to account for:

The first vowel.

The lunar days.

Bindu.

Visarga.

And their relation to Anuttara.

That's genuine exegetical work.

Not a licence to invent arbitrary meanings for anything.

### The divine calendar problem

Imagine a student asking:

"Master, does *a* represent Anuttara or belong to the tithi-series?"

Somānanda replies:

"Consider the first arrangement."

The student takes notes.

"Now consider another arrangement."

The student drops the pen.

"Which one is correct?"

"Both are interpretively possible within the articulated teaching."

The student stares at the vowels.

The vowels stare back.

One of them becomes Bindu.

This is why Sanskrit commentary can be simultaneously beautiful and absolutely exhausting.

But now Abhinavagupta does something even more interesting.

He says that the very conscious reality being expressed through these phonemic arrangements is what Somānanda teaches as:

**Spanda.**

And that changes the entire discussion.


## 4. स्पन्द - The movement that cannot go anywhere

Now comes the central statement.

ABHINAVAGUPTA REPORTING SOMĀNANDA'S TEACHING · P. 207

**स्पन्दनं च किञ्चिच्चलनम्।**

*Spandanaṃ ca kiṃciccalanam.*

"Spandana is a slight movement."

That's the deceptively simple definition.

And it's the beginning of one of the most interesting arguments we've encountered.

Because "slight movement" sounds straightforward.

A leaf trembles.

A candle flickers.

A muscle twitches.

The surface of water ripples.

Fine.

But what does "movement" mean when we're talking about universal consciousness?

If the Absolute is already everything, where is it supposed to go?

### Spanda - स्पन्द

Pulsation, throbbing, vibration, quivering or subtle movement, depending upon context.

From √**स्पन्द् - spand**, to throb or quiver.

Now learn the family:

**स्पन्दति - spandati**

It throbs or moves.

**स्पन्दन - spandana**

Throbbing, movement.

**स्पन्द - spanda**

Pulsation, dynamic stirring.

The first syllable contains the conjunct:

**स्प = स् + प**.

And the second:

**न्द = न् + द**.

So:

**स्पन्द - spanda**.

A simple word representing an extremely subtle philosophical doctrine.

### Kiṃcit - किञ्चित्

Something, a little, somewhat.

From the interrogative-indefinite pronoun family.

In the phrase:

**किञ्चिच्चलनम् - kiṃciccalanam**

A slight movement.

The sandhi is worth learning.

Underlying:

*kiṃcit + calanam.*

The final **त् - t** assimilates before **च - c**.

Thus:

**किञ्चिच्चलनम् - kiṃciccalanam**.

Notice the double **च्च**.

### Calana - चलन

Movement, motion.

From √**चल् - cal**, to move or shake.

The noun:

**चलन - calana**.

Neuter nominative or accusative singular:

**चलनम् - calanam**.

So the whole compound means a little movement or slight stirring.

### Now the problem

Imagine a pebble moving across a table.

At first it is here.

Then it moves there.

Its location changes.

This is ordinary spatial displacement.

Now imagine a wave spreading through water.

The wave propagates through a physical medium.

Again, ordinary movement.

But the supreme consciousness of Trika is not treated as a localized physical object.

It isn't sitting at one coordinate and travelling to another.

Space itself was just explained as a manifestation of the divine powers.

So if Spanda were ordinary movement from one place to another, the doctrine would become incoherent.

**The absolute ground of space cannot depend on already occupying one location and moving into an independently existing second location.**

And now Abhinavagupta gives exactly that objection.

He doesn't merely tell us to believe that divine movement is mysterious.

He tests the concept.

What counts as movement?

And what counts as slight movement?

He's about to put the whole vocabulary of motion on trial.


## 5. वस्त्वन्तराक्रमण - If Śiva goes somewhere else, where is “else”?

The passage poses a dilemma.

Suppose Spanda involves leaving one's own nature and entering a genuinely separate entity.

Then it is movement in the ordinary sense, but no longer the **slight, intrinsic stirring** under discussion.

Alternatively, suppose nothing whatsoever moves.

Then why call it movement at all?

The Sanskrit turns on three expressions.

**स्वरूप - svarūpa** - one's own nature.

**वस्त्वन्तर - vastvantara** - another thing or entity.

**आक्रमण - ākramaṇa** - entering, stepping into, occupying.

Now let's study them.

### Svarūpa - स्वरूप

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**रूप - rūpa**

Nature, form.

Thus:

**स्वरूप - svarūpa**

Own nature.

Now, in this context, the own nature of consciousness is not merely a visual appearance.

It is its intrinsic self-revealing reality.

### Vastu - वस्तु

Thing, entity, reality.

Related to √**वस् - vas**, to dwell or abide, in the established nominal family.

### Antara - अन्तर

Other, different; also interior or intervening in other contexts.

Now:

**वस्त्वन्तर - vastvantara**

Another entity.

The sandhi:

*vastu + antara → vastvantara.*

The final *u* becomes the glide *v* before the following vowel.

Thus:

**वस्त्वन्तर - vastvantara**.

### Ākramaṇa - आक्रमण

Entering, stepping into, occupying.

From:

**आ - ā**

Toward.

**√क्रम् - kram**

To step or proceed.

Thus:

**आक्रमण - ākramaṇa**.

Now join:

**वस्त्वन्तराक्रमण - vastvantarākramaṇa**

Entering or moving into another entity.

And here's the philosophical dilemma.

### Option A: Consciousness moves into something other than itself

Fine.

But what is this other thing?

If it is genuinely outside the Absolute, then the Absolute isn't all-encompassing.

It has an independently existing rival.

And now the supreme reality depends upon an external destination in order to perform its movement.

Not particularly supreme, then.

Moreover, the movement is no longer merely the inner stirring of consciousness.

It is the ordinary transition into something else.

### Option B: Consciousness doesn't move anywhere

Fine.

But if nothing happens in any meaningful sense, why describe it as Spanda?

What distinguishes living, expressive consciousness from inert immobility?

If movement is entirely absent, the word seems empty.

### The philosophical trap

The opponent has two apparently reasonable options.

Movement into another entity.

Or no movement.

And Abhinavagupta says neither captures the intended meaning.

This is the precise problem.

**Spanda is not ordinary external displacement, but it is also not inert immobility.**

It is an intrinsic expressive dynamism.

And that is why the next phrase returns us to the actual nature of consciousness.

The answer is found **within Svarūpa itself**.

Not outside it.

And now comes perhaps the most beautiful sentence of the passage.


## 6. चमत्कारात्मिका उच्छलत्ता - The marvellous upsurge within the Self

The resolution is that the movement belongs to consciousness itself, without requiring a temporal sequence that carries it into an alien substance.

The Sanskrit introduces:

**चमत्कारात्मिका - camatkārātmikā**

Having the nature of wondrous self-apprehension.

And:

**उच्छलत्ता - ucchalattā**

Upsurging, bursting forth, exuberant emergence.

The GRETIL transcription reads *ucchalattā*, whereas the Kashmir Series writes **उच्छलत्ता** with the same basic phonemic sense. The difference in transcription is minor compared with the doctrinal significance. ; \]

Let's dismantle the words.

### Camatkāra - चमत्कार

Wonder, astonishment, delighted revelation.

In Abhinavagupta's philosophical language, it can express the remarkable quality of consciousness's own self-awareness.

Not merely being startled by something unexpected.

### Ātmikā - आत्मिका

Having the nature of.

Feminine nominative singular.

Thus:

**चमत्कारात्मिका - camatkārātmikā**

Having the nature of wondrous self-apprehension.

### Ucchalattā - उच्छलत्ता

Upsurging, bubbling forth, bursting upward.

Associated with *ut-√chal* and the family of *ucchalana*, rising or surging.

Now this is a wonderful word.

Think of water surging.

Or a fountain welling up.

Or an excited thought suddenly bursting into expression.

The image conveys **dynamic manifestation**.

But it does not require the supreme conscious ground to travel from one independent substance to another.

### The great distinction: dynamism versus displacement

Now here's the central philosophical insight.

A movement can be described in relation to the **unfolding of a power**, rather than as the translation of a physical object between two spatial coordinates.

Consider an ordinary act of recognizing something.

At first, the object isn't explicitly identified.

Then recognition dawns.

There's a sudden manifestation of intelligibility.

Nothing requires consciousness itself to have physically travelled to another location.

The cognition has become manifest.

Now this is an analogy, not an equation between an ordinary mental event and universal divine freedom.

But it helps distinguish two concepts.

**Displacement:** A physical object changes location.

**Manifestation:** A power or content becomes expressed.

Spanda concerns the latter in the supreme nondual account.

And that's why Abhinavagupta uses *camatkāra*.

The dynamic is inseparable from awareness.

It is **awareness's wondrous self-expression**.

### The cosmic relocation office

Imagine Bhairava filling out a change-of-address form.

"Current address?"

"Everywhere."

"New address?"

"Everywhere."

"Reason for moving?"

"Spanda."

The clerk looks extremely confused.

"But you haven't changed location."

"Correct."

"Then how can there be movement?"

"Because the movement is the self-expression of my power, not a journey into another independently existing substance."

The clerk files the form under *Metaphysical Administrative Failures*.

And that is the entire dilemma.

**The Supreme does not need to relocate in order to manifest.**

This is one reason the modern shorthand "Spanda means vibration" can be misleading.

Vibration ordinarily suggests a process with measurable oscillations in space and time.

But Abhinavagupta is explicitly interpreting Spanda in a way that cannot be reduced to external physical displacement.

Now he introduces another word.

**Ūrmi.**

Wave.

And then a third.

**Matsyodarī.**

The fish's belly.

Yes.

We're going from the nondual Absolute to the internal movements of a fish.

And, remarkably, the metaphor helps.


## 7. ऊर्मि - The wave that never leaves the ocean

The Sanskrit uses:

**ऊर्मि - ūrmi**

Wave, billow, surge.

Now this is an ordinary Sanskrit word with extraordinary metaphorical usefulness.

### Ūrmi - ऊर्मि

Wave.

The initial vowel is long:

**ऊ - ū**.

And the word contains:

**र्मि = र् + मि**.

So:

**ऊर्मि - ūrmi**.

Now notice the image.

A wave is distinguishable.

It has a form.

It rises.

It curls.

It breaks.

But it isn't made from an independent substance utterly foreign to the ocean.

The wave is a mode of the water.

Now the analogy is limited.

An ordinary ocean has parts and physical processes.

Universal consciousness in Trika is not a physical fluid.

But the example illustrates the relation between **manifest differentiation** and **continuity of ground**.

### What makes the image philosophically useful?

Imagine a wave rising.

There is a new visible form.

But the substance hasn't arrived from an entirely separate ocean.

The wave appears within the water itself.

Now replace the physical image with the metaphysical relation.

Consciousness manifests differentiated forms.

But those forms don't require a second independently ultimate consciousness.

The diversity belongs to its own power.

And that's why the wave is a natural metaphor for Spanda.

### The important difference between a wave and Spanda

Physical waves propagate through time and space.

They involve energy, medium-dependent processes and measurable frequencies.

The supreme Spanda in Abhinavagupta's account is not simply one such process.

The analogy concerns the relationship between form and underlying reality.

Not an identity between divine consciousness and a physical wave equation.

This is important because the temptation to turn Sanskrit metaphysics into speculative physics is enormous.

"Spanda is a frequency."

"Śiva is a waveform."

"The universe vibrates at exactly this hertz value."

None of those propositions follows from the present passage.

The Sanskrit explicitly raises the problem of ordinary movement and rejects its straightforward application to the supreme reality.

So the image must be interpreted with that qualification.

**The wave illustrates intrinsic manifestation, not a measurable acoustic frequency of the Absolute.**

And now we meet the strangest metaphor.

One that Jaideva Singh connects with inward throbbing.

But which another scholarly discussion warns is historically and symbolically obscure.

**Matsyodarī.**


## 8. मत्स्योदरी - The mystery of the fish's belly

The third designation is:

**मत्स्योदरी - matsyodarī**.

Literally, fish-bellied or related to the belly of a fish.

Now this is genuinely attested in the passage as one of the Āgamic terms associated with Spanda.

But its precise traditional significance is not completely unambiguous.

That's worth emphasizing, because this is exactly the kind of term that can acquire an overconfident modern explanation.

### Matsya - मत्स्य

Fish.

Notice:

**त्स्य = त् + स् + य**.

The cluster looks complicated in Devanāgarī, but the pronunciation is manageable when you separate it.

**मत्स्य - matsya**.

### Udara - उदर

Belly, abdomen, interior.

Now the compound:

**मत्स्योदर - matsyodara**

Fish-belly.

Why the *o*?

Because:

*matsya + udara → matsyodara.*

The ordinary vowel sandhi:

**a + u → o**.

Now the feminine:

**मत्स्योदरी - matsyodarī**.

### The traditional image of internal movement

Jaideva Singh explains the term through the inward throbbing of a fish's belly, using it as an illustration of movement that does not require obvious external displacement.

That's an intelligible interpretation.

The fish may appear relatively still while internal movement continues.

So the image can suggest **hidden dynamism**.

And that matches the surrounding discussion of Spanda.

But there's an important scholarly qualification.

In *The Triadic Heart of Śiva*, Paul Eduardo Müller-Ortega discusses the related *matsyodara* imagery and explicitly notes that its precise significance is uncertain. He explores possible connections with Kaula and Matsyendra-related symbolism rather than treating a single explanation as definitively established. ; \]

So let's distinguish three things.

**What the Sanskrit attests:** The term *matsyodarī* appears among the designations of Spanda.

**A plausible traditional explanation:** It evokes subtle inward throbbing without obvious external motion.

**What remains uncertain:** The full historical and ritual meaning of the fish-belly symbolism across the relevant Kaula traditions.

That's how we avoid manufacturing certainty.

### The fish's very unusual philosophical career

Imagine a fish swimming peacefully.

A philosopher observes it.

"Ah. The belly moves."

He writes a Sanskrit treatise.

The fish continues swimming.

A thousand years later, another scholar announces:

"This fish demonstrates the metaphysics of the Supreme."

The fish has absolutely no idea.

It merely wants lunch.

But the metaphor works because it points toward a distinction between **external displacement** and **internal activity**.

And that's precisely the distinction Abhinavagupta is trying to establish.

Now, one final caution.

Ordinary biological movements of a fish's abdomen are physical events.

They are not themselves proof of nondual consciousness.

The metaphor is illustrative.

Not evidentiary in the scientific sense.

### Three names, one problem

We now have three descriptions:

| Sanskrit | IAST | Interpretive emphasis |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **उच्छलत्ता** | *ucchalattā* | Upsurging or bursting forth |
| **ऊर्मि** | *ūrmi* | Wave-like emergence |
| **मत्स्योदरी** | *matsyodarī* | Mysterious inward movement, traditionally associated with fish-belly imagery |

All three attempt to express **dynamism without independent external displacement**.

The images differ.

The underlying philosophical problem remains the same.

And now Abhinavagupta states that the Spanda in question is inseparable from Śiva and Śakti.

This is the next crucial move.

Because otherwise Spanda could become a third independent reality.

Śiva.

Śakti.

And some mysterious vibration floating between them.

Which would be a complete disaster for the nondual metaphysics.


## 9. शिवशक्तिरूपः - Spanda isn't a third thing between Śiva and Śakti

Now Abhinavagupta gives the doctrinal conclusion.

**स च शिवशक्तिरूपः सामान्यविशेषात्मा।**

*Sa ca śivaśaktirūpaḥ sāmānyaviśeṣātmā.*

"And that Spanda has the nature of Śiva and Śakti, of the universal and the particular."

This is an astonishingly compact statement.

Because it prevents us from treating Spanda as a separate substance.

There's no need for:

Śiva, the passive source.

Śakti, the active energy.

And a third independent vibration that somehow connects them.

In Trika, the distinction between Śiva and Śakti is a distinction within the one conscious reality.

And Spanda expresses its dynamic self-manifestation.

Let's learn the compound.

### Śiva - शिव

The auspicious Lord.

Within this doctrinal context, supreme self-luminous consciousness.

### Śakti - शक्ति

Power.

The capacity for manifestation and reflexive apprehension.

### Rūpa - रूप

Form, nature.

Thus:

**शिवशक्तिरूप - śivaśaktirūpa**

Having the nature of Śiva and Śakti.

Masculine nominative singular:

**शिवशक्तिरूपः - śivaśaktirūpaḥ**.

### Sāmānya - सामान्य

Universal, common, general.

Related to:

**समान - samāna**

Same, common.

In Indian logic, *sāmānya* can mean a universal shared by different particulars.

Here it also serves to explain the general, undifferentiated aspect of the divine power.

### Viśeṣa - विशेष

Particularity, difference, distinction.

From:

**वि - vi**

Apart, distinctly.

And the *viśeṣa* lexical family involving specification.

So:

**सामान्यविशेष - sāmānyaviśeṣa**

Universal and particular.

And:

**सामान्यविशेषात्मा - sāmānyaviśeṣātmā**

Having universal and particular aspects as its nature.

### The extraordinary philosophical synthesis

Consider the earlier dilemma.

If consciousness is wholly inert and undifferentiated, how can particular things manifest?

If consciousness becomes an independent collection of particular things, what remains of its nondual nature?

Spanda resolves the opposition by expressing the dynamic relation between universal self-revealing awareness and its particular manifestations.

**The universal doesn't have to disappear for the particular to appear.**

**The particular doesn't need to exist outside the universal in order to be distinguishable.**

And the movement between them isn't external displacement.

It is the self-articulation of consciousness.

### The colour analogy

Imagine a continuous field of light appearing through various colours.

Different colours become distinguishable.

But the underlying possibility of illumination hasn't been replaced by an independent universe of unrelated lights.

Now, the analogy has limits: physical light has wavelengths and measurable behaviour.

The supreme conscious light of Pratyabhijñā isn't a beam of photons.

But the distinction between universal manifestation and particular appearance is useful.

**Śiva-Śakti is one conscious reality considered through the inseparable aspects of self-luminosity and expressive freedom.**

And Spanda names its dynamism.

Not an additional cosmic machine.

### Why this matters for practice

It's easy to hear "Spanda" and immediately go looking for a special bodily vibration.

A tremor.

A pulse.

A ringing sound.

A sensation in the spine.

Those can be actual bodily experiences.

But they are not automatically identical with the supreme Spanda being explained here.

Abhinavagupta's argument concerns the very capacity for conscious self-manifestation.

Its reality isn't restricted to the occurrence of one unusual sensation.

And that gives the doctrine a more demanding philosophical character.

The question isn't simply:

"Can I feel something vibrating?"

It's:

**What is the nature of the conscious reality through which motion, stillness, sensation and their differences become manifest?**

Now that is far more interesting.

And it brings us directly back to the root Tantra.

Because Abhinavagupta has finished explaining Somānanda's interpretation of Spanda.

He now returns to the language of the original verses.

And reopens the question of how the lunar days, Bindu, Moon, Sun and phonemic powers are related.


## 10. The root Tantra returns - The lunar days, Bindu, Moon and Sun

Let's distinguish the three textual levels.

**The root Tantra** states the sacred phonemic-lunar relationship in concise metrical language.

**Somānanda** offers earlier interpretive mappings.

**Abhinavagupta** now draws those interpretations together through Spanda and returns to the root verses.

The first verse relevant to this episode is *Parātrīśikā* 5.

Its established wording includes the sequence:

**आद्यास्तिथयः - ādyās tithayaḥ**

The initial lunar days.

**स्वराः - svarāḥ**

The vowels.

**बिन्द्ववसानगाः - bindvavasānagāḥ**

Ending in Bindu.

And the pair:

**सोमसूर्यौ - somasūryau**

Moon and Sun.

The root verse locates these within the higher reality through their relation with time. The two Sanskrit witnesses agree on the key phonemic structure; they differ in minor conventions of presentation and the treatment of some compound readings elsewhere in the passage. ; \]

Now, we already studied the original verse in Parts XI-XIV.

What matters here is that Abhinavagupta is **reinterpreting it in the light of Spanda**.

The sounds aren't merely separate phonemes.

The lunar days aren't merely dates on a calendar.

And Moon and Sun aren't merely two independently ultimate astronomical bodies.

They're distinguished modes of the conscious power.

### Ādya - आद्य

Initial, first, primordial.

From:

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning.

The masculine nominative plural:

**आद्याः - ādyāḥ**.

Now it qualifies:

**तिथयः - tithayaḥ**.

The lunar days.

### Tithi - तिथि

Lunar day.

Nominative plural:

**तिथयः - tithayaḥ**.

Now note the difference between:

**तिथि - tithi**

And:

**तुटि - tuṭi**.

We studied both in Part XX.

A *tithi* is a lunar calendrical division.

A *tuṭi* is a minute temporal division in the relevant traditional system.

They're not interchangeable.

### Bindvavasānaga - बिन्द्ववसानग

Now the compound.

**बिन्दु - bindu**

Point, dot, concentrated power.

**अवसान - avasāna**

Termination, completion.

**ग - ga**

Going, reaching.

Thus:

**बिन्द्ववसानग - bindvavasānaga**

Reaching its conclusion in Bindu.

Now notice the vowel sandhi.

*bindu + avasāna → bindvavasāna.*

Final **u** becomes the semivowel **v** before the following vowel.

Then *-ga* completes the compound.

The masculine nominative plural:

**बिन्द्ववसानगाः - bindvavasānagāḥ**.

### Soma and Sūrya

**सोम - soma**

Moon, lunar principle.

**सूर्य - sūrya**

Sun, solar principle.

The compound:

**सोमसूर्य - somasūrya**

Moon and Sun.

Dual nominative:

**सोमसूर्यौ - somasūryau**.

Notice the dual ending.

Two members are being named.

Now the philosophical point.

The lunar and solar structures correspond to distinguished modes of the one power.

And the verse relates their manifestation to *kāla*, time.

But, as Abhinavagupta has already argued, time is itself an expression of the powers.

It does not stand outside consciousness as an independent creator.

Thus the temporal relation must be understood within Spanda.

### The circular-looking problem

Now here's the apparent difficulty.

The vowels give rise to temporal manifestation.

But the verse says Moon and Sun are related through time.

So which comes first?

The conscious powers?

Or the temporal structure?

Abhinavagupta's answer depends upon the distinction between ultimate nonsequence and manifest sequence.

The powers are intrinsically present within consciousness.

Their differentiated manifestation can be described through temporal order.

The chronological description doesn't require the supreme reality to have begun at an earlier time.

**The explanatory order is not an external clock governing Anuttara.**

And that is exactly why Spanda matters.

The inner expressive upsurge doesn't require another independent location or time in which to occur.

Now the commentary extends the interpretation beyond the lunar days.

All the *tattvas* belong within the phonemic powers.

From earth upward.

Through the five great divine principles.

The entire ontological system is being folded back into the vowels.

Again.

But this time the emphasis is on their ultimate inclusion within Spanda.


## 11. पृथिव्यादीनि - Earth and the five Brahmans remain inside the vowel-powers

Abhinavagupta proceeds to relate the *tattvas* beginning with earth to the powers represented by the vowels.

The key terms are:

**पृथिवी - pṛthivī**

Earth.

**ब्रह्मपञ्चक - brahmapañcaka**

The fivefold Brahman grouping.

And:

**स्वराणामन्तः - svarāṇām antaḥ**

Within the vowels.

Now this is another important return to the root Tantra.

Verses 6-7 connected the consonantal classes with the tattva system.

Abhinavagupta has already examined that in detail.

Here he is gathering the same structure under the broader reality of the vowel-powers.

### Pṛthivī - पृथिवी

Earth.

The Sanskrit noun has a long final **ī**.

Now remember the five gross elements:

Earth.

Water.

Fire.

Air.

Space.

They represent one region of the broader *tattva* hierarchy.

But the classification extends much further.

### Brahmapañcaka - ब्रह्मपञ्चक

**ब्रह्म - brahma**

Here a designation for the highest fivefold divine principles within the relevant Śaiva classification, rather than merely the creator-god Brahmā as an individual deity.

**पञ्चक - pañcaka**

A group of five.

Thus:

**ब्रह्मपञ्चक - brahmapañcaka**

The fivefold Brahman grouping.

Now the exact association of those five principles belongs to the Trika tattva scheme we've discussed previously.

The point here is their inclusion within the scope of the phonemic powers.

### Svarāṇām - स्वराणाम्

Of the vowels.

Genitive plural of:

**स्वर - svara**.

Now:

**अन्तः - antaḥ**

Within.

Thus:

**स्वराणामन्तः - svarāṇām antaḥ**

Within the vowels.

### How can the entire tattva system be within the vowels?

Not physically.

We're not being told that the written Devanāgarī character **अ** contains a miniature Earth in its ink.

The relationship is symbolic and metaphysical.

The vowel-powers express fundamental modes of Śakti.

The consonantal and tattva manifestations are grounded within those modes.

And their ultimate reality remains consciousness.

### The alphabetic nesting doll

Imagine a student studying the thirty-six *tattvas*.

They arrange them in a magnificent chart.

From Earth to Śiva.

Beautiful.

Then Abhinavagupta says:

"Excellent. Now put the entire chart inside the vowel-powers."

The student does so.

"Now put the vowel-powers inside Anuttara."

The student does so.

"Now remember that none of these divisions constitutes an independently existing second Absolute."

The student begins making tea.

A sensible decision.

But the nested structure expresses a crucial point.

**The differentiated ontology is a manifestation of powers whose ultimate ground is not itself one more differentiated object.**

And now comes an even stranger grammatical move.

Abhinavagupta asks how this happens.

The root Tantra answers:

**क्रमात् - kramāt**.

In sequence.

But he refuses to leave it there.

He turns the word into a second interpretation involving the *eating of sequence*.

A wonderful piece of Tantric *nirvacana*.

And we're about to see why it matters.


## 12. क्रमात् - In sequence, or having swallowed sequence?

Here Abhinavagupta returns to the root verse's word:

**क्रमात् - kramāt**.

Its straightforward grammatical sense is:

In order.

Successively.

According to the established sequence.

From:

**क्रम - krama**

Order, succession.

Ablative singular:

**क्रमात् - kramāt**.

This is the ordinary reading.

And it's entirely appropriate to the verse's organization of phonemic classes and *tattvas*.

But Abhinavagupta introduces another interpretation.

The Sanskrit invokes:

**क्रमस्यादनम् - kramasyādanam**

The eating of sequence.

And:

**कालग्रास - kālagrāsa**

The swallowing of time.

Now this is an important *nirvacana*.

Not an alternative historical grammatical derivation we should impose on ordinary Sanskrit usage.

It's a meaning-revealing interpretive play.

### Krama - क्रम

Sequence.

Now we've spent many installments distinguishing:

**क्रम - krama**

Sequence within manifestation.

**अक्रम - akrama**

Nonsequence at the supreme level.

And the present word brings them together.

### Adana - अदन

Eating, consuming.

From √**अद् - ad**, to eat.

Now:

**अदनम् - adanam**

Eating.

And:

**क्रमस्य - kramasya**

Of sequence.

Thus:

**क्रमस्यादनम् - kramasyādanam**

Eating of sequence.

### Kāla - काल

Time.

### Grāsa - ग्रास

Swallowing, mouthful, act of consuming.

Related to √**ग्रस् - gras**, to swallow.

Thus:

**कालग्रास - kālagrāsa**

Swallowing time.

### What is the point?

The root verse can describe the arrangement of powers sequentially.

But the same wording can be interpreted in relation to the power that **swallows sequence itself**.

And that's Spanda.

Not because movement is entirely absent.

But because the supreme conscious reality isn't constituted by the temporal succession through which its manifestations are described.

### The cosmic calendar gets eaten

Imagine Time arriving with an enormous ledger.

"First, Earth."

"Then Water."

"Then Fire."

"Then Air."

"Then Space."

"Then the higher *tattvas*."

A perfectly respectable sequence.

Now Śakti approaches.

"Lovely. Thank you."

And swallows the ledger.

The sequence hasn't become meaningless.

But it's been returned to the conscious ground that isn't itself dependent upon the ledger.

**The order of manifestation is intelligible; the supreme freedom is not governed by an independent order outside itself.**

That's why the *kramāt* interpretation is not merely a funny grammatical trick.

It's directly connected with the earlier Spanda dilemma.

If consciousness literally moves through successive independently existing objects, the supreme ground becomes dependent upon temporal sequence.

But if sequence itself belongs to its manifestation, the ultimate freedom remains *akrama*.

And that is what the second interpretation brings out.

### A crucial qualification

The alternative reading is **not** ordinary grammatical proof that the ablative singular *kramāt* always means "having swallowed time."

In normal Sanskrit, it doesn't.

Abhinavagupta is using phonemic and semantic interpretation within the Tantric exegetical tradition.

The distinction between ordinary grammar and doctrinal *nirvacana* must remain visible.

And now he gives similar treatment to another root-Tantra word.

**Suvrate.**

The Goddess is being addressed.

And the address itself becomes an occasion for discussing enjoyment, contraction and fullness.


## 13. सुव्रते - The Goddess's beautiful vow has two contrary faces

Now Abhinavagupta turns to a word that appears rather ordinary in the root Tantra:

**सुव्रते - suvrate**.

"O you of beautiful observances."

An address to the Goddess.

At first glance, that seems straightforward.

But Abhinavagupta uses the surrounding symbolism to explore the relation between enjoyment, contraction and fullness.

Let's learn the ordinary morphology first.

### Su - सु

Good, beautiful, auspicious, excellent.

A common prefix.

You've already encountered it in many Sanskrit compounds.

**सुख - sukha**

Happiness, ease, pleasantness.

**सुव्रत - suvrata**

One whose observance is good.

### Vrata - व्रत

Vow, observance, religious discipline, established undertaking.

Now:

**सुव्रत - suvrata**

Possessing a good vow.

The feminine:

**सुव्रता - suvratā**.

Vocative singular:

**सुव्रते - suvrate**.

Why **-e**?

Because feminine *-ā* stems often take *-e* in the vocative singular.

Compare:

**देवता - devatā**

Goddess.

**देवते - devate**

O Goddess.

Now the root verse directly addresses the Goddess as *suvrate*.

That's the normal grammatical reading.

### Abhinavagupta's further interpretation

The commentary then associates the address with the contrasting states of:

**भोग - bhoga**

Enjoyment, experience.

**रिक्तत्व - riktatva**

Emptiness, leanness or a diminished condition.

**भोगनिवृत्ति - bhoganivṛtti**

Cessation of enjoyment.

**पूर्णत्व - pūrṇatva**

Fullness.

These correspond to the alternating contracted and full conditions we've been studying.

The lunar-solar play hasn't disappeared.

It's being brought into the interpretation of the Goddess's address.

### Bhoga - भोग

Enjoyment, experience.

From √**भुज् - bhuj**, to enjoy or partake.

Notice that *bhoga* doesn't always mean worldly hedonism.

Within Tantric philosophical and ritual contexts, it can signify experience or enjoyment in a more technical sense.

### Riktatva - रिक्तत्व

**रिक्त - rikta**

Empty, lacking.

**-त्व - -tva**

Abstract condition.

Thus:

**रिक्तत्व - riktatva**

The condition of relative emptiness.

### Nivṛtti - निवृत्ति

Cessation, return, turning back.

From:

**नि - ni**

Down, inward.

**√वृत् - vṛt**

To turn or occur.

Thus:

**निवृत्ति - nivṛtti**

Withdrawal or cessation.

### Pūrṇatva - पूर्णत्व

Fullness.

From:

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa**

Complete.

With the abstract suffix:

**-त्व - -tva**.

Now the interpretive structure is beautiful.

The Goddess can appear in a relatively contracted mode.

And in a full mode.

The experience of manifestation and its withdrawal belong to her powers.

Neither condition establishes a second independent Goddess.

### The vow that nobody can put on a calendar

Imagine asking the Goddess:

"What is your observance?"

She replies:

"Manifestation."

"And what else?"

"Withdrawal."

"So which one is your true vow?"

"The freedom through which both appear."

The priest looks at his ritual timetable.

He's deeply concerned.

Because he's only allowed one appointment slot.

But this is exactly the difficulty the teaching is addressing.

The divine power isn't ultimately confined by one phase of manifestation.

The expression *suvrate* becomes an occasion to contemplate both relative leanness and fullness.

### An important philological limitation

We should distinguish the **ordinary vocative meaning** from the **Tantric interpretive elaboration**.

The word *suvrate* does not historically derive from all these terms concerning lunar fullness and the cessation of enjoyment.

Abhinavagupta is reading the theological implications of the Goddess's observances into the address.

That's traditional exegetical practice.

Not a replacement for ordinary Sanskrit morphology.

And now comes an even more radical example.

The root verse says:

**अमूला - amūlā**.

Without root.

Or perhaps:

**Rooted in अ - a.**

Yes.

Exactly the same written sequence permits two very different interpretations.

And Abhinavagupta wants both.

Because apparently the Absolute's root is simultaneously *a* and the absence of any prior root.

Welcome back to Tantric grammar.


## 14. अमूला - Rootless, yet rooted in *a*

Now we turn explicitly to *Parātrīśikā* 8.

The Kashmir Series root text begins:

**अमूला तत्क्रमाज्ज्ञेया।**

*Amūlā tatkramāj jñeyā.*

"She is to be understood as *amūlā*, according to that sequence."

The independent GRETIL transcription has **tatkramā jñeyā** rather than **tatkramāj jñeyā**. That difference concerns the final consonantal segmentation at the word boundary. We shouldn't silently erase it. ; \]

Now the crucial word.

### Amūlā - अमूला

This looks simple.

But Abhinavagupta exploits two possible analyses.

## अ + मूल

**A + mūla**

The letter *a* is her root.

## अ-मूला

**A-mūlā**

She has no prior root.

These interpretations look similar in transliteration.

But they're philosophically different.

Let's study each.

### First meaning: Akāramūlā - अकारमूला

Her root is the vowel **अ - a**.

Now remember the symbolism.

*A* represents Anuttara.

The supreme, unsurpassable conscious reality.

Thus:

**अकारमूला - akāramūlā**

Having *a* as her root.

The divine manifestation is rooted in Anuttara.

That is the first interpretation.

### Second meaning: Avidyamānamūlā - अविद्यमानमूला

Now the negative reading.

**अविद्यमान - avidyamāna**

Not existing.

**मूल - mūla**

Root, source, foundation.

Thus:

**अविद्यमानमूला - avidyamānamūlā**

Having no existing prior root.

Why?

Because:

**अनादित्वात् - anāditvāt**

Because of beginninglessness.

Now that's a magnificent word.

### Anādi - अनादि

**अन् - an-**

Without.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning.

Thus:

**अनादि - anādi**

Beginningless.

And:

**अनादित्व - anāditva**

The condition of being beginningless.

Ablative singular:

**अनादित्वात् - anāditvāt**

Because of beginninglessness.

Now the philosophical statement becomes clear.

The Goddess is rooted in *a*, Anuttara.

But Anuttara itself doesn't require a prior independent source.

So she can be called both:

Rooted in *a*.

And rootless.

**The supreme source isn't produced by an earlier source.**

### The infinite regress of cosmic roots

Imagine somebody asking:

"What is the root of the universe?"

"Anuttara."

"What's the root of Anuttara?"

"Anuttara is the unsurpassable conscious ground."

"But what made that?"

"If it required an earlier independent maker, it wouldn't be the unsurpassable ground."

"But what made the maker?"

And there we go.

An infinite regress.

The same logical problem we encountered in the discussion of linguistic convention.

If every explanatory ground requires an earlier independently existing ground, the explanation never reaches anything ultimate.

The Trika position identifies the self-revealing conscious reality as not requiring an external foundation.

That's why *amūlā* can mean rootless without implying sheer nothingness.

### The difference between rootlessness and nonexistence

This is essential.

**Amūlā does not mean the Goddess doesn't exist.**

It means she doesn't depend upon a prior source beyond Anuttara.

Similarly, *anādi* doesn't mean that no events occur.

It means that the ultimate conscious ground is not located at the beginning of an independently existing temporal series.

The distinction matters.

Because otherwise one could misread the philosophical claim as a doctrine of absolute annihilation.

Abhinavagupta is saying almost the opposite.

The supreme conscious reality is not founded upon an earlier thing.

And its own freedom is the source of manifested plurality.

### The third movement: sequence and its alternative

Now the commentary returns to *krama*.

The root verse contains a sequence.

But the Goddess can be described as having that sequence, while also being other than the form assigned to it.

This continues the argument of *Spanda*.

The differentiated order is real as manifestation.

But it doesn't constitute an independently ultimate temporal structure.

So *amūlā* can remain appropriate even when a sequence of phonemic powers is taught.

### Ātat - आतत्

Now here's another unusual word in Abhinavagupta's *nirvacana*.

He invokes the idea of extension or spreading forth.

The verbal family includes:

**आ - ā**

Forth, toward.

**√तन् - tan**

To stretch or extend.

Thus the interpretation associates the expansion of what is without a prior root with the manifestation of sequence.

The rootless reality unfolds into an articulated order.

But the order doesn't create a new ultimate root outside consciousness.

Now that is very close to the original Spanda argument.

**The one freedom can express differentiated succession without itself being produced by succession.**

And this is why the passage has not become a mere grammatical game.

The phonemic interpretations continue to illuminate the same metaphysical problem.

### Why this is not ordinary dictionary etymology

If you opened a Sanskrit dictionary and asked what *amūlā* means in normal prose, the basic negative sense "rootless" would be a natural answer.

The reading "rooted in *a*" is a deliberate phonemic interpretation using the sacred status of the vowel.

The two meanings belong to different levels of analysis.

And both are relevant to the Tantric commentary.

That is the precision we want.

Now here's where the argument becomes even more remarkable.

The root verse says she is **jñeyā**.

To be known.

And Abhinavagupta says:

She is also **ajñeyā**.

Not an object of ordinary knowing.

How can the Goddess be both knowable and unknowable?

The answer depends upon the distinction between knower and known.

And it is directly related to the supreme *Aham* we studied in Part XIX.


## 15. अज्ञेया ज्ञेया - The Goddess cannot be known as another thing, yet she is what is known

This is one of the most philosophically important moments of the passage.

The root text calls the divine manifestation:

**ज्ञेया - jñeyā**

To be known.

But Abhinavagupta adds:

**अज्ञेया - ajñeyā**

Not to be known as an ordinary object.

And explains:

**ज्ञातृरूपा - jñātṛrūpā**

Because she has the nature of the knower.

Now this is a magnificent paradox.

Let's learn the words.

### Jñā - ज्ञा

The verbal root meaning to know.

It's worth recognizing the conjunct:

**ज्ञ = ज् + ञ** in the traditional Devanāgarī analysis.

Although pronunciation varies substantially among modern Sanskrit-speaking traditions, IAST represents the conjunct as **jñ**.

### Jñeya - ज्ञेय

To be known, knowable.

The gerundive formation:

**ज्ञेय - jñeya**.

Feminine nominative singular:

**ज्ञेया - jñeyā**.

Why feminine?

Because it describes the Goddess or the divine creation.

### Ajñeyā - अज्ञेया

Not knowable in the relevant ordinary objectifying sense.

The negative prefix:

**अ - a-**.

Thus:

**अज्ञेया - ajñeyā**.

Now we must be careful.

Abhinavagupta isn't simply saying that the supreme reality can never be known in any sense.

That would conflict with the purpose of the teaching.

He's distinguishing ordinary object-knowledge from the self-revealing nature of consciousness.

### Jñātṛ - ज्ञातृ

Knower.

The agent noun from √**ज्ञा - jñā**.

Now:

**ज्ञातृरूपा - jñātṛrūpā**

Having the form of the knower.

And that's the key.

The Goddess is not merely one externally presented object among others.

She is the conscious power through which objects become known.

### The mirror problem

Imagine a mirror reflecting a pot.

The pot appears in the mirror.

Now imagine asking the mirror to reflect the reality that makes reflection possible.

In an ordinary physical mirror, that is a complicated optical problem.

But in the metaphor of self-revealing consciousness, awareness isn't merely a passive surface.

It possesses *vimarśa*, intrinsic self-apprehension.

Therefore it doesn't need to become a second independent object in order to be present to itself.

That's the distinction.

### Why she is ajñeyā

You cannot know the supreme conscious ground as though it were merely another object standing outside consciousness.

If it were genuinely outside consciousness, it could not be the universal conscious ground.

Thus, as an ordinary external *prameya*, she is *ajñeyā*.

Not objectifiable in that way.

### Why she is also jñeyā

But the commentary immediately explains that she is also what is known.

Why?

Because there is no ultimately independent other reality outside her.

The manifest objects are not completely foreign to consciousness.

And recognition of the conscious ground is possible.

So she's not simply an inaccessible void.

**The knower's own reality can be recognized without being transformed into an independent external object.**

Now here's the point.

The same Goddess can be called:

Unknowable as a separate object.

Knowable through self-recognition.

Those are not contradictions once the senses of knowing are distinguished.

### The cosmic epistemology interview

Imagine the Goddess being questioned.

"Are you knowable?"

"Yes."

"Can I put you on a table and examine you as a completely independent object outside awareness?"

"No."

"Then you're unknowable."

"Not in the sense that my own self-revealing reality is absent."

"Then how do I know you?"

"Examine the awareness through which knowing and known become manifest."

The philosopher looks at his notes.

He was expecting a noun.

He has received the structure of consciousness itself.

An understandable disappointment.

### The connection to Utpaladeva

Remember the definition from Part XIX:

**The repose of Prakāśa in itself is I-consciousness.**

That's exactly the philosophical background.

Self-revealing consciousness is not merely the knower of external objects.

Its own nature includes self-apprehension.

And therefore the supreme Goddess can be understood as both the conscious ground and the reality manifested through the knowable field.

That's why the root verse's *jñeyā* becomes such an important word.

Now the commentary turns to the next phonemic designation.

**Kṣāntā.**

Ending in *kṣa*.

Or perhaps something subtler.

Something to do with cessation, sensory limits and uninterrupted creation.

The poor compound is about to be interrogated from several directions.


## 16. क्षान्ता - From *a* to *kṣa*, or the ending that never ends

The root Tantra calls the divine manifestation:

**क्षान्ता - kṣāntā**.

Now the most immediately relevant phonemic interpretation is:

**क्ष - kṣa**

The final element of the traditional sacred alphabetic sequence.

And:

**अन्त - anta**

End, termination.

Thus:

*kṣa + anta → kṣānta.*

Because:

**a + a → ā**.

So:

**क्षान्ता - kṣāntā**

She who extends to or terminates in *kṣa*.

That's the straightforward Tantric interpretation.

The divine phonemic manifestation runs from the first vowel to the final alphabetic element.

But Abhinavagupta doesn't stop there.

He turns the word toward the question of **cessation**.

### Kṣānta - क्षान्त

The form can also be associated with the Sanskrit family of *kṣānta*, pacified, endured or brought to rest.

And the commentary invokes the idea of quiet, cessation and sitting silently.

Now here's the philosophical point.

The divine manifestation is not merely something that stops forever at an independently existing point.

The powers of creation, maintenance and reabsorption continue to be understood through her freedom.

So the word's apparent association with an "end" becomes an opportunity to deny that the supreme Goddess is exhausted by inert cessation.

### Tuṣṇīm - तूष्णीम्

Silently, quietly.

An indeclinable adverb.

Now compare:

**मौन - mauna**

Silence.

And:

**तूष्णीम् - tūṣṇīm**

Silently.

The latter is especially useful when reading Sanskrit prose.

### Āsana - आसन

Sitting, posture, seat.

From √**आस् - ās**, to sit.

Now the commentary uses the idea of sitting quietly to illuminate the possibility of cessation.

But, in the supreme Goddess, the expressive capacity isn't annihilated merely because a particular manifestation ceases.

### Avirata - अविरत

Unceasing, uninterrupted.

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**विरत - virata**

Stopped, ceased, withdrawn.

Thus:

**अविरत - avirata**

Uninterrupted.

Now the philosophical point is subtle.

A particular manifested event can cease.

But the supreme power isn't thereby rendered fundamentally nonexistent.

Its freedom includes both manifestation and reabsorption.

So the interpretation of *kṣāntā* cannot simply be:

"The Goddess reaches the final letter and then stops existing."

That would be absurd within this nondual system.

### The Goddess at the final letter

Imagine the divine alphabet progressing.

*A.*

*Ā.*

*I.*

All the vowels.

All the consonants.

Finally:

*Kṣa.*

The grammarian announces:

"Excellent. The universe is finished."

The Goddess replies:

"The alphabetic sequence has reached its end."

"So you've ceased?"

"No."

"Then why is there an end?"

"Because a manifested sequence can terminate without the conscious freedom underlying it becoming nonexistent."

The grammarian makes the dangerous decision to ask another question.

"What happens next?"

"Another manifestation."

The grammarian closes his notebook.

At this point, the entire grammar course has become a treatise on eternity.

### The second interpretation: Ākṣāntā

Now Abhinavagupta introduces another interpretive possibility involving:

**आक्ष - ākṣa**

Pertaining to the senses.

And:

**अन्त - anta**

End, limit, proximity or boundary.

The passage explicitly glosses the relevant *ākṣa* expression through:

**ऐन्द्रियिक - aindriyaka**

Pertaining to sensory experience.

Now, this is not simply the ordinary alphabetic *kṣa + anta* interpretation.

It is a further semantic analysis.

### Akṣa - अक्ष

Sense-organ, eye, axle or other meanings according to context.

In the present passage, the relevant sense is associated with sensory manifestation.

### Aindriyaka - ऐन्द्रियिक

Pertaining to the senses.

Related to:

**इन्द्रिय - indriya**

Sense-faculty.

Thus:

**ऐन्द्रियिक - aindriyaka**

Sensory.

Now the adjective:

**ऐन्द्रियिकानाम् - aindriyakānām**

Of sensory entities or faculties.

Genitive plural.

### Anta - अन्त

End, limit, boundary, near relation in certain interpretations.

Abhinavagupta connects the Goddess with the limit or proximity of the sensory field.

But the philosophical point is not that she is confined to the senses.

Rather, sensory manifestation reaches its intelligible condition through the same power.

### The interpretive distinction

So we have several layers.

**Kṣāntā:** The divine phonemic expression reaches *kṣa*.

**Kṣānta:** A semantic association with cessation or quietness, qualified by the Goddess's uninterrupted expressive power.

**Ākṣāntā:** A further interpretation relating her to the limit or proximity of sensory manifestation.

These shouldn't be collapsed into one alleged ordinary dictionary definition.

They're separate levels of Tantric *nirvacana*.

And their point is to show that the Goddess is simultaneously:

The phonemic source.

The completion of the phonemic sequence.

The power of sensory manifestation.

And the reality not exhausted by the cessation of any one manifestation.

### One more important distinction

The fact that Abhinavagupta offers multiple interpretations doesn't mean the Sanskrit is infinitely ambiguous.

The readings are constrained by particular phonemic elements, compounds and doctrinal associations.

The text isn't saying any word can mean absolutely anything.

It's demonstrating a specific interpretive method.

And now, having considered *amūlā* and *kṣāntā*, Abhinavagupta returns to the word that describes the entire process.

**Sṛṣṭi.**

Creation.

But here creation is not simply an event happening at the beginning of ordinary time.

It's the bringing forth of manifestation through conscious freedom.

And it is inseparable from reabsorption.


## 17. सृष्टि - Creation as the bringing forth of what has never been externally separate

The passage explains *sṛṣṭi* through an expression involving:

**अपूर्वम् आहरणम्**

*Apūrvam āharaṇam.*

The bringing forth of something not previously manifested in that form.

Now let's learn the terms.

### Sṛṣṭi - सृष्टि

Creation, emission, manifestation.

From √**सृज् - sṛj**, to emit, release or create.

The noun:

**सृष्टि - sṛṣṭi**.

Feminine.

Now remember the root Tantra's phrase:

**सृष्टिरुदाहृता - sṛṣṭir udāhṛtā**

It is declared to be creation.

The passive participle:

**उदाहृता - udāhṛtā**

Stated, cited, declared.

Feminine nominative singular.

### Apūrva - अपूर्व

Not previously existing or unprecedented.

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**पूर्व - pūrva**

Earlier, preceding.

Thus:

**अपूर्व - apūrva**

Unprecedented, not previously manifest.

Now we must be careful.

Here the term doesn't establish that an independently ultimate entity is created from absolute nothingness.

The supreme conscious ground remains one.

The unprecedented character concerns the particular manifestation.

### Āharaṇa - आहरण

Bringing, conveying, drawing forth.

From:

**आ - ā**

Toward.

**√हृ - hṛ**

To bring or carry.

Thus:

**आहरण - āharaṇa**

Bringing forth.

Now combine:

**अपूर्वम् आहरणम् - apūrvam āharaṇam**

Bringing forth what was not previously manifest in that particular form.

### The difference between manifestation and an external manufacturing process

Imagine building a pot.

You need clay.

Tools.

Time.

Physical conditions.

The pot is produced through changes in material arrangements.

That's an ordinary act of manufacturing.

Abhinavagupta's supreme *sṛṣṭi* isn't simply the same process performed on a larger scale by an invisible craftsman.

It concerns the manifestation of the world within the freedom of consciousness.

And this is why the act of bringing forth doesn't require a second independent ontological source.

The manifestation is differentiated.

Its ultimate reality is not foreign to consciousness.

### Now the reverse: Saṃhāra

**संहार - saṃhāra**

Gathering in, reabsorption, withdrawal.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Together.

**√हृ - hṛ**

To gather or carry.

Now the passage describes the inward return through:

**स्वात्मानुप्रवेश - svātmānupraveśa**.

Entry into one's own Self.

### Svātman - स्वात्मन्

One's own Self.

### Anupraveśa - अनुप्रवेश

Entering into.

Thus:

**स्वात्मानुप्रवेश - svātmānupraveśa**

Entering into one's own nature.

Now we can see the structure.

**Sṛṣṭi:** Manifestation, bringing forth.

**Saṃhāra:** Return, inward reabsorption.

Both belong to the same conscious reality.

And that's why the universe can be considered through emission and withdrawal without requiring two independent supreme causes.

### The cosmic object has never been lost

Imagine a magician producing an object.

Then making it disappear.

The audience sees:

Appearance.

Disappearance.

But the magician's capacity to perform the act isn't created only at the moment of appearance, nor necessarily annihilated when the object disappears.

The analogy is limited because ordinary magic involves physical techniques.

But the structural distinction is useful.

**A power can manifest an appearance and withdraw it without being exhausted by either state.**

And that's the role of Śakti.

Now, the *Parātrīśikā* doesn't stop at describing creation generally.

It says this divine manifestation is the source or matrix of **all mantras and all vidyās**.

And the commentary interprets their distinct Śiva-Śakti characters.

That's the next movement.


## 18. मन्त्र and विद्या - The masculine seed and the Śākta matrix

The root Tantra says that this manifestation is the source of:

**मन्त्र - mantra**

Mantras.

And:

**विद्या - vidyā**

Vidyās, sacred formulas or powers of knowledge.

Abhinavagupta explains them through the familiar relation between:

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

And:

**योनि - yoni**

Generative matrix.

Now the text has returned to the symbolism from Part XVI.

Śiva's seed.

Śakti's matrix.

Their union as the condition of differentiated manifestation.

But there's an important technical distinction.

The terms *mantra* and *vidyā* are not always used as rigidly separated masculine and feminine categories across every Sanskrit religious tradition.

The present passage interprets them through a particular Śaiva-Śākta framework.

### Mantra - मन्त्र

A sacred formula, a phonemic power, or-in certain Śaiva cosmological contexts-a class of divine conscious being.

Here the root Tantra is discussing the manifestation of mantras through the divine phonemic matrix.

Now Abhinavagupta invokes the traditional explanation:

**मनन - manana**

Thinking, contemplation.

And:

**त्राण - trāṇa**

Protection, deliverance.

### Manana - मनन

Contemplation, reflection.

From √**मन् - man**, to think.

The noun:

**मनन - manana**.

### Trāṇa - त्राण

Protection, rescue.

From √**त्रै - trai**, to protect or save.

Thus:

**त्राण - trāṇa**.

The compound:

**मननत्राण - mananatrāṇa**

Contemplation and protection.

Now here's the philological distinction.

The familiar explanation of *mantra* through contemplation and protection is a **traditional semantic derivation**.

We should not automatically treat it as a complete historical linguistic analysis of every use of the word *mantra*.

But it is relevant to the way Abhinavagupta is interpreting mantric power.

### Vidyā - विद्या

Knowledge, sacred knowledge, divine formula or Goddess-power.

From √**विद् - vid**, to know.

Now remember the philosophical association between:

**विद्या - vidyā**

Knowledge.

And:

**वेदना - vedanā**

Cognition, awareness or experience, depending upon context.

Abhinavagupta connects the *vidyās* with forms of consciousness arising through the divine seed and Śakti's generative power.

### Bīja - बीज

Seed.

In Tantra, *bīja* can designate a concentrated sacred phonemic power.

But the ordinary word also means an actual seed.

Its theological use depends upon the specific interpretive tradition.

### Yoni - योनि

Womb, source, generative matrix.

Now the symbolism becomes clear.

Mantra is interpreted through the Śiva-like seed principle.

Vidyā through the Śakti-like generative power.

And both belong ultimately to the same phonemic consciousness.

### The metaphysical distinction

Now don't imagine that an actual seed is physically deposited in a written Sanskrit consonant every time somebody chants.

The symbolism concerns the relation between generative power and differentiated manifestation.

Śiva and Śakti aren't two independently ultimate substances.

Their relation is a mode of expressing the self-revealing dynamism of consciousness.

And the mantra-vidyā distinction belongs within that wider structure.

### The divine pronunciation factory

Imagine a cosmic factory manufacturing mantras.

Department one:

Śiva-seed.

Department two:

Śakti-matrix.

Department three:

Phonemic assembly.

Department four:

Quality control.

The inspector asks:

"Where did the original power come from?"

"Anuttara."

"And what does the matrix add?"

"Differentiated expression."

"And what happens if someone treats the produced mantra as independent of the conscious ground?"

"Then they've mistaken the expression for the source."

The inspector looks at the forms.

Everything is technically correct.

Except there is no independently existing factory.

The whole operation belongs to Śakti.

That's the point.

**Mantras and Vidyās are not independent ultimate powers outside the conscious reality through which they manifest.**

And this is why the root Tantra can call the divine manifestation the source of all of them.

Now the commentator broadens the point.

Not merely all mantras.

Not merely all vidyās.

But all Tantras.

All activities.

All times.

And the entire group of *siddhis* described by the tradition.

The Goddess is called **samā**.

Equal.

Complete.

Neither deficient nor excessive.

And that's the final doctrinal statement before the discussion shifts toward Māyic differentiation.


## 19. समा - The Goddess is neither deficient nor excessive

The commentary describes the divine power as:

**समा - samā**.

Equal, uniform, complete in the relevant sense.

And then explains:

**अनूनाधिका - anūnādhikā**.

Neither deficient nor excessive.

Now this is a splendidly economical expression of fullness.

Let's learn it.

### Sama - सम

Equal, even, balanced, the same.

Feminine nominative singular:

**समा - samā**.

### Ūna - ऊन

Deficient, lacking, less than complete.

Now:

**अनून - anūna**

Not deficient.

### Adhika - अधिक

Excessive, additional, greater.

The compound:

**अनूनाधिक - anūnādhika**

Neither lacking nor excessive.

Feminine:

**अनूनाधिका - anūnādhikā**.

Now the important point.

The supreme Goddess isn't incomplete because one Tantra describes a particular ritual arrangement differently from another.

Nor does she become more complete because somebody adds another category or phonemic power.

Her ultimate nature is full.

And that is exactly what Abhinavagupta told us back in Part XVIII.

**Pūrṇatā is the essential principle.**

The enumerations belong to manifestation.

They don't manufacture the fullness of the source.

### Tantra - तन्त्र

Now the text broadens the discussion to all Tantras and associated operations.

The word *tantra* belongs to the √**तन् - tan**, to stretch or extend, family.

It can refer to a system, framework, treatise or ritual tradition.

Here the plural concerns the different scriptural and practical systems in which the divine powers become manifest.

### Kriyā - क्रिया

Action, operation.

From √**कृ - kṛ**, to do or make.

The commentary extends the Goddess's power across all activities and all times.

### Siddhisaṅgha - सिद्धिसङ्घ

Now here's another useful compound.

**सिद्धि - siddhi**

Accomplishment, attainment, success; in Tantric literature sometimes an extraordinary power.

**सङ्घ - saṅgha**

Collection, group, multitude.

Thus:

**सिद्धिसङ्घ - siddhisaṅgha**

A collection of accomplishments or powers.

The root Tantra attributes the production of such attainments to the divine matrix.

That's a traditional religious claim.

It does not mean every extraordinary siddhi described in Tantric literature has been independently verified as a practical ability.

Our purpose is to understand the metaphysical and ritual teaching accurately.

### The Goddess's extraordinary completeness

Now imagine several scriptural traditions offering their arrangements of mantras.

One describes a particular Goddess through a group of phonemes.

Another uses a different configuration.

A third arranges powers according to a distinct ritual procedure.

The names differ.

The phonemes differ.

The classifications differ.

But Abhinavagupta's claim is that the supreme conscious ground isn't constituted by any one independently ultimate arrangement.

The Goddess remains full.

And that is what *samā* expresses.

**Different systems can articulate different manifestations without multiplying the ultimate conscious reality.**

Now, the ordinary details of the systems still matter.

A particular ritual formula cannot simply be replaced with arbitrary letters without changing its traditional identity.

But the ultimate metaphysical fullness doesn't depend upon a single enumeration.

That's the balance.

And now comes a striking final wordplay.

The Goddess is **akhyātā**.

Not manifestly named or disclosed.

Yet she is **khyātā**.

Manifest.

And the differentiated phonemes emerge through a condition called **akhyāti**.

The similarity of the words is deliberate.

But their philosophical roles are distinct.

And this is where the next major section begins.


## 20. अख्याता, ख्याता, अख्याति - The same power, now obscured by Māyic differentiation

We have reached Kashmir Series p. 209.

The Sanskrit introduces:

**अख्याता - akhyātā**

Not explicitly manifested, not declared or known.

And:

**अप्रकटा - aprakaṭā**

Unmanifest, not made outwardly evident.

Then the discussion turns to:

**अख्याति - akhyāti**

Nonrecognition, failure of apprehension, ignorance.

Now this is the threshold between **the pure phonemic powers** and **their differentiated Māyic manifestation**.

The wording requires care.

### Khyā - ख्या

To declare, make known, appear or become manifest.

Now:

**ख्यात - khyāta**

Known, declared, manifest.

Feminine:

**ख्याता - khyātā**.

### Akhyātā - अख्याता

Not known or not outwardly manifest in a given respect.

The negative prefix:

**अ - a-**.

Thus:

**अख्याता - akhyātā**.

### Prakaṭa - प्रकट

Manifest, apparent, revealed.

Now:

**अप्रकट - aprakaṭa**

Unmanifest.

Feminine:

**अप्रकटा - aprakaṭā**.

### Akhyāti - अख्याति

Nonrecognition or failure of apprehension.

This is philosophically important.

In Pratyabhijñā, the problem of bondage is not simply that consciousness has physically ceased to exist.

It is that its own universal nature is not recognized.

The differentiated manifestations become apprehended as though they were fundamentally separate from the conscious ground.

That's where *akhyāti* becomes relevant.

### Māyīya - मायीय

Belonging to Māyā, to the differentiating and limiting domain.

Now the phrase:

**अख्यातिरूपतां मायीयाम्**

*Akhyātirūpatāṃ māyīyām.*

The Māyic condition of nonrecognition.

And then:

**भेदो वर्णानाम्**

*Bhedo varṇānām.*

The differentiation of phonemes.

This is the explicit transition. ; \]

### Bheda - भेद

Division, distinction.

From √**भिद् - bhid**, to split or divide.

The masculine nominative singular:

**भेदः - bhedaḥ**.

### Varṇānām - वर्णानाम्

Of phonemes.

Genitive plural of:

**वर्ण - varṇa**.

Now here's the key philosophical distinction.

The same divine phonemic powers can be understood at the pure mantric level as expressions of universal consciousness.

But under Māyic differentiation, they become involved in the experience of limitation, difference and nonrecognition.

This is not the claim that the physical sounds suddenly cease to exist.

It's a claim about their mode of manifestation and apprehension.

### The difference between a phoneme and its limiting identification

Take a Sanskrit sound.

**क - ka**.

At the ordinary linguistic level, it's a phoneme with a particular place and manner of articulation.

It can appear in many words.

It may participate in a sacred mantra.

Now the Tantric interpretation distinguishes the divine power represented by the phoneme from its limited outward manifestation.

The same sound-form can be approached through different levels of understanding.

But its ordinary acoustic identity and the supreme Śakti it symbolizes are not simply interchangeable concepts.

The philosophical account depends on that distinction.

### Where we stop

Abhinavagupta now begins the next argument by saying that the very phonemes previously understood as pure mantra-powers first enter a manifestation of cognitive states associated with error, incapacity and other differentiating conditions.

The crucial vocabulary is:

**शुद्धमन्त्ररूपा वर्णाः**

*Śuddhamantrarūpā varṇāḥ.*

Phonemes having the nature of pure mantra.

Then:

**भावसृष्टि - bhāvasṛṣṭi**

The creation of cognitive or mental states.

And the resulting concealment of their own nature.

This is the exact beginning of the next genuine unit, continued across pp. 209-210.

The subsequent passage introduces a traditional classification of fifty cognitive dispositions involving error, incapacity, satisfaction and accomplishment, with extensive square-bracketed editorial elaboration drawn from Sāṃkhya materials.

That deserves a separate reading.

We stop **before** its full enumeration.

The present movement-from Somānanda's Mātṛkā to Spanda, through the alternative interpretations of the root Tantra, and into the threshold of Māyic differentiation-is complete.

### Four Devanāgarī patterns worth writing

**1. The central paradox of Spanda**

## स्पन्दनं च किञ्चिच्चलनम्

*Spandanaṃ ca kiṃciccalanam.*

Watch the conjuncts **स्प**, **न्द** and **च्च**.


**2. Transformed and untransformed**

## विकृत - अविकृत

*Vikṛta - avikṛta.*

One negative prefix changes the meaning, while the philosophical interpretation requires both.


**3. Two senses of *amūlā***

## अकारमूला - अमूला

*Akāramūlā - amūlā.*

Rooted in *a*; without a prior independent root.


**4. The knower and the known**

## ज्ञाता - ज्ञेयम् - ज्ञानम्

*Jñātā - jñeyam - jñānam.*

Knower, knowable object, knowledge. Three forms from the √**ज्ञा - jñā** family.


## 23. The final insight - The movement never needed somewhere else to go

Now we can finally answer the question that began this installment.

**How can the supreme consciousness move if there is nowhere outside it?**

The difficulty arises because we ordinarily understand movement through space.

Something is here.

Then somewhere else.

The object changes location.

But the supreme consciousness of Trika isn't one object situated inside an independently existing space.

It is the ultimate ground through which spatial differentiation itself becomes manifest.

Therefore ordinary displacement cannot define its dynamism.

And yet, Abhinavagupta refuses to treat supreme consciousness as an inert blank.

Its nature is self-revealing.

Reflexive.

Free.

Capable of manifestation.

And that power is expressed through **Spanda**.

Not a journey from one independent substance into another.

But an intrinsic upsurge.

A wave-like manifestation.

The marvellous movement of consciousness within its own nature.

Now here's the extraordinary connection.

The same principle governs the entire Sanskrit alphabet.

The divine Mātṛkā can be described as transformed.

Because distinct sounds become manifest.

And untransformed.

Because her supreme conscious ground doesn't become divided into independent ultimate substances.

The root Tantra can describe an ordered phonemic sequence.

And Abhinavagupta can interpret the swallowing of that sequence.

The Goddess can be rooted in *a*.

And rootless.

She can be knowable.

And not an independently objectifiable thing.

She can manifest the entire universe.

And repose within herself.

These aren't arbitrary contradictions.

They arise from distinguishing the **supreme conscious ground** from its **differentiated expressions**.

And that's why the text keeps returning to *svātantrya*.

Freedom.

The power of manifestation isn't forced upon Śiva by an external cause.

It belongs to his own conscious nature.

Now think about an ordinary experience.

A thought arises.

You recognize something.

A feeling appears.

An image changes.

And yet awareness hasn't necessarily travelled to a different spatial location.

There has been manifestation.

Transformation.

Apprehension.

Now those ordinary cognitive events are not identical in power or scope with the universal Spanda.

But they illustrate the distinction.

**Change in what appears need not be described as the spatial relocation of awareness itself.**

And that is why the doctrine can be both philosophically rigorous and contemplatively interesting.

It asks us to examine not merely *what* appears, but the conscious reality through which appearing is possible.

The wave.

The upsurge.

The mysterious fish-belly image.

All are attempts to illuminate that extraordinary dynamism.

And at the deepest level, the conscious ground hasn't gone anywhere.

It didn't need to.

**Spanda is not the Absolute travelling to become something else. It is the freedom through which the Absolute manifests its own possibilities without ceasing to be itself.**

That's the heart of Part XXII.

And now the text reaches a darker question.

If the same pure phonemic powers are present throughout consciousness, how do they become associated with limited cognition?

How does the sound-body of divine freedom become the basis of error?

Of ignorance?

Of bondage?

And how does the very capacity for meaningful words become implicated in misrecognition?

That's the next movement.

The pure alphabet becomes entangled with Māyā.

The Goddess has not lost her power.

But the finite knower no longer recognizes the fullness of its source.

And Abhinavagupta is about to explain that concealment through the classical categories of cognition, including the extraordinary claim that **no determinate cognition arises without the penetration of speech**.

The alphabet is about to become bondage.

And then, perhaps, the means of recognizing freedom again.
