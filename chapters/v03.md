PART III - The Heart of Bhairava, the Secret of Kaulika Siddhi, and the Goddess Who Rules Your Mind

Sixteen.

Abhinavagupta has spent the better part of an entire philosophical expedition explaining why the unsurpassable cannot be surpassed.

One question.

## 1. Anuttara versus Anuttama - The grammar police have arrived

Before declaring the sixteenfold exposition complete, Abhinavagupta confronts a grammatical problem.

Why **anuttara**?

Why not **anuttama**?

And why does this matter to supreme consciousness?

Welcome to the most characteristically Abhinavaguptan argument imaginable: the difference between a comparative and a superlative adjective has become a question about the ultimate structure of existence.

### The Sanskrit laboratory

# उत्तर

### uttara

Higher, further, subsequent

# उत्तम

### uttama

Highest, best, foremost

Sanskrit uses the grammatical suffixes **तरप् - tarap** and **तमप् - tamap** to form comparatives and superlatives.

For example:

- **शुक्ल - śukla:** white.
- **शुक्लतर - śuklatara:** whiter.
- **शुक्लतम - śuklatama:** whitest.

Now here's the subtlety.

The comparative form ordinarily involves comparison with a particular alternative.

The superlative selects something as outstanding within a comparison class.

But Abhinavagupta argues that the latter does not introduce some entirely different quantity of superiority. Much of the distinction concerns how the comparison is expressed and whether a particular comparator is specified.

He even takes a swing at the loose grammatical reasoning that treats every comparative expression as an endlessly increasing series.

This is the same man who will derive extraordinary metaphysical significance from sacred phonemes, now accusing other people of showing off their half-digested grammatical knowledge.

Absolutely magnificent.

### The word pratiyogin

**प्रतियोगिन् - pratiyogin**

A counterpart, correlate, or something against which another thing is compared.

Let's break it down.

*Prati-* commonly indicates opposition, correspondence, return, or relation towards something.

*Yogin* is formed from *yoga*, connection, and a possessive suffix.

But here *pratiyogin* is a technical term: a comparison partner or counter-correlate.

If I declare:

"This mango is sweeter than that mango."

The second mango functions as the comparator.

Fine.

But what if I declare:

"Anuttara is superior to every other ultimate reality."

We've already assumed there might be other independently ultimate realities to compare it against.

Which is precisely what the nondual ontology denies.

If the Absolute were merely one entity ranked above a collection of other ultimate entities, it would already be delimited by that collection.

Abhinavagupta refuses to let his metaphysics become a cosmic fruit competition.

### The grammatical forms in context

**अनुत्तरम् - anuttaram**

The unsurpassable.

**अनुत्तमम् - anuttamam**

A negated superlative expression that Abhinavagupta allows here with the same intended philosophical referent, despite its different grammatical form.

He explicitly says that *anuttamam* can convey the same meaning in this context.

The difference isn't that one names an inferior supreme reality and the other names a superior supreme reality.

There aren't two competing Absolutes.

And then he cites another Āgama expressing a paradox: the supreme cannot be adequately known even by accomplished knowers, yet is not simply unknown to anyone.

Why?

Because it isn't a particular object whose existence depends upon your successfully identifying it.

The entire distinction between being known and unknown already occurs within consciousness.

This is the trick.

You cannot step outside awareness to inspect awareness as though it were a suspicious object sitting on a table.

But neither can you have an experience wholly disconnected from the awareness in which it appears.

Now Abhinavagupta invokes Utpaladeva.

Utpaladeva distinguishes ordinary activity, which proceeds successively under time, from the eternal divine power of activity, which is not bound to succession in the same way.

So we arrive at the conclusion of Part II:

**Anuttara has the nature of unrestricted divine freedom, whose power of activity cannot be reduced to ordinary temporal processes.**

The grammar has led us back into metaphysics.

And Abhinavagupta, having successfully interrogated the suffixes, finally announces that the sixteenfold explanation is complete. ; \]


## 2. The Heart-knot - Sixteen ways to understand one astonishing reality

Now comes an actual verse cited from the **Trikasāraśāstra**.

ATTESTED SANSKRIT - TRIKASĀRAŚĀSTRA, QUOTED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA

**अनुत्तरं तद्धृदयं हृदये ग्रन्थिरूपता।**

**ग्रन्थिं षोडशधा ज्ञात्वा कुर्यात्कर्म यथासुखम्॥**

*Anuttaraṃ tad hṛdayaṃ hṛdaye granthirūpatā.*

*Granthiṃ ṣoḍaśadhā jñātvā kuryāt karma yathāsukham.*

A fairly literal rendering:

Anuttara is that Heart; in the Heart there is the condition of a knot. Having known the knot in sixteen ways, one may act according to one's ease or pleasure.

Now that's an extraordinary little teaching.

Not because it says there's a knot inside your physical heart.

And not because sixteen is some supernatural lock combination that opens a portal.

But because the verse connects **the supreme Heart, the knot of manifestation, understanding, and freedom of action**.

### Word by word

| Sanskrit | Grammar | Literal meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *anuttaram* | Neuter singular | Unsurpassable reality |
| *tad* | Demonstrative | That |
| *hṛdayam* | Neuter singular | Heart |
| *hṛdaye* | Locative singular | In the Heart |
| *granthi* | Masculine noun | Knot |
| *rūpatā* | Abstract feminine noun | Condition of having a form |
| *granthim* | Accusative singular | The knot |
| *ṣoḍaśadhā* | Indeclinable adverb | In sixteen ways |
| *jñātvā* | Absolutive | Having known |
| *kuryāt* | Optative verb | One should or might do |
| *karma* | Neuter accusative | Action |
| *yathāsukham* | Adverbial expression | According to ease or pleasure |

Now we're going to do the thing you like: understand the small grammatical machinery rather than simply memorizing English equivalents.

### Hṛdaya - हृदय

**हृदय - hṛdaya**

Heart.

Look at the writing:

**हृ = ह + ृ**

That little sign below ह represents the vowel **ṛ**.

It is not the same as *ri*.

In IAST:

- *hṛ* \= हृ
- *hri* \= ह्रि

The second requires the consonant र् and an *i* vowel, whereas the first uses the vocalic ṛ sign.

So *hṛdaya* is written:

**हृ + द + य**

Three syllabic units.

Now, a lovely temptation is to invent a mystical breakdown of *hṛdaya* from roots meaning "take," "give," and "go."

Traditional contemplative interpretations of the word do exist, but we must distinguish them from ordinary historical etymology. Abhinavagupta's point in the present passage doesn't depend on such an invented derivation.

Here the Heart refers to the supreme centre of conscious manifestation.

Not a physical location inside the rib cage.

Not merely a pleasant feeling of affection.

And not a second substance hidden behind your thoughts.

The Heart is the inexhaustible centre of awareness and its power to manifest.

### Granthi - ग्रन्थि

Now look at:

**ग्रन्थि - granthi**

A knot.

From the verbal root √*granth*, to bind, tie, or string together.

The word is associated with things joined or bound.

And its writing is a lovely little lesson.

# ग्रन्थि

gra-nthi

ग् + र = ग्र

न् + थ + ि = न्थि

Notice the cluster **न्थ**.

That is *ntha*, with the aspirated dental consonant **थ - tha**.

It is not **ठ - ṭha**, the retroflex aspirate.

Sanskrit cares about these differences.

And when you begin reading conjuncts rather than trying to decode every printed mark as an isolated letter, words like *granthi* become much easier to recognize.

### Jñātvā - ज्ञात्वा

Now this is a beautiful verb form.

**ज्ञात्वा - jñātvā**

Having known.

From √**jñā**, to know, with the absolutive suffix **-tvā**.

The idea is that one action is completed before the next:

Having understood the knot, one may act.

The cluster at the beginning is:

**ज् + ञ = ज्ञ**

The *jñ* consonantal combination.

And later we have:

**त् + व = त्व**

So the full form contains two conjunct clusters.

This is precisely why the virāma matters. You aren't reading a sequence of independent consonants each followed by an inherent *a*.

The letters belong to larger consonantal units.

### Kuryāt - कुर्यात्

One should do.

From √**kṛ**, to do or make.

This is a third-person singular optative form.

The optative can express what one should do, might do, or would do under certain conditions.

And here's the interesting bit.

The cited verse does not just say that knowing the sixteenfold knot gives you another conceptual explanation.

It connects understanding with action.

**कुर्यात्कर्म - kuryāt karma**

*Kur-yāt* ends in *t*.

*Karma* begins with *k*.

Thus the final t remains a consonant joined across the word boundary in the continuous writing.

### What is the actual knot?

Abhinavagupta quotes the verse immediately after finishing the sixteen interpretations.

He doesn't then give an additional list of sixteen different anatomical or psychological knots.

The connection is the sixteenfold understanding of Anuttara itself.

That distinction matters.

We should not invent a separate sixteen-stage subtle-body system here merely because the number invites one.

The philosophical reading is that the supreme Heart is the reality in which differentiation, limitation and their recognition become intelligible.

The knot is associated with how that reality is grasped and articulated.

And the freedom implied by understanding it isn't simply permission to do whatever the ego happens to fancy.

A compulsive desire and the freedom of universal consciousness are not automatically identical in their practical significance.

The verse points toward action whose relation to one's real nature has been transformed.

Imagine spending years desperately trying to become the person you already fundamentally are, but because you've mistaken your identity, every effort reinforces the very premise you're trying to escape.

You become the metaphysical equivalent of a man searching his entire house for the spectacles he's wearing.

And every new room becomes another spiritual initiation.

"Perhaps they're in the kitchen."

No.

"Perhaps I need to purify the bathroom."

No.

"Perhaps the spectacles are actually situated in the crown cakra."

MY BROTHER.

LOOK THROUGH THEM.

The analogy is imperfect because Trika recognition is not merely finding a misplaced physical object. But the essential problem is the same: the means of discovery depends upon that which has been misrecognized.

Abhinavagupta also begins citing another verse about a knot situated in the Heart, but supplies only its opening words in this passage. So we won't manufacture the missing continuation.

The sixteen explanations are complete.

And now we move to the next word of the Goddess's question.


## 3. कथम् - Katham: How the hell does this work?

The Goddess asks:

**कथम् - katham**

How?

And Abhinavagupta says that this one tiny word can frame the inquiry in three distinct ways.

This is one of the most important transitions in the commentary.

We've spent sixteen interpretations determining what Anuttara is not limited by.

Now the question concerns the **manner** in which it confers realization.

### The ordinary Sanskrit

**कथम् - katham**

An interrogative adverb.

It is formed from the interrogative base **ka-** with the adverbial suffix traditionally called **thamu**.

The visible ending is **-tham**.

This is a grammatical formation, not the phrase *ka + tha + ma* meaning three unrelated mystical entities.

Abhinavagupta specifically discusses the suffix and its role in indicating **prakāra**.

What's that?

**प्रकार - prakāra**

Manner, kind, mode, way.

In the word:

**प्र + कार**

The prefix *pra-* is joined to a formation related to doing, making or producing.

But the established noun *prakāra* means a manner, kind or mode.

It is not identical with *prakāśa*, manifestation or illumination, despite their similar opening sounds.

The difference between **र - ra** and **श - śa** is doing important work here.

Now the three readings.

### First question: Must the higher levels be abandoned?

The Goddess might be asking:

Does Anuttara confer perfection through relinquishing everything classified as *uttara*, everything apparently higher or successive?

In other words, must one abandon all spiritual gradations, all distinctions, all successive means?

Think about it.

Abhinavagupta has just said the Absolute is beyond hierarchy.

So does that mean the hierarchy is useless?

Should we burn the yoga manuals, throw away the meditation cushion and tell the entire tradition to pack up and go home?

Not necessarily.

The point is that a practice's relative usefulness does not make it an independent ultimate reality.

One can employ differentiated means while recognizing that the reality they disclose is not manufactured by those means.

This distinction will appear repeatedly throughout the commentary.

### Second question: How can the undivided appear divided?

Here the problem becomes genuinely philosophical.

The universe is filled with distinctions.

Knower and known.

Subject and object.

Pleasure and pain.

Self and other.

And these are not meaningless distinctions within ordinary experience.

But if all these differentiations arise within Anuttara, how can Anuttara appear as a world in which things oppose one another?

The commentary uses the terms:

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge or knowing.

**ज्ञेय - jñeya**

The knowable, the object to be known.

Now here's a lovely grammatical connection.

Both involve √*jñā*, to know.

But *jñeya* contains a gerundive formation expressing what is to be known.

So:

**jñāna** - knowing.

**jñeya** - what is to be known.

They are conceptually distinct.

But the tradition asks whether they can be separated from the conscious manifestation through which both are disclosed.

And the contrast between higher and lower appears through those differentiated structures.

**औत्तराधर्य - auttarādharya**

Relative higher-and-lower status.

This is not a word you're likely to use while buying vegetables.

Unless you're purchasing particularly metaphysical vegetables.

The word brings together the notions of superior and inferior ordering.

Abhinavagupta asks how the unsurpassable is related to a universe in which such orderings actually appear.

Not merely:

"Why is the universe an illusion?"

But:

How does differentiated experience arise from undivided consciousness without establishing a second ultimate reality?

That's a much stronger question.

### Third question: Is recognition possible only in liberation?

Now comes my favourite interpretation.

Does Anuttara grant perfection only in a state already identified as liberation?

Or can it do so **within what is ordinarily regarded as bondage**?

The Sanskrit contrast is:

**मोक्ष - mokṣa**

Liberation.

**बन्ध - bandha**

Bondage.

And Abhinavagupta speaks of **bandhābhimata**, that which is regarded or conceived as bondage.

Notice the participial element:

**अभिमत - abhimata**

Considered, regarded, approved or intended, depending on context.

The word does not necessarily establish that the condition so described is ultimately what it appears to be.

This is a devastatingly interesting question.

Because if the highest realization can only occur after a person has completely eliminated every ordinary worldly experience, what exactly makes it immediate?

Wouldn't the practitioner first need to manufacture some suitable state?

And if universal consciousness is present throughout differentiated experience, why should a supposedly impure moment be metaphysically disconnected from it?

Abhinavagupta is setting us up for his later discussion of the Goddess's powers, including anger, desire and the movements of the mind.

He's going to argue that the very forces appearing as bondage can, when known in their real nature, disclose liberation.

Not that ordinary anger automatically makes you enlightened.

Not that every impulsive act is sanctified.

But that the fundamental nature of those movements is not ultimately separate from consciousness.

That's the radical claim.

And Abhinavagupta allows the question *katham* to encompass all three possibilities because its grammatical function concerns the manner or mode, rather than being restricted to one particular case relation.

One word.

Three philosophical problems.

And we're still moving in the order of his commentary.


## 4. देव - Deva: Who is the Goddess actually addressing?

Next comes:

**देव - deva**

O Lord.

A masculine vocative singular.

The dictionary meaning is familiar: god, deity, divine being, lord.

But look at the grammar.

**देवः - devaḥ**

The god, as a nominative singular.

**देवम् - devam**

The god, as an accusative singular.

**देव - deva**

O god, directly addressed.

The last is the form in our verse.

The Goddess is speaking *to* Bhairava.

Not describing some third party.

This is why you cannot translate Sanskrit just by memorizing a vocabulary list. Endings express the grammatical relationship between words.

And there is one important textual point here.

**Abhinavagupta does not launch into a fresh elaborate explanation of *deva* at this precise location.**

He explicitly says that it has already been explained earlier.

So we should not pretend an invented interpretation belongs here.

The earlier theological setting is that Parameśvara operates through his inseparable power of grace, *anugraha*, and the apparent dialogue between the Goddess and Bhairava occurs within the single reality of divine consciousness.

The grammatical form is beautifully appropriate.

The Goddess addresses Bhairava as the Lord, yet the commentary has already denied that Śakti is ultimately separate from Śiva.

Consciousness is addressing consciousness.

And the speech expressing the address is itself a movement of the same power.

Now we reach the enormous compound that carries the real burden of her question.

**कौलिकसिद्धिदम् - kaulikasiddhidam.**

And this is where things become properly outrageous.


## 5. कुल - Kula: The universe is a family

This is where the central Kaula teaching begins to unfold.

**कुल - kula**

Ordinarily, a family, clan, household, collection, or group.

But Abhinavagupta isn't content with the ordinary sociological meaning.

He begins with the world of manifestation.

The body.

The senses.

The breath.

The elements.

The subtle structures of experience.

Even the relationship between causes and effects.

He identifies *kula* with this interrelated totality.

And then makes the enormous philosophical move:

**That totality is consciousness itself appearing in condensed and differentiated form.**

### The Sanskrit of manifestation

He introduces three levels:

**स्थूल**

sthūla

Gross, material or fully differentiated.

**सूक्ष्म**

sūkṣma

Subtle, not ordinarily available as a gross object.

**पर**

para

Higher, supreme or transcendent.

And several categories within this collective manifestation:

**प्राण - prāṇa:** vital life.

**इन्द्रिय - indriya:** sense faculty or instrument.

**भूत - bhūta:** an existent, particularly an element.

**कार्य - kārya:** an effect, something produced.

**कारण - kāraṇa:** a cause or instrument.

Now here's a useful grammatical relationship.

### Kārya versus Kāraṇa

Both are related to √**kṛ**, to do or make.

But the formations differ.

**कार्य - kārya**

That which is to be done, or something accomplished or produced.

In philosophical contexts, often an effect.

**कारण - kāraṇa**

A cause, instrument, or that by which something is done.

So one concerns what is brought about, while the other concerns what brings it about.

Now put them together:

**कार्यकारणभाव - kāryakāraṇabhāva**

The relationship of effect and cause.

Break it into:

*kārya + kāraṇa + bhāva*

That last word, *bhāva*, refers to being, condition or state.

Abhinavagupta includes the causal relationships of manifested things within *kula*.

This is crucial.

He isn't merely saying that the universe contains many objects.

He is saying that the objects exist within relations of mutual dependence and manifestation.

The whole interconnected collection is what the Kaula perspective begins to consider.

### The universe is not a pile of disconnected furniture

Imagine a house.

A table.

A chair.

A kettle.

A lamp.

Now imagine somebody declaring that these objects have absolutely no relation to one another.

They are merely independent substances occupying a common container.

But look closer.

The table was produced from wood.

The wood came from a tree.

The tree grew through conditions involving water, light, minerals and biological development.

And its entire significance as a table arises through conscious interaction and interpretation.

Everything participates in relations.

Abhinavagupta is developing a much more radical claim than ecological interdependence, however.

The very appearance and intelligibility of these relationships depends upon consciousness.

Thus *kula* names the differentiated totality that is nevertheless inseparable from its ultimate ground.

### The astonishing word āśyāna

Abhinavagupta next describes manifested reality through:

**आश्यान - āśyāna**

Coagulated, congealed, thickened or condensed.

This is one of the most evocative expressions in the passage.

Consciousness manifests as though it has condensed into the differentiated forms of the world.

Now be careful.

He is not claiming that consciousness is literally a liquid that cools into physical matter.

Nor is he proposing an experimentally established theory of matter.

This is a metaphysical explanation of appearance.

The universal consciousness freely manifests what appears to be a world of definite, solid, relatively independent things.

The objects are differentiated.

But their manifest nature does not become independent of the light of consciousness.

Imagine liquid honey cooling into a thicker substance.

The forms and textures change.

Yet the underlying material hasn't magically become something unrelated to itself.

That analogy gets at the notion of apparent condensation.

But Trika's metaphysical claim is even stronger: manifestation occurs through the freedom of conscious reality itself.

### The Sanskrit: बोधस्वातन्त्र्य

**बोधस्वातन्त्र्य - bodhasvātantrya**

The freedom of consciousness.

Two elements:

- **bodha** - awareness, knowing, consciousness, from √*budh*, to awaken or understand.
- **svātantrya** - autonomy, freedom, independence.

You already know the second word.

And now it's doing actual philosophical work.

It is by the freedom of consciousness, says Abhinavagupta, that consciousness can manifest itself under the appearance of bondage.

Which is an extraordinary statement.

The individual doesn't become limited because some independent enemy outside Śiva successfully attacks the Absolute.

The limited form of experience arises within the freedom of Śiva himself.

That's the central Trika paradox.

**Freedom includes the power to manifest limitation.**

But limitation doesn't become an ultimate second reality.

Abhinavagupta also draws on the dictionary association of *kula* with gathering, condensation, and relatives.

So the ordinary word *family* acquires an astonishing metaphysical scope.

The universe is the family of Śiva.

Not because all the rocks and trees have been invited to a cosmic wedding.

But because they belong to the one differentiated manifestation of consciousness.

And that prepares us for *kaulikī siddhi*.


## 6. कौलिकी सिद्धिः - Kaulikī Siddhi: Perfection of the whole family

Now we can finally dismantle the phrase properly.

**कौलिकसिद्धिदम् - kaulikasiddhidam**

In the verse it is a single compound.

But Abhinavagupta's prose explains the expression using:

**कौलिकी सिद्धिः - kaulikī siddhiḥ**

This distinction matters.

The verse has a neuter compound describing Anuttara.

The commentary uses a feminine noun phrase referring to the perfection itself.

### Build it from the roots

Start with:

**कुल - kula**

Family, group, collective manifestation.

Then:

**कौल - kaula**

A derivative associated with *kula*.

Notice the vowel change.

# कुल → कौल

kula → kaula

The short vowel u is strengthened to au in this derivative formation.

This is an example of **vṛddhi**, vowel strengthening.

You should learn these correspondences because they appear constantly in Sanskrit derivation:

| Basic vowel | Vṛddhi grade |
|--------|--------|
| अ - a | आ - ā |
| इ - i | ऐ - ai |
| उ - u | औ - au |
| ऋ - ṛ | आर् - ār |

The precise application depends on the derivational rule, but the pattern is extremely useful.

So *kaula* does not mean something unrelated to *kula*.

It is a derivative belonging to the same word family.

Next:

**कौलिक - kaulika**

Pertaining to the Kaula tradition, or belonging to that complex of meanings.

And the corresponding feminine form:

**कौलिकी - kaulikī**

Why the long ī?

Because the adjective is agreeing with the feminine noun *siddhiḥ*.

Then:

**सिद्धि - siddhi**

Accomplishment, perfection, attainment.

From √*sidh*, to succeed or accomplish.

And finally:

**द - da**

Giver.

From √*dā*, to give.

So:

**kaulika + siddhi + da**

That which grants Kaulika perfection.

With the neuter accusative ending:

**कौलिकसिद्धिदम् - kaulikasiddhidam**

And remember that this compound describes *anuttaram*, also neuter accusative singular.

The grammar agrees.

Excellent.

Now we're ready for the philosophy.

### What is the first meaning of Kaulikī Siddhi?

Abhinavagupta describes it as a firmness of recognition involving the transformed understanding of the totality.

The differentiated world, previously taken as something fundamentally separate, is recognized in its identity with the supreme consciousness constituting the Heart.

He associates the resulting state with bliss and the reflexive awareness of Śiva.

The crucial term is:

**तादात्म्य - tādātmya**

Identity or sameness of essence.

Let's learn it.

*Tat* means that.

*Ātman* means self.

The abstract form *tādātmya* expresses identity with that reality.

The morphological history is more involved than simply placing two independently declined words beside one another, but the semantic components help you understand the result.

And this is what he's arguing.

The world isn't destroyed.

The senses don't disappear.

The body doesn't need to evaporate.

Rather, the apparent separateness of the totality is understood through its real identity with consciousness.

### But why call that Siddhi?

Abhinavagupta cites a definition linking *siddhi* with *niścaya*, *vyavasthiti*, *pratiṣṭhā*, and *nirvṛtti*.

These are important.

**निश्चय - niścaya**

Certainty, definite ascertainment.

**व्यवस्थिति - vyavasthiti**

Established condition, orderly stability.

**प्रतिष्ठा - pratiṣṭhā**

Firm establishment, foundation.

**निर्वृत्ति - nirvṛtti**

Fulfilment, completion, satisfaction.

These terms suggest that attainment is not merely the production of an unusual experience.

It involves stable ascertainment of reality.

That is why Abhinavagupta places so much weight on the firmness of recognition.

Imagine someone who has a beautiful meditation experience.

For thirty minutes, everything feels open, luminous and peaceful.

They emerge from meditation.

Somebody criticizes their pronunciation of *Bhairava*.

And immediately the entire universe has become an intolerable personal insult.

Now, did they have an experience?

Yes.

Did they necessarily establish stable recognition of their true nature?

That's another question.

**Siddhi is not reducible to an impressive passing state.**

The first interpretation therefore concerns the perfection of *kula* through its recognition as the expression of universal consciousness.


## 7. सद्यः - Sadyaḥ: Why liberation is not scheduled for Thursday

Now we reach the word that makes this whole thing philosophically explosive.

**सद्यः - sadyaḥ**

Immediately.

Straightaway.

At once.

And Abhinavagupta takes it apart with extraordinary precision.

### From the same day to the same instant

He explains *sadyaḥ* through the sense:

**समानेऽहनि - samāne 'hani**

On the same day.

Here:

*samāne* \= in the same.

*ahani* \= on the day, locative singular of *ahan*, day.

And the written combination illustrates sandhi:

**samāne + ahani → samāne 'hani**

The avagraha **ऽ** marks the elision of the initial *a*.

Now he tightens the meaning.

Not merely the same day.

**समाने क्षणे - samāne kṣaṇe**

In the same instant.

The word *kṣaṇa* is worth learning.

**क्षण - kṣaṇa**

Moment, instant.

Look at the first conjunct:

**क् + ष = क्ष**

*k + ṣ = kṣ*

Not *ch*.

Not *kh*.

And the vowel *a* belongs after the consonant cluster.

So:

*kṣaṇa*.

Now we're about to see why this tiny word becomes such a problem.

### What makes the present moment present?

Think of ordinary time.

Past.

Present.

Future.

Simple.

But now Abhinavagupta starts interrogating the present.

What makes the present different from the past?

What makes it different from the future?

Its definition seems to depend on those other temporal categories.

The present is distinguished from what is no longer present and what is not yet present.

So if you remove that relational structure, what remains of ordinary temporal presentness?

The Sanskrit expression:

**भूतभविष्यत् - bhūtabhaviṣyat**

Past and future.

*Bhūta* from √*bhū*, to be or become.

*Bhaviṣyat* is a future participial form, what will be.

And then:

**वर्तमान - vartamāna**

Present, occurring, being in progress.

Connected with √*vṛt*, to occur, turn or exist in a state.

This is a lovely trio to learn because these are not arbitrary labels.

They're derived from verbal conceptions of being, becoming and occurring.

Now Abhinavagupta's argument.

If the realization of Anuttara belongs to the present, but that present is conceived only in opposition to past and future, then the highest realization still appears confined within ordinary time.

However, he is not merely promising a very rapid future experience.

He pushes the meaning of immediacy toward freedom from temporal delimitation.

The central realization is not located at a specially privileged instant that replaces every other instant.

**It concerns the consciousness in which the distinctions between moments become manifest.**

Now this is subtle.

The clock may continue ticking.

The body continues acting.

Thoughts continue succeeding one another.

But the supreme reality is not established through the clock's succession.

### The sun of Bhairava

Abhinavagupta then invokes a magnificent image.

**भैरवभानु - bhairavabhānu**

The sun of Bhairava.

*Bhānu* means sun, radiance, or luminous one.

And the world is described through:

**रश्मिचक्र - raśmicakra**

A circle or wheel of rays.

- **raśmi** - ray, beam, luminous strand.
- **cakra** - wheel, circle, group or organized power.

Remember that *cakra* doesn't always mean an anatomical energy centre.

Its meaning depends on context.

Here the rays symbolize the manifestation of the divine power.

Now picture the sun.

From it streams an immense field of light.

The light illuminates a world full of differentiated things.

But the rays aren't completely unrelated to the luminous source from which they emerge.

Similarly, Abhinavagupta presents *kula* as the expanded manifestation of Bhairava's own radiance.

And then he describes an inward reabsorption or restraint, *nirodha*, characterized by identification with the supreme consciousness of Bhairava.

That doesn't simply mean forcibly suppressing thoughts.

It concerns the return of manifest differentiation to recognition of its own source.

### The nectar beyond past and future

He cites the Vādyatantra, which speaks of restraining the circle of rays, tasting supreme nectar, and being happy in a present not delimited by past and future.

That nectar is:

**अमृत - amṛta**

Literally deathless, immortal.

Break it down:

**a- + mṛta**

*Mṛta* means dead, from √*mṛ*, to die.

The negative prefix reverses the condition.

Thus *amṛta* means deathless, and by extension the nectar of immortality.

In the present context, the nectar signifies the bliss associated with supreme recognition.

It's not a literal beverage dispensed by a celestial barman.

Although after sixteen interpretations of Anuttara, the man deserves one.

### The actual philosophical conclusion

When the expanded energies of consciousness are recognized in their own undivided ground, the realization is not produced as an object arriving from the future.

It is the disclosure of an already-present reality.

This is why Abhinavagupta can speak of immediacy.

But notice the important qualification:

It doesn't follow that everybody is automatically liberated in the ordinary experiential sense.

**The reality to be recognized is always present; the recognition of it may nevertheless be obscured.**

And that distinction preserves the significance of teaching, initiation, practice, grace and understanding.

The Absolute does not wait.

The limited experiencer may still fail to recognize it.




## 8. The second Kaulika Siddhi - Your senses run on divine power

Now the commentary turns back to *kaulikasiddhidam* and gives another interpretation.

And this is one of my favourite developments, because it's so different from the first.

The first concerned the perfection of recognizing the totality as consciousness.

Now he examines the actual operations of embodied experience.

The body.

The breath.

Perception.

Even the recognition of colours and pleasures.

The Sanskrit mentions:

**नील - nīla**

Blue.

And:

**सुख - sukha**

Pleasure, ease, happiness.

The question is how the body and the faculties of cognition produce the ascertainment that something is blue, pleasurable or otherwise differentiated.

You look at a blue object.

You recognize it.

"This is blue."

Ordinarily, this seems like the most mundane thing imaginable.

But Abhinavagupta asks what fundamentally enables such determination.

Because the senses don't possess independent, self-revealing consciousness.

Their functioning depends upon the powers of awareness and manifestation.

### The Sanskrit: niścaya

**निश्चय - niścaya**

Definite ascertainment.

This is a word worth remembering for epistemology.

When cognition determines something as being a particular way, that is a form of *niścaya*.

A blue patch is apprehended as blue.

A pleasant feeling is apprehended as pleasant.

The differentiation is meaningful.

But its manifestation is grounded in consciousness.

Abhinavagupta relates this to the extraordinary powers symbolized by the phonemes of the universal *aham*.

Remember:

**अहम् - aham**

I.

The phonemic symbolism associated with *a* and *ha* expresses the relationship between the primordial consciousness and the divine power of emission.

The world of determinate perception is not outside that expression.

Now imagine somebody proudly announcing:

"I have transcended all ordinary perception. Blue is illusion!"

And then walking straight into a blue door.

The door has made its philosophical objection.

Trika doesn't require you to deny differentiated appearances.

The point is to recognize their underlying reality.

A blue object may remain blue.

Pleasure may remain pleasurable.

Differentiated cognition can continue without being mistaken for something metaphysically separate from consciousness.

The second interpretation therefore understands Kaulikī Siddhi through the accomplished manifestation and ascertainment of differentiated experience itself.

**The functioning world is already an expression of the power whose perfection the Goddess asks about.**

This is a radically affirmative ontology.


## 9. The third Kaulika Siddhi - Jīvanmukti, extraordinary powers, and the limits of practice

Now Abhinavagupta gives another interpretation.

This time explicitly involving:

**जीवन्मुक्तता - jīvanmuktatā**

The condition of being liberated while living.

And he also mentions extraordinary accomplishments, including powers such as:

**अणिमा - aṇimā**

The power traditionally described as becoming minute.

Now, be precise.

These are traditional yogic and Tantric claims. Their inclusion in scripture does not establish that supernatural feats have been scientifically demonstrated.

And the central philosophical point of the present passage concerns the relationship between such accomplishments, liberation, and supreme consciousness.

### The morphology of jīvanmukti

**जीवन् - jīvan**

Living, alive.

From √*jīv*, to live.

**मुक्ति - mukti**

Release, liberation.

From √*muc*, to release.

**जीवन्मुक्ति - jīvanmukti**

Liberation while living.

Now add the abstract suffix:

**-ता - -tā**

And we get:

**जीवन्मुक्तता - jīvanmuktatā**

The condition or state of being liberated while living.

The idea is not that you must wait until the physical body dies before liberation becomes possible.

Recognition may occur within embodied existence.

And now Abhinavagupta invokes Somānanda.

He asks what use elaborate cultivation and ritual instruments have when Śiva is perpetually present.

This is a serious philosophical challenge.

If realization is established, what further instrument could make the supreme consciousness more fully itself?

### The word bhāvanā

**भावना - bhāvanā**

Cultivation, imaginative realization, contemplation, bringing into being.

Related to √*bhū*, to be or become, through its causative formation.

And:

**करण - karaṇa**

Instrument, means, or action of doing.

From √*kṛ*.

Now compare:

**भावना - bhāvanā**

Cultivation or meditative construction.

**करण - karaṇa**

Instrument or means.

Abhinavagupta cites teachings according to which, once Śiva's omnipresent nature has been firmly recognized, further cultivation and instrumental action have no outstanding task in producing that recognition.

But read that carefully.

**Once firmly recognized.**

Not:

"I have read half a chapter of the Śiva Sūtras, therefore I have transcended the need for practice."

My brother, you've transcended the need to finish the bloody chapter.

The argument is about the completion of realization.

It is not permission to confuse intellectual familiarity with stable recognition.

Abhinavagupta's own writings contain extensive systems of practice, ritual, initiation and philosophical instruction.

He isn't contradicting them merely by saying that the ultimate realization is not mechanically manufactured by practice.

The distinction is between the conditions enabling recognition and the intrinsic nature of what is recognized.

Third interpretation: Kaulikī Siddhi can signify the attainment of liberation while living, with the tradition's broader range of extraordinary powers, grounded in the recognition of the ever-present nature of Śiva.


## 10. The fourth Kaulika Siddhi - Manifestation itself is an accomplishment

And now comes the interpretation that prevents us from turning all this into a purely inward doctrine.

Abhinavagupta says that *siddhi* can also concern the manifestation of differentiation itself.

The unfolding of Śakti.

The expansion of phonemic powers.

The appearance of the external world.

Yes.

The universe itself.

A manifestation of divine accomplishment.

### The Sanskrit: prasara

**प्रसर - prasara**

Expansion, spreading, extension.

Related to *pra-* and √*sṛ*, to move or flow.

And:

**विकास - vikāsa**

Expansion, opening, unfolding, development.

Imagine a flower opening.

Its petals become distinguishable.

Its form unfolds.

But the differentiation of the petals does not mean the flower has become a collection of unrelated objects.

Similarly, Abhinavagupta presents differentiated manifestation as the expansion of Śakti.

The distinction between knower and known, the expansion of sounds, and the appearance of external forms are all expressions of divine freedom.

### Two directions, one reality

This is an especially important point in Trika.

There's an inward movement toward recognition of the undivided Heart.

And there's the outward movement of manifestation.

We mustn't imagine that one is fundamentally divine and the other is an unfortunate cosmic accident.

The very power that allows consciousness to recognize itself also allows it to manifest multiplicity.

The movement of differentiation is not a defect in Śiva.

It's an expression of **svātantrya**, sovereign freedom.

So if someone declares:

"The ultimate truth is pure consciousness. Everything else is a regrettable mistake."

Abhinavagupta asks you to explain how the supposedly regrettable world could manifest independently of consciousness.

If it depends on consciousness for its appearance, then the apparent otherness of the world is itself an expression of conscious freedom.

The divine didn't accidentally create an unauthorized second universe and then have to call technical support.

**The power of manifestation belongs to the nature of the Absolute.**

And after developing this interpretation, Abhinavagupta effectively says: Why get tangled up in any more words?

Which is extremely funny coming from a man currently buried neck-deep in several thousand words about the first line of a short Tantra.

But the point is substantial.

The differentiated universe does not require an ultimate explanation outside consciousness.

We've reached the end of his four principal interpretations of Kaulika Siddhi in this passage.

; \]


## 11. येन विज्ञातमात्रेण - By the mere knowing of which

And now we arrive at perhaps the most philosophically delicate expression in the entire verse.

**येन विज्ञातमात्रेण - yena vijñātamātreṇa**

By the mere knowing or recognition of which.

At first glance, it seems fairly straightforward.

But Abhinavagupta does something extraordinary.

He starts giving it multiple readings, including one based on the word **mātrā**, which isn't the same grammatical analysis as the ordinary *mātra* meaning "mere."

And if we don't distinguish them, we miss the whole Sanskrit game.

### First, ordinary grammar

**येन - yena**

By which.

This is the instrumental singular of the relative pronoun *yad*.

Compare:

| Form | Meaning |
|--------|--------|
| **यत् - yat** | Which, that which |
| **येन - yena** | By which |
| **यस्मात् - yasmāt** | From which |
| **यस्मिन् - yasmin** | In which |

Then:

**वि - vi**

A prefix that can indicate differentiation, distinction, or a particularized manner of action.

**ज्ञात - jñāta**

Known, understood.

From √*jñā*.

And:

**मात्र - mātra**

Mere, only, nothing but.

Finally:

**-ेण - -eṇa**

The instrumental singular ending of this compound.

Thus:

**विज्ञातमात्रेण - vijñātamātreṇa**

By the mere knowing, or through its being known.

In the context of the verse, this refers to recognition of Anuttara.

And notice something important.

It does not say:

"By the mere existence of a person who has heard the word Anuttara."

Nor:

"By the mere verbal repetition of a proposition."

It refers to knowing of the reality itself.

And the commentary will now push the meaning of knowing much further.

### The second reading: Mātrā as a divine power

Here's where Abhinavagupta deliberately resegments the expression.

Instead of reading *mātra* only as "mere," he explores:

**मात्रा - mātrā**

A measure, but here interpreted through knowledge and protective or governing power.

And he associates it with elements carrying the senses of measuring or knowing, and protecting.

This is sacred explanatory wordplay.

Not a claim that every ordinary occurrence of *mātra* means a metaphysical goddess.

He identifies a fourfold structure:

**प्रमातृ**

pramātṛ

The knower, cognizing subject.

**प्रमाण**

pramāṇa

Means or instrument of valid knowing.

**प्रमेय**

prameya

The object known or knowable.

**प्रमिति**

pramiti

Knowledge or the accomplished act of knowing.

These are absolutely foundational Sanskrit philosophical terms.

And look how beautifully they relate.

All belong to the technical vocabulary of cognition.

The knower.

The instrument.

The object.

The knowing.

The whole epistemological event.

A person sees a mango.

There's the knower.

There's the functioning of vision.

There's the mango as object.

And there's the cognition of the mango.

Four distinguishable aspects.

Now ask:

What is the fundamental reality of the whole event?

Abhinavagupta's answer is not that the four aspects are practically indistinguishable.

It is that their possibility and manifestation depend upon consciousness.

And the special reading of *vijñātamātra* connects the knowledge of these dimensions with the supreme awareness that sustains them.

The word becomes an analysis of cognition itself.

### Third reading: Once genuinely recognized, it does not need to be manufactured again

Now Abhinavagupta considers the firmness of apprehension.

**प्रतिपत्ति - pratipatti**

Understanding, apprehension, realization.

And:

**दार्ढ्य - dārḍhya**

Firmness, stability.

When the reality has been definitively recognized, it is not something that needs to be brought into existence through repeated imaginative production.

The important difference is between:

"I keep constructing an idea of what Śiva might be."

And:

"The nature of reality has been decisively recognized."

This is not a dismissal of repetition in practice.

It's a distinction between provisional conceptual construction and realization.

Suppose you're learning Devanāgarī.

At first, you look at **ग्रन्थि** and see a pile of incomprehensible marks.

You laboriously identify the conjuncts.

You consult the vowel signs.

Eventually you know how the word is structured.

Once that understanding is established, you don't need to manufacture the same knowledge from absolute ignorance every time you encounter the word.

The analogy is limited, but it illustrates the distinction.

Recognition changes how something is understood.

It doesn't create the reality of the letters.

And in the Trika case, what is being recognized is not simply a linguistic pattern, but the nature of awareness itself.

### Fourth reading: Beyond the opposition of knower and known

Then he goes further still.

The object, such as a pot, ordinarily appears only as something known.

Not as the knower.

And Māyā sustains the appearance of the separation between knowing subject and known object.

But Abhinavagupta describes the transcendence of this division through the recognition of their single luminous ground.

This is not saying that the physical pot becomes a person with a private mind.

It's saying that the pot's manifested reality is inseparable from the conscious ground of manifestation.

At the level of universal consciousness, the distinction does not establish two independent ultimate realities.

And this is what makes the phrase *vijñātamātreṇa* so powerful.

The knowledge is not an ordinary subject acquiring one more external object.

It is recognition of the reality that makes the entire knower-knowing-known structure possible.

**The very instruments of knowing are themselves disclosures of the reality being known.**

And now the Goddess asks what happens through this recognition.

What does one attain?

**खेचरीसमताम् - khecarīsamatām.**

And, my friend, we're about to get into the actual workings of your mind.




## 12. खेचरी - Khecarī: The Goddess who moves through consciousness

At last.

One of the most beautiful terms in the entire tradition.

**खेचरी - khecarī**

And here it's worth slowing right down.

Because this isn't merely the name of a supernatural female being flying around the sky.

Abhinavagupta gives the word a profoundly philosophical interpretation.

### The literal morphology

**ख - kha**

Space, sky, expanse.

**खे - khe**

In space.

The locative singular form of *kha*.

Then:

**चर् - √car**

To move, wander, proceed, act.

And:

**चरी - carī**

A feminine moving or wandering one.

Thus:

**खेचरी - khecarī**

She who moves in space.

But what is *space* here?

Abhinavagupta interprets it as the nondual expanse of Brahman or supreme consciousness.

So Khecarī is the power moving within that reality.

Now here's the brilliant reversal.

Ordinarily, we imagine consciousness is some little private chamber inside our heads, and things move around outside it.

But Abhinavagupta is developing the opposite relationship.

The movements of the mind and senses occur within the universal expanse of consciousness.

Khecarī is the divine power through which they are manifest.

And she does not merely float around doing nothing.

She knows objects.

She enables activity.

She manifests the differentiated functions of mind, senses and experience.

### Why Khecarī is feminine

The ending **-ī** marks the feminine form.

And Abhinavagupta expressly identifies her as **Śakti**.

Not merely a grammatical accident.

The feminine theological symbolism represents the active and expressive power of the supreme consciousness.

Śiva and Śakti are inseparable.

And Khecarī is one way that the divine power is understood within the functioning of experience.

But now things become seriously interesting.

Because she's not alone.

Or rather, she only *appears* to be divided into several powers.

Abhinavagupta introduces four modes.

## 13. The four Carī powers - Your entire experience is the Goddess at work

**व्योमचरी - Vyomacarī** 1

*Vyoma* \= space, expanse.

Associated here with the void-like state in which the distinction between knower and known is not manifest.

**गोचरी - Gocarī** 2

Associated with the manifestation of awareness and the inner cognitive sphere.

**दिक्चरी - Dikcarī** 3

*Diś/dik* \= direction.

Associated with the functioning of the senses and the developing distinction of experiencer and experienced.

**भूचरी - Bhūcarī** 4

*Bhū* \= earth, being.

Associated with the differentiated field of external objects and clearly manifested multiplicity.

These names have different associations across Tantric literature. Here we're following how Abhinavagupta orders them in this particular commentary.

And the claim is extraordinary.

**All four are fundamentally inseparable from Khecarī.**

### Vyomacarī - Before subject and object emerge

**व्योम - vyoma**

Space, sky, expanse.

**व्योमचरी - vyomacarī**

She who moves in the expanse.

In this passage the power is associated with a state in which the ordinary contrast of knowing subject and known object is not manifest.

Think about the possibility of experience before it takes the form of a distinct subject confronting a distinct object.

Now, that is only an analogy.

Abhinavagupta is describing a particular structure of manifestation, not simply a developmental stage in infant psychology.

But it helps us understand the philosophical role.

The ordinary knower-known relation has not yet become articulated in the familiar manner.

### Gocarī - The inner cognitive field

**गोचर - gocara**

Range, sphere, domain of perception, something accessible to a faculty.

Interestingly, *go* has several Sanskrit meanings, including cow, earth, speech and sense-related usages.

In compounds such as *gocara*, the established meaning involves a range or field.

Here the power is associated with cognitive manifestation and the inner faculties.

And the term connects with:

**अन्तःकरण - antaḥkaraṇa**

The inner instrument.

Break it down:

- **antar / antaḥ** - within, inner.
- **karaṇa** - instrument or means.

In Indian philosophical systems, *antaḥkaraṇa* can include intellect, ego-function, and mind, although classifications vary by tradition.

Now imagine the inner scene.

A thought arises.

An image appears in imagination.

A memory is recalled.

A desire begins to form.

The ordinary person says:

"These are my private mental contents."

Abhinavagupta asks what their ultimate nature is.

The inner cognitive field is itself an expression of consciousness.

Gocarī is not an alien agent attacking your mind.

She's a differentiated power of the same universal awareness.

### Dikcarī - The senses reaching outward

**दिक् - dik**

Direction.

Related to *diś*, direction.

**दिक्चरी - dikcarī**

She who moves through directions.

Now the senses become active.

You hear something.

You reach for an object.

You withdraw from something unpleasant.

The commentary explicitly associates this level with activities such as taking and abandoning.

**आदान - ādāna**

Taking, receiving.

**हान - hāna**

Abandoning, leaving, relinquishing.

And these operations occur through the sensory and active instruments.

The world appears directionally organized.

Here.

There.

Near.

Far.

Approaching.

Leaving.

But the awareness in which these distinctions appear is not itself a physical object traveling through space.

That's the distinction.

### Bhūcarī - The world of apparently external objects

Finally:

**भू - bhū**

Earth, existence, being.

**भूचरी - bhūcarī**

She who moves in the terrestrial or manifested field.

Now we're dealing with the sharply differentiated world of objects.

Tables.

Trees.

Sounds.

Colours.

Physical bodies.

And the commentary emphasizes the progressively pronounced manifestation of difference.

The object appears to be outside the knower.

The distinction becomes fully evident.

But the point of the sequence is not that Bhūcarī has somehow escaped the divine reality.

All these powers are expressions of the same supreme Śakti.

### The big revelation

Imagine an orchestra.

Different instruments perform different functions.

The violin doesn't sound like the drum.

The flute doesn't sound like the cello.

And the music depends upon their differentiation.

But the performance can be understood as one integrated event.

Likewise, the four powers are functionally differentiated.

Yet they don't establish four independent ultimate consciousnesses.

Abhinavagupta calls attention to the single divine power underlying them.

And he cites the teaching that the entire world consists of Śiva's powers, while Mahādeva is the possessor of those powers.

Now think about that.

Your senses.

Your mind.

Your capacity to recognize colours.

Your capacity to remember.

Your capacity to act.

All of these are being interpreted as expressions of divine Śakti.

Not merely as obstacles that must be eliminated.

**The whole apparatus of experience is a manifestation of consciousness.**

And this is where the discussion of Khecarī becomes radically practical.




## 14. समता - Samatā: What does equality with Khecarī actually mean?

The original verse does not merely say that one recognizes Khecarī.

It says one attains:

**खेचरीसमताम् - khecarīsamatām**

Equality or sameness with Khecarī.

Let's learn the grammar.

**सम - sama**

Equal, even, alike.

**समता - samatā**

Equality, sameness, evenness.

The abstract suffix **-tā** forms the noun.

Then:

**समताम् - samatām**

Accusative singular.

The state of equality, functioning as the object of attaining.

Now join:

**khecarī + samatām**

The result:

**खेचरीसमताम् - khecarīsamatām**

The equality of or with Khecarī.

The compound does not mean you're literally becoming a second goddess with identical clothing and a matching celestial address.

It concerns the recognition of the undivided divine nature of the powers operating through experience.

And Abhinavagupta immediately makes the discussion much more interesting.

Because the same Khecarī appears through:

**काम - kāma**

Desire.

And:

**क्रोध - krodha**

Anger.

Right.

Here we go.

The Goddess is not confined to the serene states of a person who has enjoyed seven hours of sleep, a perfectly balanced breakfast, and absolutely no annoying messages.

She is also present in the movement of desire.

In anger.

In the turbulent structures of ordinary consciousness.

And now the question is:

**What makes those movements binding?**

Abhinavagupta's answer concerns *vaiṣamya*.

### Vaiṣamya - विषम्य / वैषम्य

**विषम - viṣama**

Uneven, unequal, irregular, discordant.

**वैषम्य - vaiṣamya**

Unevenness, inequality, disequilibrium.

Notice the derivative vowel strengthening.

The initial *vi* becomes *vai* in this formation.

And *vaiṣamya* names the condition in which the powers are not recognized in their common divine nature.

Now imagine anger arising.

There's an initial disturbance.

A sudden heat.

An impulse.

Perhaps an unpleasant memory.

Then the mind begins constructing a world around it.

"That person insulted me."

"They always do this."

"I need to respond."

"This proves everything I've always thought about them."

Congratulations.

The Goddess has manifested an entire administrative department of resentment.

But Abhinavagupta is making a more subtle point than simply saying:

"Observe anger mindfully."

He is asking about the very nature of the power manifesting as anger.

What is the awareness in which it appears?

What is its relation to universal Śakti?

And how does misrecognition convert a movement of consciousness into the experience of a bound individual?

### The problem is not that a mental event occurs

This is vital.

Abhinavagupta does not argue that liberation requires all mental events to stop.

He is examining how their apparent differentiation is related to the underlying unity of consciousness.

The anger is not a second independent substance outside Śiva.

But the limited subject can mistake the anger and its associated conceptual constructions for the complete definition of personal reality.

"I am angry."

Then:

"I am this particular injured person."

Then:

"The entire world is organized around the fact that I have been injured."

The universal experiencer has been conceptually restricted to a narrow personal identity.

And that restriction is closely connected with the doctrine of the three *malas*.


## 15. The three Malas - How Śiva becomes the miserable little individual

This is one of the most important passages in this entire installment.

Abhinavagupta connects the unevenness of mental experience with the three impurities or limitations of the bound subject.

Remember:

**मल - mala**

Impurity, stain, obscuration.

But in Trika, these are not merely moral sins.

They describe fundamental limitations of consciousness as manifested in individual experience.

### First: Āṇava Mala

**आणवमल - āṇavamala**

The limitation associated with the finite individual.

From *aṇu*, the minute or limited subject.

Now the grammar.

**अणु - aṇu**

Minute, atomic, small.

**आणव - āṇava**

Pertaining to the limited individual.

Notice the long initial ā and the derivative form.

Then *mala*.

Āṇava mala.

The fundamental condition of regarding oneself as incomplete.

The commentary associates this with the attribution of limitedness to one's own self.

The universal "I" is experienced as a contracted "I".

Instead of recognizing itself as universal consciousness, it takes itself to be a particular finite experiencer.

And the crucial feature is the sense:

**अपूर्ण - apūrṇa**

Incomplete.

Break it down:

*a- + pūrṇa*

*Pūrṇa* means full, complete.

*A-pūrṇa* means incomplete.

Now what happens when you regard yourself as incomplete?

You naturally try to remedy the deficiency.

Which leads directly into the second mala.

### Second: Māyīya Mala

**मायीयमल - māyīyamala**

The limitation of differentiated perception.

Consciousness begins to regard objects and experiences as fundamentally separate.

The sense of incompleteness generates the desire to acquire something that will repair the deficiency.

"I lack something."

"That object will complete me."

"That experience will make me whole."

And now the universe is divided into things you possess, things you lack, and things you desperately want.

The world has become an enormous shopping centre for a self that believes it is incomplete.

But remember: Abhinavagupta is not saying every ordinary distinction is false or useless.

The problem is taking differentiation as ultimate separation.

### Third: Kārma Mala

**कार्ममल - kārmamala**

The limitation associated with karmic action and its residual tendencies.

The commentary describes the grasping of dispositions connected with good and bad actions.

The Sanskrit:

**शुभ - śubha**

Auspicious, good.

**अशुभ - aśubha**

Inauspicious, bad.

And:

**वासना - vāsanā**

A latent tendency, disposition, impression.

Now the pattern becomes clear.

**आणवमल - Āṇava mala**

The fundamental sense of incompleteness: "I am merely this limited individual."

**मायीयमल - Māyīya mala**

Differentiated experience is mistaken for ultimate separation: "I need something outside myself to become complete."

**कार्ममल - Kārma mala**

Action and its residual dispositions reinforce the condition of limitation.

This diagram expresses the particular explanatory progression of the passage, not the entirety of Trika's complex doctrine of the three malas.

Now think about how brilliant this is.

The commentary has moved from the ultimate nature of Anuttara to an explanation of why your thoughts can perpetuate bondage.

And the explanation isn't that consciousness is a defective substance.

It's that the limited subject fails to recognize its true nature and experiences the manifestations of consciousness under the condition of incompleteness.

The same powers can therefore appear as binding or liberating according to whether their real nature is recognized.

That's an extraordinary claim.

And Abhinavagupta is about to make it explicit.


## 16. The terrifying and liberating Goddesses of thought

The commentary now expands on the powers associated with the senses and mental processes.

These powers are described as rays of Śiva.

And they may be classified into gentle and fierce divine forms.

He mentions traditional ritual categories such as pacification, expulsion and destructive rites, along with deities invoked in the associated Tantric systems.

These are historical ritual classifications. Their supernatural effects shouldn't be presented as established practical facts.

But their philosophical function in the present argument is significant.

The same Śakti that manifests cognition can appear as something frightening.

The mind encounters differentiation.

Words arise.

Concepts form.

Fear enters.

And consciousness, failing to recognize its own powers, becomes entangled in the very manifestations that depend upon it.

Now listen to this distinction.

**The powers are not intrinsically separate from Śiva.**

But their manifestation can produce bondage when their divine nature is obscured.

This is one of the reasons Khecarī is such an important concept.

She is not merely an abstract symbol of pleasant awareness.

She encompasses the actual powers of experiencing, thinking, desiring and acting.

### The Sanskrit: Vikalpa

**विकल्प - vikalpa**

Conceptual construction, differentiated determination.

And:

**निर्विकल्प - nirvikalpa**

Without conceptual differentiation.

Now be careful.

It is tempting to assume that the goal is simply to eliminate every *vikalpa* and spend the rest of one's existence in an unchanging state of *nirvikalpa*.

But Abhinavagupta's analysis is more subtle.

He discusses the emergence of conceptual thought from an initially undifferentiated movement.

The thought becomes clothed in words.

It develops through phonemes.

It acquires distinctions.

But the appearance of those distinctions does not by itself sever the underlying continuity of consciousness.

And the liberated knower does not have to regard every conceptual thought as an invasion by a hostile metaphysical substance.

The content may arise.

The movement may occur.

The reality of awareness is not thereby divided into independent pieces.

### The really extraordinary moment

Consider what happens when anger arises.

At the first instant, there may be a simple disturbance.

Then language appears.

A story forms.

The mind introduces a subject.

An object.

An injury.

A future retaliation.

And the original movement becomes elaborated into an extensive conceptual structure.

Abhinavagupta's point concerns the relationship between that conceptual structure and the awareness in which it arises.

**Recognition reveals that conceptual differentiation has never established a second, independent consciousness.**

And this is where the teaching becomes almost impossibly radical.

Because it means the very movement that appears as bondage can disclose the nature of the consciousness from which it manifests.

But a qualification is essential.

This is not an ethical exemption.

Recognizing anger as a movement of consciousness doesn't automatically make angry behaviour wise, harmless or justified.

Nor does the mere experience of anger count as liberation.

The issue is recognition of the real nature of the power, not permission to indulge every impulse.

Abhinavagupta is distinguishing the ontological ground of an experience from the contracted identification that makes it binding.

### The wheel of powers

He cites a teaching from the Spanda tradition in which mastery involves becoming the lord of the wheel of powers.

**चक्रेश्वर - cakreśvara**

Lord of the circle or wheel.

Break it down:

*cakra + īśvara*

Notice the sandhi:

**a + ī → e**

Thus:

*cakra + īśvara → cakreśvara*

Another beautiful Sanskrit formation.

And the meaning?

The individual who formerly experienced the powers as forces governing and binding him comes to recognize their inseparability from the supreme consciousness.

The wheel hasn't vanished.

Its relation to the experiencer has changed.

The one formerly enthralled by the powers becomes established in their real nature.

This is not psychological self-control alone, although it has implications for the way mental experiences are encountered.

It is a metaphysical transformation of the experienced relationship between consciousness and its manifestations.

## 17. The great conclusion - Khecarī Samatā is liberation

And now Abhinavagupta finally brings the whole argument together.

We've travelled through the sixteen meanings of Anuttara.

The Heart-knot.

The structure of *kula*.

The perfection of the totality.

The meaning of immediacy.

The fourfold apparatus of knowing.

The powers of the Goddess.

The three malas.

The emergence of thoughts.

And he arrives at the recognition of Khecarī's fundamental equality or sameness.

**That equality is liberation.**

Not merely a poetic comparison.

In his account, liberation is tied to recognition of the always-manifest essential nature of Anuttara.

And the culminating language invokes the union of Śakti and Śiva, established through their reflexive awareness and bliss.

### The Sanskrit: Saṅghaṭṭa

**सङ्घट्ट - saṅghaṭṭa**

Coming together, impact, conjunction, union.

In this context, the profound union of divine powers.

The spelling is worth learning.

सङ् + घट्ट

The consonant before *gha* is the velar nasal **ङ् - ṅ**.

This is a Sanskrit phonological feature, not an arbitrary decorative mark.

The consonant is articulated in the same general region of the mouth as *k* and *g*.

This is the kind of sound correspondence that becomes increasingly intuitive as you learn the language.

And then:

**समापत्ति - samāpatti**

Attainment, complete convergence, absorption or coming together.

Followed by:

**आनन्द - ānanda**

Bliss.

And:

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness.

The culmination is not simply:

"Everything is one."

That would be a disappointingly thin conclusion to such an enormous commentary.

It is that the apparent plurality of conscious powers is recognized in its inseparability from the self-luminous, reflexively aware reality of Bhairava.

The subject is not destroyed.

The object is not discarded.

The powers are not abolished.

Their fundamental reality is recognized.

And the unity is not a blank absence of differentiation.

It is a freedom capable of manifesting the most extraordinary diversity without becoming metaphysically fragmented.

### The example of an ordinary thought

Let's finish with a simple example.

Suppose you're sitting quietly.

A thought appears:

"I want something."

Immediately, the mind may create the structure:

I am incomplete.

There is an object that will complete me.

I must acquire that object.

My happiness depends upon the result.

And the entire machinery begins.

Now compare that with what Abhinavagupta has established.

The thought is a manifestation of conscious power.

Its differentiated content appears within awareness.

The apparently limited subject also appears within awareness.

The recognition of the true nature of the experiencer is not another object to be acquired.

It is an apprehension of the universal ground of the whole event.

You might still choose to acquire the object.

You might not.

The physical and practical distinctions don't necessarily disappear.

But the fundamental identification underlying the experience may be transformed.

And that is why *samatā* matters.

Not because every experience feels identical.

Not because desire and pain become empirically indistinguishable.

Not because a liberated person is guaranteed perpetual pleasant emotion.

But because the differentiated movements of consciousness no longer need to establish an ultimately separate, incomplete identity.

**Khecarī's powers are recognized as manifestations of the one supreme consciousness.**

And this, according to the commentary, is the liberation sought in the Goddess's question.


## The entire argument, in one movement

Let's gather the Sanskrit we've learned into its philosophical progression.

**अनुत्तर - Anuttara**

The unsurpassable reality, not confined by a hierarchy.


**हृदय - Hṛdaya**

The supreme Heart, the ground of manifestation.


**कुल - Kula**

The differentiated totality, inseparable from consciousness.


**कौलिकी सिद्धिः - Kaulikī Siddhiḥ**

The perfection associated with the recognition and manifestation of that totality.


**सद्यः - Sadyaḥ**

Immediately, ultimately not confined to ordinary temporal succession.


**विज्ञातमात्रेण - Vijñātamātreṇa**

Through the mere recognition of that which grounds knowing itself.


**खेचरीसमता - Khecarī-samatā**

The equality of the Goddess's powers in their undivided divine reality.

And now the genius of the original question becomes apparent.

The Goddess doesn't ask for an entirely new reality.

She asks how the unsurpassable gives perfection through knowing.

And Abhinavagupta's reply gradually reveals that the knowing, the knower, the known, the means of knowledge, the powers of cognition and the universe of manifestation all depend upon the same conscious reality.

The question has become an exposition of the whole Trika metaphysics.

What began as a question about *siddhi* has turned into an account of consciousness and its freedom.

And the truly remarkable thing is that none of this requires the world to be annihilated.

The world continues.

Thoughts continue.

Words continue.

The senses continue.

But their apparent separateness does not ultimately compromise the fullness of consciousness.

The Śiva of Trika is not a remote deity waiting on a distant mountain.

Nor is Śiva merely the limited ego inflated to cosmic proportions.

Śiva is the universal consciousness whose expressive power manifests the entire field of experience.

And the Goddess's question asks how that reality can be recognized.

Not after the creation of another reality.

Not after an impossible journey outside consciousness.

But through realization of what is already fundamental.

**That is the extraordinary claim at the heart of this first verse.**

And three especially useful grammatical patterns:

**1. Vowel strengthening**

कुल → कौल

*kula → kaula*

**2. Absolutive formation**

ज्ञा → ज्ञात्वा

*jñā → jñātvā*, having known.

**3. Abstract noun formation**

सम → समता

*sama → samatā*, equality.

Once those patterns become familiar, Sanskrit philosophical prose becomes much easier to dismantle and understand.
