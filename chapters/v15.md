# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XV - The One Power That Knows, Wills, and Acts

*Abhinavagupta summons Somānanda, asks whether Śiva can know a pot without wanting to know it, discovers that the universe begins with something like the first tremor of a hand about to close into a fist, and then transforms the entire Sanskrit alphabet into thirty-six tattvas through the concentrated power of divine emission.*

Commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series, pp. 178-183


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

We've finally assembled the sacred vowel-system.

The simple vowels disclosed the supreme powers of consciousness.

The unusual vocalic vowels **ऋ ॠ ऌ ॡ** carried us into the subtle relations of activity and repose.

The combined vowels **ए ऐ ओ औ** expressed the mutual involvement of the earlier powers.

Then **अं - aṃ**, Bindu, gathered differentiated manifestation into awareness.

And **अः - aḥ**, Visarga, expressed the divine power of emission.

Finally, Abhinavagupta declared that will, knowledge and action aren't three independent substances.

They're three distinguishable manifestations of the sovereign freedom of Bhairava.

Now, at Kashmir Series pp. 178-179, he turns to Somānanda.

And this is going to be particularly interesting.

Because Somānanda is not merely repeating the familiar claim that everything is consciousness.

He asks a considerably more technical question:

**Can the supreme consciousness ever exist without will, knowledge and action?**

Even when it's resting in its own bliss?

Even before any external object is manifested?

Even when nothing is being visibly created?

And how does the first movement of divine will arise without making Śiva dependent on something outside himself?

Somānanda's answer involves some extraordinary Sanskrit.

But also some very ordinary examples.

A pot.

A hand.

Water.

Waves.

And the subtle inclination preceding overt action.

The extraordinary thing is that Abhinavagupta is using these philosophical arguments to explain the emergence of consonants from vowels.

We are not abandoning the alphabet.

We're finally explaining how the alphabet becomes the universe.

### The textual distinction that matters

At p. 178, Abhinavagupta completes a quotation from the seventh *āhnika* of the Śivadṛṣṭi and then turns to its first *āhnika*, described in the commentary as the *Saṃpradāya-prathamāhnika*.

The Kashmir Series contains a particularly long square-bracketed explanation **inside the first cited verse**. That editorial gloss must be skipped when reconstructing the continuous root quotation.

And later, around p. 180, the edition again inserts illustrative verses in brackets.

We'll use those illustrations, because they're excellent, but label them properly rather than pretending they're part of Abhinavagupta's uninterrupted prose. ; \]

Right.

Let's hear Somānanda.


## 1. From the seventh āhnika - How many Śivas does the universe contain?

We resume precisely after Abhinavagupta's appeal to the seventh chapter of the *Śivadṛṣṭi*.

Its surviving conclusion states:

**एकमेव हि तत्तत्त्वं न सङ्ख्यातोऽतिरिक्तता।**

*Ekam eva hi tat tattvaṃ na saṃkhyāto 'tiriktatā.*

There is indeed only that one principle; numbering does not establish something independently additional.

What does this mean?

We've just distinguished:

- Five principal groups of phonemes.
- Several levels of speech.
- Multiple arrangements of Mātṛkā and Mālinī.
- Will, knowledge and action.
- Bindu and Visarga.
- Thirty-six tattvas.

A great many distinctions.

And yet Somānanda insists that numerical enumeration does not manufacture additional ultimate realities.

Now, that doesn't mean the distinctions are completely imaginary in every respect.

They organize the manifestation.

They have intelligible functions.

But their differences do not establish a second supreme consciousness existing independently of Śiva.

### Saṃkhyātaḥ - सङ्ख्यातः

Here's a useful grammatical detail.

**सङ्ख्या - saṃkhyā**

Number, enumeration, reckoning.

The form **saṃkhyātaḥ** in this verse means *from the standpoint of counting* or *by reason of number*.

Don't mistake it for the ordinary ablative singular of the feminine noun *saṃkhyā*, which would be *saṃkhyāyāḥ*.

The form uses an adverbial *-tas* construction.

And:

**अतिरिक्तता - atiriktatā**

Additionality, independent excess or separateness.

From:

**अतिरिक्त - atirikta**

Additional, beyond, distinct.

With the abstract suffix **-ता - -tā**.

The philosophical sense is simple.

Counting fifty sacred phonemes does not establish fifty independent absolutes.

The number distinguishes their manifestations.

It does not divide the supreme ground.

### Abhinavagupta's actual conclusion

His prose then characterizes the Śiva-principle through an enormous compound containing:

**अनन्त - ananta** - endless.

**विचित्र - vicitra** - diverse, variegated.

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya** - sovereign freedom.

**स्फुरण - sphuraṇa** - flashing forth, manifestation.

**शक्ति - śakti** - power.

**चमत्कार - camatkāra** - wonder, delighted self-revelation.

**भैरवभाव - bhairavabhāva** - the condition or reality of Bhairava.

The argument is that the infinite diversity of divine manifestation belongs to the self-revealing freedom of the one Śiva-principle.

And now he says that the same relationship is explained in the first *āhnika* of the inherited philosophical teaching.

This is where the next verse begins.


## 2. स यदास्ते - What is Śiva doing when he's doing nothing?

SOMĀNANDA'S ŚIVADṚṢṬI, FIRST ĀHNIKA · QUOTED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA

**स यदास्ते चिदाह्लादमात्रानुभवतल्लयः।**

*Sa yadāste cidāhlādamātrānubhavatallayaḥ.*

When he abides absorbed in nothing but the experience of conscious delight...

This is the beginning of the quoted verse.

The next half asserts that his will, knowledge and action are present even there.

Now that is a wonderfully provocative claim.

Because if Śiva is simply resting in his own bliss, what is there to will?

What is there to know?

What is there to do?

Doesn't action require a particular activity?

Doesn't knowing require an object?

Doesn't willing require something desired?

Somānanda's answer is **not at the fundamental level of divine freedom**.

And the grammar reveals the intended state.

### Saḥ - सः

He.

Masculine nominative singular.

The pronoun refers to the Supreme Lord.

### Yadā - यदा

When.

An indeclinable introducing a temporal condition.

Now, ordinarily, *yadā* establishes a relation between events or states.

But here it is being used in the description of the supreme reality's repose.

Don't therefore assume Somānanda is asserting that Śiva's ultimate nature begins at a particular clock-time.

The sequential expression belongs to the teaching.

Remember the distinction between *krama* and *akrama*.

### Āste - आस्ते

He sits, remains, abides.

From √**ās**, to sit or remain.

Third-person singular present middle voice.

Now compare:

**अस्ति - asti**

He or it is.

From √*as*, to be.

**आस्ते - āste**

He sits or abides.

The forms are not interchangeable.

Their root formations and grammatical uses differ.

### Cidāhlāda - चिदाह्लाद

Now the beautiful compound.

**चित् - cit**

Consciousness.

**आह्लाद - āhlāda**

Delight, joy, blissful refreshment.

Thus:

**cidāhlāda**

The delight of consciousness.

This isn't merely pleasure from a sensory object.

Somānanda is describing the supreme Self's repose in its own conscious nature.

### Mātra - मात्र

Only, mere, nothing but.

So:

**चिदाह्लादमात्र - cidāhlādamātra**

Nothing but conscious delight.

### Anubhava - अनुभव

Experience, apprehension.

And:

**तल्लय - tallaya**

Absorbed in that.

**तत् - tat**

That.

**लय - laya**

Absorption, merging, dissolution.

Thus the compound describes the Lord as absorbed in the experience of conscious delight alone.

### A universe with no outstanding administrative business

Imagine the Supreme Lord isn't creating a galaxy.

He isn't producing another tattva.

He isn't arranging thirty-six metaphysical departments.

He is simply abiding in his own conscious fullness.

Now the opponent says:

"Excellent. So there's no will."

Somānanda replies:

"Not quite."

"No knowledge?"

"Also no."

"No action?"

"Still no."

The opponent stares at him.

"Then what the hell do those words mean?"

And that is precisely the question the next lines answer.


## 3. The three powers do not clock off when creation stops

Somānanda's verse continues:

**तदिच्छा तावती तावज्ज्ञानं तावत्क्रिया हि सा।**

*Tadicchā tāvatī tāvaj jñānaṃ tāvat kriyā hi sā.*

The will is of that extent; knowledge is of that extent; and so too is action.

Now the repetition is important.

**तावत् - tāvat**

So much, of that extent, that much.

**तावती - tāvatī**

The corresponding feminine form.

The verse uses these forms to say that will, knowledge and action aren't absent merely because they aren't manifesting as distinct relations to external objects.

They are present in the reality of conscious delight.

And this brings us to one of Somānanda's most important ideas.

**The powers can exist in complete mutual unity without yet being explicitly distinguished.**

### Śaktitritaya - शक्तित्रितय

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

**त्रितय - tritaya**

A group of three.

Thus:

**शक्तित्रितय - śaktitritaya**

The triad of powers.

### Sāmarasya - सामरस्य

Harmony, equality of essence, complete coalescence.

This is one of the most beautiful technical words in Trika.

Its meaning is not simply that three independent entities have agreed to cooperate.

It describes the condition of being thoroughly united in one nature.

The verse calls this:

**सुसूक्ष्मशक्तित्रितयसामरस्य**

*Susūkṣmaśaktitritayasāmarasya.*

The harmony of the exceedingly subtle triad of powers.

Now the grammar.

**सु - su**

Good, excellent, very, intensive.

**सूक्ष्म - sūkṣma**

Subtle.

Together:

**सुसूक्ष्म - susūkṣma**

Exceedingly subtle.

Then:

**śaktitritaya**

Triad of powers.

And:

**sāmarasya**

Unity or harmonious coalescence.

The complete compound describes the three powers in their subtle, nonseparate condition.

### What makes them subtle?

Not that they're physically tiny.

Nor that they can only be detected by a particularly sophisticated microscope.

Their subtlety concerns the absence of fully articulated distinctions among:

The one who wills.

What is willed.

The act of willing.

And similarly for knowledge and action.

At this level, the three powers are present, but not differentiated through the ordinary object-relations by which the finite individual recognizes them.

And that is why Somānanda can maintain that Śiva possesses will, knowledge and action even while resting in his own conscious bliss.

### A critical editorial distinction

The Kashmir Series places a long square-bracketed explanation between the opening words *sa yadāste* and the continuation of the cited verse. It discusses the absence of explicit object-distinctions and explains how will, knowledge and action can remain present in a unified condition.

That interpretation is useful, but **the bracketed discussion is editorial material**.

The continuous first-*āhnika* quotation is preserved more cleanly in the GRETIL transcription. Its two verses belong to Somānanda, while the enormous explanatory paragraph belongs to the edition's annotation. ; \]

The distinction matters because Somānanda's own formulation is remarkably economical.

The powers are present.

Their subtle harmony is undivided.

And Śiva's nature remains conscious delight.

Not three separate divine managers holding an emergency meeting.

One self-revealing freedom.


## 4. The next question - Can Śiva know a pot without willing to know it?

Now Somānanda moves from supreme bliss to something deeply ordinary.

A pot.

Again.

The poor pot has become the most overworked teaching assistant in Indian philosophy.

Earlier, Abhinavagupta used pot-cognition to distinguish the known object from self-manifesting awareness.

Now Somānanda is asking a different question.

**What powers are already involved when the pot is known?**

The cited verses occur in *Śivadṛṣṭi* 1.24-25.

The opening is transmitted as:

**घटादिग्रहकालेऽपि घटं जानाति सा क्रिया।**

*Ghaṭādigrahakāle 'pi ghaṭaṃ jānāti sā kriyā.*

Even when apprehending a pot or similar object, the knowing of the pot is activity.

Now that is a striking statement.

Knowing is an activity.

Not merely a passive reception of something external.

But Somānanda pushes further.

The verse also relates knowing to will, and says that cognition would be impaired without it.

### Ghaṭādigrahakāle

Let's dismantle this splendid compound.

**घट - ghaṭa**

Pot.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning with, and so forth.

**ग्रह - graha**

Grasping, apprehension.

Related to √**ग्रह् - grah**, to grasp.

**काल - kāla**

Time, occasion.

Now:

**घटादिग्रहकाल - ghaṭādigrahakāla**

The occasion of apprehending a pot or similar object.

Locative singular:

**घटादिग्रहकाले - ghaṭādigrahakāle**

At the time of apprehending the pot.

And:

**अपि - api**

Even, also.

The continuous form:

*ghaṭādigrahakāle 'pi*

Uses an avagraha to indicate the elided initial *a* in the conventional written sandhi.

### Jānāti - जानाति

He, she or it knows.

From √**ज्ञा - jñā**, to know.

Now compare:

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

**ज्ञातुम् - jñātum**

To know.

**ज्ञात - jñāta**

Known.

All belong to the same root-family.

And:

### Sā kriyā - सा क्रिया

That is action.

**सा - sā**

That, feminine nominative singular.

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action, activity.

Feminine nominative singular.

The verse isn't simply equating every mental occurrence with visible bodily motion.

It's arguing that knowing itself involves an operative power of consciousness.

Now that creates a serious philosophical distinction.

In ordinary language, we may contrast knowing and doing.

"I know how to build a chair."

Versus:

"I am building a chair."

Somānanda doesn't deny their functional difference.

He argues that the knowing-power itself cannot be understood as entirely devoid of activity.

Its manifestation belongs to the agency of consciousness.

### The second manuscript reading

Here the textual comparison is particularly valuable.

The Kashmir Series transcription has a damaged-looking first pāda ending **यावसा**, and GRETIL marks the beginning of the quotation as incomplete.

But an independent continuous edition of *Śivadṛṣṭi* 1.24 preserves the reading:

**घटं जानाति सा क्रिया**

*Ghaṭaṃ jānāti sā kriyā.*

This supports the philosophically straightforward statement: knowing the pot is action.

I'm using that reading where the witnesses differ, rather than pretending the garbled Kashmir Series form is unproblematic. ; \]

And now the really important question.

What makes consciousness turn toward the pot at all?

Somānanda's answer:

**Will.**


## 5. निरिच्छोर्वेदनक्षतिः - What happens if there is no will?

The same verse contains:

**निरिच्छोर्वेदनक्षतिः।**

*Niricchor vedanakṣatiḥ.*

For one devoid of will, there is an impairment or absence of the relevant knowing.

Let's dismantle it.

### Niriccha - निरिच्छ

Without will or desire.

From:

**निर् - nir-**

Without, away from.

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will.

Thus:

**निरिच्छ - niriccha**

Without will.

The form in the verse is:

**निरिच्छोः - niricchoḥ**

Genitive singular of the relevant *-a* stem, after the appropriate inflection and sandhi.

Now:

### Vedana - वेदन

Knowing, apprehension, awareness.

From √**विद् - vid**, to know.

The related feminine noun:

**वेदना - vedanā**

Can mean awareness, experience or feeling according to context.

Here the concern is the occurrence of cognition.

### Kṣati - क्षति

Damage, impairment, loss.

From √**क्षि / क्षण्** in the lexical history of destruction and harm.

The immediate meaning is loss or impairment.

Now the compound:

**वेदनक्षति - vedanakṣati**

Loss or impairment of knowing.

And:

**वेदनक्षतिः - vedanakṣatiḥ**

Nominative singular.

So the point is that the relevant cognition cannot be entirely divorced from will.

### Does this mean we must consciously decide to perceive everything?

No.

That would be much too crude.

You can hear an unexpected sound without first explicitly thinking:

"I hereby choose to exercise my hearing faculty."

Somānanda's **icchā** is not restricted to deliberate, verbally formulated decisions.

The underlying issue is **the orientation or directedness of the power of consciousness**.

This is why the next term is so important.

**औन्मुख्य - aunmukhya.**

The initial turning-toward.

The first subtle inclination.

Now, within Somānanda's system, even a cognition that appears involuntary can be understood through a deeper structure of divine freedom.

That is a metaphysical explanation.

It isn't a neuroscientific claim that every stimulus requires a prior conscious decision.

But it reveals something central to his philosophy.

**Knowing and doing are not independent of the will-power inherent in consciousness.**

And that is why *icchāśakti* is so fundamental.


## 6. The three powers cannot be separated without destroying the phenomenon you're explaining

Somānanda's next verse relates the absence of *aunmukhya* to the cessation of the relevant activity.

It also says that one does not proceed toward something aversive without the corresponding conscious orientation, and that one does not know without consciousness.

Now we need to keep the three powers distinct.

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action.

Somānanda is not saying all three words mean precisely the same thing.

Their functions are distinguishable.

But their ultimate conscious reality is inseparable.

### The example of an unpleasant object

Imagine something disgusting appears in front of you.

You recoil.

Now what happened?

The object was apprehended.

There was a differentiating awareness of it.

And an orientation of avoidance appeared.

That orientation doesn't require a pleasant desire to possess the object.

Will can take the form of rejection or withdrawal.

This is important because people often imagine *icchā* must mean attraction.

But will is not identical with liking.

A movement away from something can still involve a directed conscious power.

The Sanskrit uses:

**द्वेष्य - dveṣya**

What is disliked, hateful or aversive.

Related to √**द्विष् - dviṣ**, to hate or be hostile.

And:

**प्रवर्तते - pravartate**

Engages, proceeds, becomes active.

From:

* pra-√vṛt*, to proceed or operate.

Now this is a subtle relationship.

The directedness of consciousness is not simply one pleasant emotion among others.

It can underlie attraction, aversion and other kinds of action.

And the relevant knowledge-power cannot be removed from the occurrence without changing what the event is.

### The terrible philosophical vending machine

Imagine trying to construct cognition from three independently purchased components.

First you buy knowledge.

Then will.

Then action.

But the machine won't dispense knowledge without awareness.

It won't provide meaningful action without direction.

And when you try to remove all operative power from will, nothing actually turns toward an object.

Somānanda's point is that the three can't be assembled as unrelated substances after the fact.

Their relation belongs to the structure of consciousness.

And he now makes that claim explicit through the most important term in this section.

**Aunmukhya.**


## 7. औन्मुख्य - The first tiny leaning of consciousness

## औन्मुख्य

*Aunmukhya* - orientation, eagerness, turning-toward.

This is a genuinely beautiful Sanskrit word.

And it's central to Somānanda's distinctive account of divine agency.

### The morphology

Start with:

**मुख - mukha**

Face, mouth, direction.

Now:

**उन्मुख - unmukha**

Facing toward, oriented upward or outward, attentive.

The prefix:

**उद् - ud**

Up, out, forth.

Combines with *mukha* to produce the familiar directional sense.

Then:

**उन्मुखता - unmukhatā**

The condition of being turned toward something.

And the abstract derivative:

**औन्मुख्य - aunmukhya**

Orientation, eagerness, the condition of turning toward.

Notice the initial vowel change:

**उ - u → औ - au**

This is a *vṛddhi*-type strengthening in the derivative formation.

And the final:

**-ख्य - -khya**

Belongs to the nominal derivative structure.

The whole word expresses a disposition of turning toward.

Not necessarily a gross physical movement.

### The importance of the face metaphor

Why *mukha*, face?

Because directing your face toward something is an ordinary image of orientation.

You turn toward a sound.

Toward a person.

Toward a task.

Your attention becomes engaged.

But Somānanda is using the metaphor for the much subtler beginning of the divine power of will.

Before an overt action is completed.

Before the object of action is fully articulated.

There is the beginning of an orientation.

The initial movement of expression.

That's *aunmukhya*.

### The difference between aunmukhya and explicit icchā

This is crucial.

Suppose someone says:

"I want to write a book."

That's an explicit intention.

But before the intention takes that definite form, there may be a more subtle inclination toward expression.

The impulse is not yet formulated as a detailed project.

But a directedness is emerging.

Somānanda calls attention to that preliminary condition.

It's not the absence of power.

And it's not yet the full differentiated action.

It is the first movement toward manifestation.

### The scholarly qualification

This interpretation isn't merely a loose modern extrapolation.

Studies of Somānanda's *Śivadṛṣṭi* identify **aunmukhya** as a characteristic concept of his philosophy: the initial eagerness preceding the explicit formation of will and action.

John Nemec's *The Ubiquitous Śiva* and Raffaele Torella's work on Somānanda's philosophy discuss its role in divine agency and the stages of manifestation. ; \]

And now the argument becomes particularly interesting.

**The first orientation can be described through a sequence without making Śiva himself dependent upon a sequence.**

Remember the distinction between *krama* and *akrama*.

Our explanation can analyse the emergence of divine will.

But the supreme consciousness isn't a finite agent waiting for the next instant to acquire a missing capacity.

The stages articulate its power.

They don't manufacture it.

And that's why the next verse says the two aspects of activity must be distinguished.


## 8. Will itself has two phases - Before and after the turning-toward

Somānanda now states:

**तस्याः पूर्वापरौ भागौ कल्पनीयौ।**

*Tasyāḥ pūrvāparau bhāgau kalpanīyau.*

Two aspects of that activity-the earlier and the later-are to be distinguished.

This comes from *Śivadṛṣṭi* 1.19, quoted by Abhinavagupta around p. 180.

Now this is important.

We have just established that divine will doesn't disappear in the supreme condition.

But when willing is considered through its manifested relation to a particular object, we can distinguish aspects.

An initial orientation.

And a more explicit manifestation.

### Pūrvāparau - पूर्वापरौ

**पूर्व - pūrva**

Earlier, former.

**अपर - apara**

Later, subsequent.

The combined form:

**पूर्वापरौ - pūrvāparau**

The earlier and later, masculine dual.

Notice **-au**.

Here it really is a dual ending.

Not a locative singular like *śaktau*.

Not a general signal that any word ending in *-au* means "two."

You must identify the stem and grammatical context.

### Bhāgau - भागौ

Two parts or portions.

From:

**भाग - bhāga**

Part, portion, share.

Masculine nominative dual:

**भागौ - bhāgau**.

So:

**pūrvāparau bhāgau**

The earlier and later parts.

And:

### Kalpanīyau - कल्पनीयौ

To be conceived, distinguished or posited.

From √**कॢप् - kḷp**, to arrange, be suitable or form.

The verbal adjective:

**कल्पनीय - kalpanīya**

To be conceived or formulated.

Dual:

**कल्पनीयौ - kalpanīyau**.

Three masculine dual forms agree.

That's beautiful Sanskrit grammar.

And it gives us the philosophical instruction:

**Distinguish two aspects of the manifestation of willing.**

### The first phase: Aunmukhya

The first is the subtle orientation.

The readiness.

The turn toward a possible manifestation.

Not yet the complete external performance.

### The second phase: Explicit will and activity

The orientation becomes more determined.

The object or activity is specified.

The power becomes engaged through more explicit knowing and action.

Now here's the important point.

Somānanda does not infer that Śiva himself becomes a separate material object because his activity can be analysed through stages.

**The stages belong to the expression of freedom, not the creation of freedom from something external.**

And now we're ready for the famous analogy.

The water.

The waves.

The trembling hand.

It's an astonishingly concrete set of examples for such an abstract metaphysical claim.

But first, a crucial editorial distinction.


## 9. The missing distinction in the printed layout - The waves are authentic Somānanda, but inserted by the editor

Here's what the Kashmir Series actually does at p. 180.

Abhinavagupta quotes a sequence from the *Śivadṛṣṭi* concerning will and its stages.

Then the editor inserts a substantial **square-bracketed explanation**.

That explanation reproduces other authentic *Śivadṛṣṭi* verses about water becoming wavy and a hand beginning to close into a fist.

The quotation resumes after the bracket.

So we must distinguish three things.

1. **Somānanda actually composed the wave and hand verses.** They are independently attested in *Śivadṛṣṭi* 1.13-15.
2. **The Kashmir Series editor has inserted them here** to clarify *aunmukhya*.
3. **Abhinavagupta's own unbracketed prose does not pause to quote this entire additional run of verses at this position.**

That's the precise textual situation.

And it's worth getting right.

Because there is a difference between saying:

"Abhinavagupta quotes Somānanda on will."

And:

"The editor supplies additional Somānanda verses illustrating the quotation."

Both are valuable.

But they're not identical claims. ; \]

Now that we've established the distinction, let's enjoy the analogy.

Because it is excellent.


## 10. The water before the waves - The first disturbance

Somānanda invokes:

**निस्तरङ्गस्य जलस्य**

*Nistaraṅgasya jalasya.*

Of water without waves.

And the movement toward:

**अतितरङ्गिताम् - atitaraṅgitām**

A highly wavy condition.

Now the image.

Imagine a perfectly calm pond.

The surface is still.

No visible ripples.

Then a disturbance begins.

The water has not yet become a large field of waves.

But something has started.

A slight movement.

The beginning of agitation.

And Somānanda uses that initial transition to illustrate *aunmukhya*.

### Nistaraṅga - निस्तरङ्ग

Without waves.

From:

**निस् - nis**

Without.

**तरङ्ग - taraṅga**

Wave, ripple.

Together:

**निस्तरङ्ग - nistaraṅga**

Waveless.

Now the genitive singular:

**निस्तरङ्गस्य - nistaraṅgasya**

Of the waveless.

And:

### Jalasya - जलस्य

Of water.

**जल - jala**

Water.

Genitive singular:

**जलस्य - jalasya**.

So:

**nistaraṅgasya jalasya**

Of waveless water.

Now:

### Taraṅgita - तरङ्गित

Made wavy, rippled.

And:

**अतितरङ्गिता - atitaraṅgitā**

A condition of intense wave-formation.

Notice the repeated *taraṅga* family.

The image distinguishes the relatively motionless state from the manifest wave-state.

But the crucial object of attention is the transition.

### Ārambha - आरम्भ

Beginning, commencement.

Related to *ā-√rabh*, to undertake or begin.

The verse specifically asks us to look at the beginning.

Not merely the fully formed waves.

That's where *aunmukhya* is recognized.

### The microscopic first movement

Imagine the water has just begun to stir.

An enormous wave hasn't appeared.

But the initial movement is present.

Now Somānanda's analogy illustrates how a more explicit activity can be preceded by a subtler orientation.

The analogy is not a claim that the supreme consciousness is literally made of water.

And we mustn't import the physics of waves as proof of the metaphysics.

But it is a useful way to distinguish:

A relatively undifferentiated condition.

An initial movement.

A fully explicit manifestation.

And that is exactly the structure Somānanda is trying to explain.

### The metaphysical difference

Now someone might object:

"If Śiva enters action, doesn't his nature change into something else?"

Somānanda's broader argument rejects that conclusion.

The movement is a manifestation of his own power.

Not the destruction of his consciousness.

Just as the appearance of waves doesn't require that the water first cease to be water.

Of course, physical water can have changing states in a way that isn't directly identical to the supposedly unsurpassable consciousness of Trika.

The analogy is illustrative, not an ontological identity.

But it reveals the intended relationship.

**Manifestation involves distinguishable phases without requiring the original reality to become completely foreign to itself.**

And now the hand provides an even more immediately recognizable example.


## 11. The hand about to close - The first tremor

Somānanda's next illustration concerns:

**व्रजतो मुष्टितां पाणेः**

*Vrajato muṣṭitāṃ pāṇeḥ.*

Of a hand moving toward the condition of a fist.

And:

**पूर्वः कम्पः - pūrvaḥ kampaḥ**

The initial tremor.

Now imagine your hand resting open.

You're about to close it into a fist.

Before the fist is fully formed, there may be an incipient muscular movement.

The hand begins to orient toward the completed act.

Somānanda takes that first movement as an analogy for *aunmukhya*.

### Pāṇi - पाणि

Hand.

Genitive singular:

**पाणेः - pāṇeḥ**.

Notice the change:

*pāṇi → pāṇeḥ*.

This is another *-i* stem.

### Muṣṭi - मुष्टि

Fist.

Now:

**मुष्टिता - muṣṭitā**

The condition of being a fist.

The abstract suffix **-ता - -tā**.

Accusative singular:

**मुष्टिताम् - muṣṭitām**.

And:

### Vrajataḥ - व्रजतः

Moving, going.

From √**व्रज् - vraj**, to go or move.

Here the form functions in relation to the hand moving toward the fist-condition.

### Kampa - कम्प

Tremor, shaking, vibration.

From √**कम्प् - kamp**, to tremble.

Now the philosophical point.

The tremor is not yet the completed fist.

But neither is it wholly unrelated to the act of making the fist.

It's the beginning of the movement.

And Somānanda wants you to notice that first orientation.

### The extraordinary relevance to practice

Now here's a useful experiential analogy.

Suppose an angry response is about to arise.

Before you speak, before you have formulated a full accusation, you may notice a subtle inclination.

A tightening.

A movement toward a particular interpretation.

A readiness to react.

That preliminary movement may be easier to overlook than the fully developed thought or action.

Now, Somānanda isn't writing a modern psychology manual.

And *aunmukhya* is a metaphysical term concerning the will-power of consciousness.

But the analogy is useful because it makes the distinction experientially intelligible.

**The beginning of an action is not necessarily identical with its fully explicit completion.**

And the beginning can itself be a meaningful expression of agency.

### Why this matters to liberation

Within Trika, contraction and misrecognition can be reinforced through subtle orientations of awareness before they become explicit verbal narratives.

The tradition's account of divine will therefore gives us a framework for examining the beginning of differentiation.

But don't equate the noticing of a bodily tremor with the full realization of Bhairava.

The metaphysical claim is broader.

The incipient movement illustrates how conscious freedom can become manifest through an increasingly determinate activity.

Now Somānanda takes the analogy a step further.

He describes the orientation itself as a subtle expansion of consciousness.

And some earlier thinkers call it a slight swelling.

Which is an extremely funny image for the beginning of the universe.


## 12. किञ्चिदुच्छूनता - The cosmic swelling

In the editorially inserted *Śivadṛṣṭi* passage, Somānanda reports that some earlier masters describe the initial condition as:

**किञ्चिदुच्छूनता**

*Kiñciducchūnatā.*

A slight swelling or expansion.

Now that is an extraordinary metaphor.

The supreme consciousness is not literally inflating like a balloon.

We're talking about the first tendency toward manifestation.

But the image captures a subtle change in explicitness.

### Kiñcit - किञ्चित्

Something, a little, slightly.

A common indefinite form.

### Ucchūna - उच्छून

Swollen, expanded, slightly raised.

Related to a verbal family concerning swelling or expansion.

Now:

**उच्छूनता - ucchūnatā**

The condition of swelling.

And:

**किञ्चिदुच्छूनता - kiñciducchūnatā**

A slight swelling.

Now the important textual point.

Somānanda is reporting a view associated with other great teachers.

The interpretation of *aunmukhya* and this "slight swelling" is historically connected with debates in early Kashmir Śaivism about the nature of Śakti's emergence.

It isn't necessary to flatten every version into one identical definition.

Somānanda's preferred language emphasizes the **initial directedness or eagerness** of consciousness.

The swelling image provides a related way of understanding the beginning of manifestation.

### The cosmic balloon analogy

Imagine somebody explaining creation:

"First there was the Absolute."

"Then what?"

"A slight swelling."

"Of what?"

"Consciousness."

"Into what?"

"Manifestation."

"Did Śiva gain mass?"

"No."

"Did he become spatially larger?"

"No."

"Then why are we talking about swelling?"

Because the metaphor expresses the emergence of distinction, not a literal increase in physical dimensions.

The Absolute doesn't become a bigger object.

Its power becomes manifest through a more explicit form.

That's what the metaphor is trying to describe.

And it leads to the next claim.

**Even this emerging will does not make Śiva a gross material object.**


## 13. शिवः स्थूलत्वभाक् क्वचित् - Śiva doesn't become a lump of matter

Somānanda's statement is:

**न चौन्मुख्यप्रसङ्गेन शिवः स्थूलत्वभाक् क्वचित्।**

*Na caunmukhyaprasaṅgena śivaḥ sthūlatvabhāk kvacit.*

Nor does Śiva, because of the appearance of *aunmukhya*, ever become possessed of gross materiality.

This is a deeply important qualification.

And it's precisely where the Kashmir Series quotation resumes after the large editorial bracket.

Now the grammar.

### Sthūla - स्थूल

Gross, coarse, thick, large.

In the tattva system, the word can refer to the gross manifested levels as distinguished from subtler ones.

### Sthūlatva - स्थूलत्व

Grossness, the condition of being gross.

From:

*sthūla + tva*.

### Bhāj - भाज् / Bhāk - भाक्

Having a share in, possessing or participating in.

The form:

**स्थूलत्वभाक् - sthūlatvabhāk**

Possessing grossness.

Now:

### Kvacit - क्वचित्

Somewhere, at some time, ever in relevant negative constructions.

With:

**न - na**

Not.

So:

**न ... क्वचित्**

Not anywhere, not ever.

The point is emphatic.

Śiva's manifestation of the will-power does not force the supreme consciousness to become a separate gross material object.

### The contrast between levels of manifestation

Now this is essential.

Within the thirty-six-tattva system, the gross elements have specific functions.

Earth.

Water.

Fire.

Air.

Space.

And all the intervening principles.

But their manifestation does not imply that the ultimate ground has lost its own conscious nature.

Śiva doesn't become ontologically trapped inside the gross category merely because the gross category manifests.

The supreme ground remains the basis of the whole structure.

And that is why Somānanda insists on the distinction.

**Manifestation of grossness is not identical to the supreme consciousness becoming an independently existing gross substance.**

### A further textual qualification

The verse belongs to *Śivadṛṣṭi* 1.17, not to a continuous series of entirely adjacent verses following 1.19.

Abhinavagupta's commentary cites relevant passages selectively, and the Kashmir Series editor inserts further examples into that quotation.

The proper philosophical sequence is coherent, but the printed selection should not be confused with a continuous reproduction of Somānanda's entire first chapter. ; \]

Now the argument has reached its conclusion.

Will is not absent from the supreme state.

It becomes manifest through *aunmukhya*.

Its activity is related to knowledge and action.

Yet the supreme consciousness does not become a gross material substance.

And therefore the whole universe can be understood as the manifestation of Śiva's power.

Which is exactly what Abhinavagupta says next.

The universe is emitted.

The Sanskrit alphabet becomes a differentiated matrix.

And the sixteenth power comes into view.

This is where the metaphysics becomes phonemic again.


## 14. स एष परमेश्वरो विसृजति विश्वम् - And thus the Lord emits the universe

Now Abhinavagupta returns to his own prose.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · PP. 180-181

**स एष परमेश्वरो विसृजति विश्वम्।**

*Sa eṣa parameśvaro visṛjati viśvam.*

This very Supreme Lord emits the universe.

Beautiful.

Short.

And, after all the previous argument, extraordinarily dense.

Let's learn every part.

### Sa eṣa - स एष

This very one.

**सः - saḥ**

He.

**एषः - eṣaḥ**

This one.

In connected speech:

*saḥ eṣaḥ → sa eṣaḥ*.

The final visarga changes according to the sandhi environment.

Then:

### Parameśvaraḥ - परमेश्वरः

The Supreme Lord.

**परम - parama**

Supreme.

**ईश्वर - īśvara**

Lord, sovereign.

The combination:

*parama + īśvara → parameśvara*.

Because:

**a + ī → e**.

And masculine nominative singular:

**परमेश्वरः - parameśvaraḥ**.

### Visṛjati - विसृजति

Emits, releases, sends forth, creates.

From:

**वि - vi**

Outward, apart, variously.

**√सृज् - sṛj**

To emit, let go, release or create.

Now:

**विसृजति - visṛjati**

Third-person singular present.

And:

### Viśvam - विश्वम्

The universe, all that is.

Neuter accusative singular.

The object of *visṛjati*.

So the sentence is grammatically straightforward:

The Supreme Lord emits the universe.

But what does that mean metaphysically?

### Creation isn't production from a foreign material

Somānanda has already established that the supreme consciousness possesses the powers of will, knowledge and action even in its undivided state.

Now the manifestation of those powers can be understood as the emergence of the universe.

But the universe isn't produced from an independent substance completely outside Śiva.

Nor does Śiva cease to be conscious when the universe appears.

The differentiated universe belongs to his own expressive power.

That's the Trika claim.

Now here's the critical distinction.

**This is not merely a claim that a powerful deity creates an independent universe and then watches it from outside.**

It's a nondual account of manifestation.

The ultimate reality of the universe is not independent of the conscious freedom through which it appears.

This is why the verb *visṛjati* is so important.

Emission doesn't mean that consciousness throws an independently existing substance into a second universe.

It means that the supreme power manifests differentiated forms.

And the next phrase identifies the full scope of those forms.

**From Earth through Śakti.**

The entire tattva-system.


## 15. धरादिशक्त्यन्तम् - From Earth to Śakti, everything is included

The commentary says:

**धरादिशक्त्यन्तं कादिक्षान्तरूपम्।**

*Dharādiśaktyantaṃ kādikṣāntarūpam.*

Having the range from Earth to Śakti and the phonemic form extending from *ka* to *kṣa*.

Now there's our long-awaited transition.

The universe is being interpreted through the consonantal series.

The vowel-powers have unfolded.

And now the differentiated tattvas are expressed through consonants.

Let's actually dismantle the compounds.

### Dharā - धरा

Earth.

From the root-family √**धृ - dhṛ**, to hold or support.

Now the ordinary noun:

**धरा - dharā**

That which supports; Earth.

It is a feminine noun.

And:

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning.

So:

**धरादि - dharādi**

Beginning with Earth.

### Śakti - शक्ति

Power.

And:

**अन्त - anta**

End, limit.

So:

**शक्त्यन्त - śaktyanta**

Ending with Śakti.

Notice the vowel transformation.

*śakti + anta → śaktyanta.*

The final *i* becomes the semivowel *y* before the following vowel.

Thus:

**धरादिशक्त्यन्त - dharādiśaktyanta**

Beginning with Earth and extending to Śakti.

Now the second compound.

### Kādi - कादि

Beginning with *ka*.

**क - ka**

The first consonant in the ordinary Sanskrit consonantal series.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning.

So:

*ka + ādi → kādi.*

### Kṣānta - क्षान्त

Ending with *kṣa*.

But be careful.

This is a phonemic compound involving:

**क्ष - kṣa**

And:

**अन्त - anta**

End.

The form:

**क्षान्त - kṣānta**

Contains the long ā through sandhi.

It resembles another Sanskrit adjective *kṣānta*, patient or endured, but the present context is explicitly the range of the phonemic series.

So we should segment it according to the alphabetic discussion.

### Kādi-kṣānta - कादिक्षान्त

Beginning with *ka*, ending with *kṣa*.

And:

**रूप - rūpa**

Form, nature.

So:

**कादिक्षान्तरूप - kādikṣāntarūpa**

Having the form of the series from *ka* to *kṣa*.

Now we've got the complete meaning.

The universe as the differentiated tattva-system is expressed through the consonantal powers.

And that gives us the transition from the earlier vowels to the concrete differentiated world.

### The philosophical relationship

Remember the previous structure.

**Vowels** express divine seed-powers.

**Consonants** express the differentiated matrices in which those powers become manifest.

Now this doesn't mean vowels are intrinsically divine while consonants are somehow evil or spiritually inferior.

Their distinction is functional and symbolic.

The consonantal series expresses the differentiation of manifestation.

But the differentiated series remains grounded in the divine vowel-powers.

And that is exactly what the next pages explain.

The consonants arise through the **condensation** of the vowels.

The divine powers become dense, specific and articulated.

But they don't lose their ultimate conscious nature.

### The alphabet becomes a universe

Think about a word.

A vowel alone can form a syllabic nucleus.

A consonant ordinarily requires a vowel in the syllabic system we're studying.

The consonant contributes a particular articulation.

Together, they form differentiated sound.

Now Abhinavagupta interprets that phonological relationship cosmologically.

The vowel-powers express the underlying divine energy.

The consonantal powers articulate its differentiated manifestation.

The physical sound system supplies the symbolic framework.

The metaphysical doctrine supplies the significance.

And the whole arrangement remains one expression of consciousness.

That's the transition.

But now there's an important question.

If the differentiated universe extends through the consonants, what is the supreme power through which that emission occurs?

Abhinavagupta answers:

**Ṣoḍaśī Kalā.**

The sixteenth power.

And this is one of the most beautiful and easily misunderstood ideas in the passage.


## 16. षोडशी कला - The immortal sixteenth power

Now the text says:

**विसर्गशक्तिः षोडशी कलेति गीयते।**

*Visargaśaktiḥ ṣoḍaśī kaleti gīyate.*

The power of emission is called the sixteenth *kalā*.

This is a crucial identification.

And it connects the entire phonemic cosmology with the lunar symbolism from the root Tantra.

### Ṣoḍaśa - षोडश

Sixteen.

Now this is a lovely Sanskrit word to learn.

It isn't simply written as the transparent modern expression *daśa + ṣaṭ*.

The conventional numeral is:

**षोडश - ṣoḍaśa**.

Sixteen.

Now the ordinal:

**षोडश - ṣoḍaśa**

Sixteenth, masculine/neuter in relevant uses.

And feminine:

**षोडशी - ṣoḍaśī**

Sixteenth.

The noun being qualified is:

**कला - kalā**.

Feminine.

Thus:

**षोडशी कला - ṣoḍaśī kalā**

The sixteenth power or phase.

### Kalā - कला

Part, portion, digit, power, aspect.

Now this word is particularly rich.

In lunar contexts, *kalā* can denote a lunar digit or phase-unit.

In Tantric cosmology it can express an aspect or power of manifestation.

And in the thirty-six-tattva system *Kalā* is also a specific limiting principle.

Those meanings are related historically and symbolically in some contexts, but they're not automatically interchangeable.

Here the sixteenth *kalā* is the special power of emission.

### Visargaśakti - विसर्गशक्ति

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission, release.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

Thus:

**विसर्गशक्ति - visargaśakti**

The power of emission.

Now the important point.

This isn't merely the statement that the sixteenth written vowel exists at the end of a list.

It is the doctrine that the full power of emission is expressed as the culminating lunar/phonemic *kalā*.

The vowel-series becomes the basis of the differentiated universe.

And the supreme power of emission is the source of the further consonantal manifestation.

### The immortal *kalā*

Abhinavagupta then quotes a short scriptural fragment:

**तामाहुरमृतां कलाम्।**

*Tām āhur amṛtāṃ kalām.*

They call her the immortal *kalā*.

Now learn the grammar.

**ताम् - tām**

Her, that feminine entity.

Accusative singular.

**आहुः - āhuḥ**

They say, call or declare.

An irregular third-person plural form of √**ah**, to say.

**अमृताम् - amṛtām**

Immortal, deathless.

Feminine accusative singular.

Agreeing with:

**कलाम् - kalām**

The *kalā*, feminine accusative singular.

Thus:

They call that *kalā* immortal.

### Why immortal?

The bracketed editor explains the association through the power's capacity to nourish the other *kalās* and its ever-present character.

That gloss is useful.

But the main prose itself identifies the sixteenth power with divine Visarga.

The theological point is that the power of emission belongs to supreme consciousness.

It isn't an independently existing mortal mechanism operating outside Śiva.

And that is why the sixteenth *kalā* is associated with *amṛta*, immortality.

### What this doesn't mean

It doesn't mean the physical Moon possesses a scientifically measurable substance called the sixteenth *kalā*.

Nor does the passage establish that lunar phases literally generate human consciousness.

We're dealing with a traditional symbolic and cosmological system.

The Moon is a sacred model of fullness, differentiation, nourishment and cyclical manifestation.

The sixteenfold pattern expresses the complete unfolding of a divine power.

And Abhinavagupta interprets that power through the phonemic system.

Now here's the really important distinction.

He explicitly says that this *kalā* must be understood in a specifically Śaiva manner.

Not simply imported unchanged from another philosophical system.

And that brings us to a beautiful sectarian-philosophical clarification.


## 17. Is this Sāṃkhya, Vedānta, or specifically Śaiva? A genuine textual variant

Now comes a very important sentence.

The Kashmir Series prints:

**न साङ्ख्येया नापि वैदान्तिकी दृक् अपि तु शैव्येव।**

The force of that reading is that the power is not to be understood through Sāṃkhya or an ordinary Vedāntic account, but through the specifically Śaiva perspective.

However, the GRETIL transcription reads the first disputed word as:

**na saṃkhyeyā**

Rather than:

**na sāṅkhyeyā**.

And this matters.

Because:

**सङ्ख्येय - saṃkhyeya**

Can mean countable, enumerable.

Whereas:

**साङ्ख्य - sāṅkhya**

Pertains to the Sāṃkhya philosophical tradition.

The forms aren't identical.

The difference could change the first part from a sectarian contrast to a statement about numerical enumeration.

And the surrounding passage has just been discussing the sixteenth power, so both the linguistic distinction and the context matter.

### The secure part of the argument

The text explicitly identifies the power as:

**शैवी - śaivī**

Śaiva, belonging to Śiva.

And associates her with:

**परमेश्वर - parameśvara**

The Supreme Lord.

And:

**विसर्गशक्ति - visargaśakti**

The power of emission.

So whatever we conclude about the disputed first word, the immediate philosophical claim is secure.

**This sixteenth power is being interpreted as the supreme Śaiva power of emission.**

She is not merely one numbered physical object among others.

And Abhinavagupta relates her to the ground of supreme bliss.

### Paramānanda - परमानन्द

**परम - parama**

Supreme.

**आनन्द - ānanda**

Bliss.

Through sandhi:

*parama + ānanda → paramānanda.*

So:

**परमानन्द - paramānanda**

Supreme bliss.

And the passage calls the emissive power a seed of that supreme blissful ground.

That's important.

The differentiated universe arises through a power that remains grounded in bliss.

It is not a foreign substance imposed upon consciousness.

### The doctrinal distinction

Now someone might ask:

"Isn't the idea of an underlying supreme reality also found in Vedānta?"

Certainly, different Vedānta traditions discuss Brahman and the relationship between ultimate reality and manifestation.

But we shouldn't simply assume that a Śaiva account of *vimarśa*, *svātantrya*, *visarga* and the phonemic Goddess is identical to every Vedāntic ontology.

Abhinavagupta's interpretation gives intrinsic self-aware freedom and expressive agency a central role.

The divine Śakti isn't an independent second substance.

Nor is she merely an irrelevant appearance added to an inert absolute.

That is a distinctive commitment of the present system.

Similarly, the thirty-six-tattva scheme is not simply the classical twenty-five-principle Sāṃkhya system copied without transformation.

The Śaiva system incorporates and reinterprets those lower principles within a more extensive hierarchy.

So the broad philosophical distinction is real.

But **the precise textual reading of the disputed word should remain marked as a variant**, not silently settled by preference.

That's the kind of care this text demands. ; \]


## 18. घनतापत्त्या - How a vowel becomes a matrix

Now we reach Kashmir Series p. 182.

And this is the moment we've been working toward for several installments.

Abhinavagupta says that the form beginning with *a* acquires a state of density and takes the form of a **yoni**, a generative matrix, without departing from its own nature.

The key word is:

**घनतापत्त्या - ghanatāpattyā**

Through acquiring density or compactness.

Now let's dismantle it.

### Ghana - घन

Dense, solid, compact, massed.

The word has ordinary meanings involving density and compactness.

But in the present metaphysical context it refers to the **condensation of a subtle power into a more determinate form**.

Not necessarily an increase in physical mass.

### Ghanatā - घनता

Density, compactness.

The abstract suffix:

**-ता - -tā**.

Thus:

*ghana + tā → ghanatā*.

### Āpatti - आपत्ति

Arrival, acquisition, becoming subject to a condition.

Related to *ā-√pad*, to come into or arrive at.

So:

**घनतापत्ति - ghanatāpatti**

The acquisition of density.

Instrumental singular:

**घनतापत्त्या - ghanatāpattyā**.

Now notice the final **-yā**.

A feminine instrumental singular formation.

The phrase tells us *by means of* or *through* this process of condensation.

### Yonirūpatā - योनिरूपता

Now:

**योनि - yoni**

Womb, source, generative matrix.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form, nature.

**-ता - -tā**

Condition.

Thus:

**योनिरूपता - yonirūpatā**

The condition of taking the form of a generative matrix.

And Abhinavagupta says the prior phonemic power acquires this condition.

But there's a crucial qualification.

**स्वरूपाप्रच्युत - svarūpāpracyuta**

Not fallen away from its own nature.

Let's dissect that.

**स्वरूप - svarūpa**

Own nature.

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**प्रच्युत - pracyuta**

Fallen away, departed, displaced.

From *pra-√cyu*, to fall, move or depart.

So:

**स्वरूपाप्रच्युत - svarūpāpracyuta**

Not departed from its intrinsic nature.

This is a beautiful philosophical statement.

The vowel-power becomes a more determinate matrix.

But it doesn't cease to be grounded in consciousness.

### The cosmic condensation

Imagine steam condensing into water.

The form changes.

But there is continuity in the underlying physical substance.

Now apply the analogy carefully.

The divine powers aren't literally steam.

And the sequence isn't a thermodynamic process.

But the image illustrates how a subtler form can become more determinate without requiring an entirely unrelated substance.

**The consonantal matrix is a denser manifestation of the vowel-power, not a foreign material introduced from outside consciousness.**

And that is how the alphabet becomes cosmological.

Vowels.

Their powers.

Their condensation.

Consonants.

The differentiated tattvas.

One continuous expressive reality.

Now the name for that transition is:

**Visarga.**

Again.

But in a much more technically specified sense.


## 19. The supreme Visarga is where the Goddess finds repose

Abhinavagupta quotes a verse that begins:

**स विसर्गो महादेवि यत्र विश्रान्तिमृच्छति।**

*Sa visargo mahādevi yatra viśrāntim ṛcchati.*

That is the Visarga, O Great Goddess, where repose is attained.

The next line identifies the same locus through the expressions **guruvaktra**, the guru's mouth, and **śakticakra**, the circle of powers.

This is an attested scriptural quotation within Abhinavagupta's prose, not a new root verse of the Parātrīśikā.

Now let's understand those terms.

### Visarga - विसर्ग

Emission, release, sending forth.

From *vi-√sṛj*, to emit.

We've studied this before.

But the present passage isn't merely describing outward emission.

It also describes the repose or resting point of the generative power.

That's the subtlety.

### Mahādevi - महादेवि

O Great Goddess.

Vocative singular feminine.

**महा - mahā**

Great.

**देवी - devī**

Goddess.

Vocative:

**देवि - devi**.

Now the final *-ī* of *devī* becomes *-i* in the vocative singular.

That's an important declensional pattern.

### Viśrānti - विश्रान्ति

Repose, rest, settling.

From *vi-√śram*.

Now:

**विश्रान्तिम् - viśrāntim**

Accusative singular.

And:

### Ṛcchati - ऋच्छति

Reaches, attains, goes toward.

From √**ṛ**, to go or attain, in this verbal formation.

Thus:

**viśrāntim ṛcchati**

Attains repose.

Now the philosophical point.

The power of emission is not simply an endless outward movement away from its source.

The manifesting power remains related to its own ground.

The very process of expression includes the possibility of repose within that ground.

And that is why the verse can speak of Visarga as a locus of rest.

### Guruvaktra - गुरुवक्त्र

The guru's mouth or face.

**गुरु - guru**

Teacher.

**वक्त्र - vaktra**

Mouth, speaking organ, face.

The compound can carry both literal and esoteric significance.

In the transmitted Tantric context, it participates in a sacred account of the locus from which instruction and mantra emerge.

But we shouldn't automatically reduce the entire doctrine to the physical mouth of a teacher.

The expression has ritual and symbolic significance.

### Śakticakra - शक्तिचक्र

The circle or organized totality of divine powers.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

**चक्र - cakra**

Wheel, circle, system.

Thus:

**śakticakra**

The wheel of Śaktis.

Now this is wonderfully appropriate.

We've just been studying an entire series of divine powers.

The vowels.

Their condensed consonantal forms.

The thirty-six tattvas.

All are expressions of the same supreme power.

So the *śakticakra* is the organized totality of those manifestations.

The guru's mouth and the circle of powers are brought together in the quoted verse to express the sacred locus of emission and repose.

### Why the guru appears here

Remember the original purpose of the Tantra.

The transmission of a supreme teaching.

Bhairava instructing the Goddess.

The Goddess asking the question.

The power of speech manifesting through divine self-revelation.

The relationship between the guru and the disciple is an expression of the same conscious power.

Now the scripture uses *guruvaktra* to connect the metaphysical emission of divine speech with the locus of spiritual instruction.

This doesn't make an ordinary human guru metaphysically independent of Śiva.

The opposite.

The teaching's power is grounded in the divine Śakti.

And the transmission is one mode of that manifestation.

Now we've reached the most concrete part of the passage.

**Which vowel becomes which consonantal group?**

The answer is remarkably systematic.

And after fourteen installments of metaphysical abstraction, we're finally going to move our actual mouths.


## 20. The vowels condense into the consonantal classes

Here is Abhinavagupta's mapping, in the order of the main Sanskrit prose.

VOWEL-POWERS AND THEIR CONSONANTAL MATRICES

| **अ**<br><br>a | **क ख ग घ ङ**<br><br>ka kha ga gha ṅa | Velar / throat-region |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **इ**<br><br>i | **च छ ज झ ञ**<br><br>ca cha ja jha ña | Palatal |
| **उ**<br><br>u | **प फ ब भ म**<br><br>pa pha ba bha ma | Labial |
| **ऋ**<br><br>ṛ | **ट ठ ड ढ ण**<br><br>ṭa ṭha ḍa ḍha ṇa | Retroflex / cerebral |
| **ऌ**<br><br>ḷ | **त थ द ध न**<br><br>ta tha da dha na | Dental |

Now this is beautiful.

Each of the five principal vowel classes is associated with a corresponding consonantal class.

The explanation is based upon traditional Sanskrit **places of articulation**.

And that's what makes the mapping more than an arbitrary symbolic arrangement.

### The first: A and the velars

The Sanskrit says:

**अकारस्यैव घनता कवर्गः - akārasyaiva ghanatā kavargaḥ.**

The *ka*-class is the condensed form of *a*.

And the reason supplied is:

**कण्ठ्यत्वात् - kaṇṭhyatvāt**

Because of its throat-region articulation.

Now ordinary Sanskrit phonetics traditionally associates *a* and the velar group with the relevant region of articulation.

The Tantric interpretation takes that classification and gives it a generative meaning.

The vowel-power becomes the consonantal matrix.

### The second: I and the palatals

The *ca*-class arises through the condensed form of *i*.

The reason:

**तालव्यत्वात् - tālavyatvāt**

Because of palatal articulation.

Now learn:

**तालु - tālu**

Palate.

**तालव्य - tālavya**

Palatal.

**तालव्यत्व - tālavyatva**

The condition of being palatal.

Ablative singular:

**तालव्यत्वात् - tālavyatvāt**

Because of palatality.

That's the grammar of the causal explanation.

### The third: U and the labials

The *pa*-class corresponds to the vowel *u*.

The reason:

**औष्ठ्यत्वात् - auṣṭhyatvāt**

Because of labial articulation.

Now:

**ओष्ठ - oṣṭha**

Lip.

**औष्ठ्य - auṣṭhya**

Labial, pertaining to the lips.

And:

**औष्ठ्यत्व - auṣṭhyatva**

The condition of being labial.

Ablative:

**auṣṭhyatvāt**.

Now try the sounds.

**प - pa**

**फ - pha**

**ब - ba**

**भ - bha**

**म - ma**

All use the lips.

The class has a concrete articulatory basis.

### The fourth: Ṛ and the retroflexes

The *ṭa*-class is associated with vocalic *ṛ*.

The reason:

**मूर्धन्यत्वात् - mūrdhanyatvāt**

Because of retroflex or cerebral articulation.

Now:

**मूर्धन् - mūrdhan**

Head, crown.

**मूर्धन्य - mūrdhanya**

Produced in the cerebral or retroflex articulation region.

The class is:

**ट ठ ड ढ ण**

*ṭa ṭha ḍa ḍha ṇa.*

Now notice the distinction from the dental series.

Your tongue is placed differently.

The retroflex articulation involves a retracted or curled-back tongue position.

### The fifth: Ḷ and the dentals

The *ta*-class is associated with vocalic *ḷ*.

The reason:

**दन्त्यत्वात् - dantyatvāt**

Because of dental articulation.

Now:

**दन्त - danta**

Tooth.

**दन्त्य - dantya**

Dental.

**दन्त्यत्व - dantyatva**

Dentality.

Ablative:

**दन्त्यत्वात् - dantyatvāt**.

And the consonants:

**त थ द ध न**

*ta tha da dha na.*

Now you can actually feel the difference.

The tongue approaches or contacts the teeth for the dental class.

The articulation differs from the retroflex group.

### The textual variant in the retroflex mapping

Here the witnesses contain a notable discrepancy.

The Kashmir Series clearly gives the retroflex group as:

**टवर्ग - ṭavarga**

And explains it through *mūrdhanyatva*, retroflex articulation.

The GRETIL transcription at this position has *tavarga*, paired with a spelling corresponding to *mardhanyatva*.

That combination is inconsistent with the usual phonetic classification.

The Kashmir Series reading is coherent: **ṛ → ṭavarga → mūrdhanya**.

So I'm following that reading and marking the difference rather than silently copying a likely transcription error. ; \]

### The theological point

The five vowel-powers are interpreted as generating denser consonantal forms.

The phonological classes supply the structure.

The divine powers supply the symbolic explanation.

And the result is the emergence of a more differentiated expressive universe.

But now we must not forget the other consonants.

The semivowels.

The sibilants.

And their relationships with the five classes.

Abhinavagupta proceeds to those next.


## 21. य श, र ष, ल स - The semivowels and sibilants enter the system

Now Abhinavagupta continues immediately.

The five consonantal classes have been established.

But the Sanskrit alphabet contains more than those five classes.

There are the semivowels:

**य र ल व**

*ya ra la va.*

And the sibilants:

**श ष स**

*śa ṣa sa.*

Plus **ह - ha**, which has a special position in the broader phonemic system.

Now the passage connects the semivowels and sibilants with the corresponding consonantal classes.

In particular:

**य - ya** and **श - śa** belong with the palatal group.

**र - ra** and **ष - ṣa** with the retroflex group.

**ल - la** and **स - sa** with the dental group.

And **व - va** is described in relation to the dental and labial groups.

Now this isn't simply random.

The correspondences are based upon the traditional analysis of Sanskrit articulation.

Let's learn them.

### Ya - य

A palatal approximant or semivowel.

In the traditional Sanskrit phonetic scheme, it belongs to the palatal region.

Compare:

**इ - i**

And:

**य - ya**.

The vowel and semivowel have a recognizable phonetic relationship.

And Sanskrit sandhi makes that relationship visible:

**इ + अ → य**

More precisely, the *i* vowel develops a *y* glide before the following vowel, as in the ordinary *iko yaṇ aci* pattern.

Now that's useful.

Because the Tantric interpretation can connect the vowel-power with a consonantal expression without inventing a completely arbitrary relationship.

The phonology already recognizes the connection.

### Śa - श

The palatal sibilant.

Pronounced with the tongue in a different position from the dental **स** or retroflex **ष**.

Now compare:

**शिव - śiva**

Śiva.

And:

**सर्व - sarva**

All.

Different sibilants.

Not interchangeable written symbols.

### Ra - र

The rhotic consonant.

Associated with the retroflex/cerebral group in the traditional articulatory classification being used.

Remember:

**ऋ - ṛ**

The vocalic rhotic.

And:

**र - ra**

The consonant.

The earlier phonemic exposition explicitly connected *ṛ* with the *repha* sound.

Now the consonantal mapping returns to that relationship.

### Ṣa - ष

The retroflex sibilant.

Now this is one of the most distinctive Sanskrit consonants for learners.

Don't confuse:

**श - śa**

Palatal.

**ष - ṣa**

Retroflex.

**स - sa**

Dental.

These are different phonemes.

And the Tantric symbolism preserves their conventional articulation as part of the mapping.

### La - ल

The lateral semivowel.

Associated here with the dental group.

And:

**ऌ - ḷ**

The vocalic lateral.

Again, there's a recognizable relation between the vocalic and consonantal members.

### Sa - स

The dental sibilant.

Now:

**ल - la** and **स - sa**

Are both associated with the dental class.

And finally:

### Va - व

The labial or labiodental-type approximant in modern pronunciation descriptions, with varying realization across Sanskrit recitational traditions.

In the traditional Sanskrit system, **व - va** participates in the relation between the dental and labial categories.

Abhinavagupta explicitly associates it with both.

Now the important point.

He is not saying that the semivowels and sibilants are ordinary members of the five stop-consonant rows.

They're distinguishable sound classes.

But their phonetic relationships permit them to be understood through the same broader generative arrangement.

And the Tantric interpretation treats them as condensed expressions of the divine vowel-powers.

### The full relationship

| **Palatal** | च छ ज झ ञ | य, श |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **Retroflex** | ट ठ ड ढ ण | र, ष |
| **Dental** | त थ द ध न | ल, स |
| **Labial / dental relation** | प फ ब भ म | व |

These are the traditional articulatory associations invoked by Abhinavagupta, not a claim that all Sanskrit consonants have identical articulatory positions.

Now we've explained the principal consonantal classes and the related semivowels and sibilants.

But why does the commentator spend so much time on the place of articulation?

Because he is establishing a **continuity between the vowel-powers and their denser consonantal expressions**.

The phonetic relationship is meaningful.

And the Tantric interpretation says the divine powers themselves can be understood through that relationship.

Now here's where everything comes together.

The vowel doesn't simply vanish when the consonant becomes manifest.

Its power remains intrinsic to the differentiated form.

Just as Anuttara does not disappear when the universe appears.

The same metaphysical structure is recurring at every level.

**Supreme consciousness → divine power → phonemic differentiation → manifested tattvas.**

And now the text makes the connection to *kriyāśakti* explicit.


## 22. Even the formless power is operative - Kriyāśakti does not require gross matter

Now Abhinavagupta states that even what is considered formless, or as mere consciousness, is not independent of the power of action.

The important expression is:

**अमूर्तस्यापि चिन्मात्रस्यापि क्रियाशक्तिरूपतैव।**

*Amūrtasyāpi cinmātrasyāpi kriyāśaktirūpataiva.*

Even of what is formless, even of consciousness alone, there is the character of *kriyāśakti*.

Now the full sentence also includes **abodhasya**, a term involving absence of awareness or knowledge. That must be interpreted cautiously within the surrounding Śaiva hierarchy; the bracketed commentary associates such conditions with loss of sovereign freedom rather than identifying supreme consciousness itself as absolutely unconscious.

The secure point is that **operative divine power is not restricted to an already gross physical form**.

### Amūrta - अमूर्त

Formless, not embodied in a gross material form.

From:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**मूर्त - mūrta**

Formed, embodied, solidified.

Thus:

**अमूर्त - amūrta**

Formless.

And:

**अमूर्तस्य - amūrtasya**

Genitive singular.

Now:

### Cinmātra - चिन्मात्र

Consciousness alone.

**चित् - cit**

Consciousness.

**मात्र - mātra**

Only, mere.

So:

**चिन्मात्र - cinmātra**

Nothing but consciousness.

Genitive singular:

**चिन्मात्रस्य - cinmātrasya**.

Now:

### Kriyāśaktirūpatā - क्रियाशक्तिरूपता

The condition of having the form of the power of action.

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form, nature.

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract condition.

Thus:

**kriyāśaktirūpatā**

The condition of being characterized by the power of action.

Now here's the philosophical distinction.

If action required gross physical form as an independently existing prerequisite, the supreme consciousness could not be the ultimate ground of agency.

It would depend upon something outside itself.

But Somānanda and Abhinavagupta have insisted that the power of agency belongs intrinsically to consciousness.

Thus the manifestation of consonants and tattvas can be understood as the **densification of an already operative freedom**.

Not the emergence of freedom from an inert substance.

### Why formlessness isn't equivalent to incapacity

Imagine someone claiming:

"Anything without a gross physical body cannot possess power."

Now, that may reflect a particular materialist metaphysical position.

But it isn't the position of Trika.

The Śaiva argument distinguishes the gross effects of action from the underlying capacity for manifestation.

The gross form is one expression of power.

It isn't the ultimate source of power.

And that's why Abhinavagupta connects the formless ground with *kriyāśakti*.

The consonants express the powers in a denser form.

But the powers are not created from nothing when the consonants appear.

The same conscious freedom is present.

And now the final arithmetic arrives.

Thirty-six.

The entire tattva-system.


## 23. Five times six plus six - The thirty-six tattvas emerge

Here is the numerical statement at the end of the present passage.

**पञ्च प्रसृताः षड्गुणितास्त्रिंशत्। षड्भिः सह षट्त्रिंशद्भवन्ति।**

*Pañca prasṛtāḥ ṣaḍguṇitās triṃśat. Ṣaḍbhiḥ saha ṣaṭtriṃśad bhavanti.*

Five, expanded sixfold, make thirty; together with six, they become thirty-six.

And now the entire phonemic cosmology converges upon the thirty-six-tattva system. ; \]

Let's learn the number-words.

### Pañca - पञ्च

Five.

### Ṣaṭ - षट्

Six.

Now notice the final consonant.

**ट् - ṭ**

The retroflex stop.

### Guṇita - गुणित

Multiplied, compounded, furnished with.

Related to:

**गुण - guṇa**

Quality, constituent, strand.

And in mathematical language, multiplication or repeated combination.

The participial form:

**गुणित - guṇita**

Multiplied.

So:

**षड्गुणित - ṣaḍguṇita**

Multiplied by six.

Why *ṣaḍ-* rather than *ṣaṭ-*?

Because the final consonant undergoes voicing before the following voiced *g*.

Thus:

**षट् + गुणित → षड्गुणित**

*ṣaṭ + guṇita → ṣaḍguṇita.*

An excellent sandhi example.

### Triṃśat - त्रिंशत्

Thirty.

### Ṣaḍbhiḥ - षड्भिः

With six.

Instrumental plural of the numeral stem.

Notice the ending:

**-भिः - -bhiḥ**.

And the voiced final *ḍ* before it.

### Ṣaṭtriṃśat - षट्त्रिंशत्

Thirty-six.

The compound expresses six and thirty.

Now the arithmetic:

$$
5\times 6 + 6 = 36
$$

Simple.

But the interpretive structure isn't quite so simple.

### Don't confuse thirty-six tattvas with thirty-six consonants

This is an important warning.

The standard Sanskrit consonantal inventory, depending on the treatment of *kṣa*, special signs and phonemic conventions, is not simply identical with a mechanically counted list of thirty-six independent consonant phonemes.

So the statement:

"Five times six, plus six, equals thirty-six"

should **not** be interpreted as a proof that the Sanskrit alphabet contains exactly thirty-six ordinary consonants arranged in six equal phonetic rows.

That isn't what the preceding articulatory mapping shows.

The text is relating a fivefold expansion through six powers to the **thirty-six tattvas** of Śaiva cosmology.

The numerological correspondence is part of the Tantric explanatory system.

It does not replace ordinary linguistic classification.

### The thirty-six tattvas

Now remember the broad structure.

The pure principles.

The limiting principles.

The individual subject.

The cognitive and operative faculties.

The subtle sensory qualities.

The gross elements.

The full differentiated manifestation.

And the entire system is interpreted as arising through the expressive freedom of Śiva.

Now that is the climax.

We began with one supreme conscious reality.

We distinguished intrinsic powers.

Those powers became expressed through the sacred vowels.

The vowels condensed into consonantal matrices.

And the consonantal structure became the expression of the differentiated tattva-system.

**From the first vowel to the thirty-six principles of manifestation.**

That is the overarching movement of the passage.

### The philosophical payoff

The thirty-six principles are not separate substances competing for ultimate reality.

They are distinguishable manifestations.

And the divine freedom underlying them is not produced by their enumeration.

The numbers articulate the manifestation.

They don't divide the ultimate conscious ground.

Which brings us right back to the statement from Somānanda at the beginning:

**One principle alone is ultimate; numerical plurality does not establish a separate Absolute.**

Now the whole argument is complete.

The three powers are inseparable.

Their action is expressed through *aunmukhya* and differentiated manifestation.

The universe is emitted through Visarga.

The vowel-powers condense into consonants.

And the thirty-six tattvas become intelligible as the organized manifestation of one consciousness.

That's the natural textual boundary.

Because the next sentence introduces a new image.

**Śiva's seed becomes a flower.**

And suddenly the entire system is going to be interpreted through a **triangular generative matrix**.

The three corners are the knower, knowing and known.

Which is precisely the philosophical structure we've been studying since the pot appeared.

But now it becomes ritual geometry.

And that's where Part XVI begins.


## 24. The crucial Sanskrit distinction - Wanting to know is already a kind of doing

Before we leave Somānanda, there is one final grammatical point worth retaining from *Śivadṛṣṭi* 1.19.

The verse says, in substance:

**यत इच्छति तज्ज्ञातुं कर्तुं वा**

*Yata icchati taj jñātuṃ kartuṃ vā.*

What he wills to know or to do…

Now notice the three verbal concepts.

### Icchati - इच्छति

He wills, wishes, desires.

From √**इष् - iṣ**, to desire, through the established present formation *iccha-*.

### Jñātum - ज्ञातुम्

To know.

From √**ज्ञा - jñā**.

The infinitive ending is:

**-तुम् - -tum**.

### Kartum - कर्तुम्

To do, perform or make.

From √**कृ - kṛ**, to do.

Again, the infinitive:

**-तुम् - -tum**.

Now the philosophical significance is extraordinary.

The Lord wills **to know**.

Or wills **to act**.

But the act of willing itself is a mode of agency.

This is why Somānanda can argue that will is not merely a passive state awaiting the arrival of action from somewhere else.

**The directedness of will is already an expression of conscious power.**

Yet its more explicit manifestation as knowing or outward action remains distinguishable.

This is the whole point of *aunmukhya*.

A preliminary orientation.

Then further development.

Then a manifested act.

The distinction is meaningful.

But the power is one.

And this is why the universe can be described as manifesting through Śiva's will without implying that Śiva has become a finite craftsman dependent upon foreign materials.

### A final reading variation

The last pāda of Somānanda's opening verse at *Śivadṛṣṭi* 1.4 is transmitted with **सदा - sadā**, "always," in the Kashmir Series and GRETIL versions of the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*, whereas the independently published *Śivadṛṣṭi* gives **तदा - tadā**, "then."

That's a real difference.

Both readings support the immediate doctrinal context, but they emphasize it differently: an abiding condition versus the condition at the time being described.

The exact wording should not be silently harmonized. ; \]

### Four particularly useful writing patterns

**1. The double meaning of a dual ending**

## पूर्वापरौ भागौ

*Pūrvāparau bhāgau*

The two aspects, earlier and later. Both words are masculine dual.


**2. A genuine Sanskrit sandhi**

## षट् + गुणित → षड्गुणित

*Ṣaṭ + guṇita → ṣaḍguṇita*

The final retroflex *ṭ* becomes voiced *ḍ* before *g*.


**3. The divine emission**

## विसर्ग - विसृजति

*Visarga - visṛjati*

Emission; he emits. Both belong to the √*sṛj* family.


**4. The complete consonantal matrix**

## कवर्ग - चवर्ग - टवर्ग - तवर्ग - पवर्ग

*Kavarga - cavarga - ṭavarga - tavarga - pavarga*

The five principal stop-consonant classes. Learn them by articulation, not just memorized order.


## 26. Where we stop in the original Sanskrit

We've followed the transition from Kashmir Series p. 178 through p. 183.

We began with Somānanda's argument for the unity of the divine powers in supreme bliss.

Then the cognition of a pot demonstrated their interdependence.

The discussion of *aunmukhya* distinguished the first orientation from fully manifested action.

The authentic Somānanda examples of waves and the trembling hand clarified that distinction, although their placement in the Kashmir Series belongs to the editorial bracket.

We then returned to Abhinavagupta's own prose.

The Supreme Lord emits the universe.

The universe extends from Earth to Śakti and is expressed through the phonemic series from *ka* to *kṣa*.

The power of Visarga is identified with the immortal sixteenth *kalā*.

The earlier vowel-powers condense into the five consonantal matrices according to their traditional places of articulation.

And the sixfold expansion of five powers, with six added, yields the thirty-six-tattva structure.

**That's the natural stopping point.**

Because the next sentence begins:

**तदेवं शिवबीजमेव स्वातन्त्र्यात्...**

*Tad evaṃ śivabījam eva svātantryāt...*

Thus the very seed of Śiva, through its freedom...

And the text moves into a new image.

The seed condenses into a flower.

The flower becomes a generative matrix.

And the matrix is described through a triangle consisting of:

**ग्राहक - grāhaka**

The apprehender.

**ग्रहण - grahaṇa**

Apprehension.

**ग्राह्य - grāhya**

What is apprehended.

Now that is an extraordinary development.

Because the previous installments investigated the relationship between knower, knowing and known at a philosophical level.

And Abhinavagupta is about to express that exact relationship through the sacred **triangle of the Goddess**.

Then come the lunar, solar and fiery corners; the relations among the three channels *iḍā*, *piṅgalā* and *suṣumnā*; the divine generative matrix; and the corresponding ritual symbolism.

That is Part XVI.


## 27. The final insight - Even while Śiva rests, the universe is possible

Somānanda's great contribution here is to insist that divine freedom cannot be reduced to overt action.

The absence of a visibly manifested universe doesn't mean Śiva has lost the power of will.

The absence of a distinct object doesn't mean the supreme consciousness has lost the power of knowledge.

And the absence of gross physical activity doesn't mean divine agency has become nonexistent.

The powers can remain in subtle unity.

And their outward distinction belongs to manifestation.

That's why the first orientation-*aunmukhya*-matters so much.

It expresses the initial emergence of a differentiated activity without requiring that the underlying consciousness become a foreign substance.

The wave is not the destruction of water.

The closing hand doesn't become a fundamentally independent reality unrelated to its earlier condition.

And the manifestation of the universe does not require Śiva to abandon his own nature.

Then Abhinavagupta extends exactly the same principle to sacred speech.

The vowel-powers condense into consonants.

The consonants articulate the tattvas.

The tattvas constitute the differentiated universe.

But their ground remains one conscious freedom.

**The letters become distinguishable without the Goddess becoming divided into independently existing substances.**

That's the philosophical achievement of this passage.

And now, after fifteen installments, we've moved from the first vowel all the way to the differentiated consonantal universe.

The entire alphabet is becoming the body of Śakti.

And the next great symbol is waiting.

A triangle.

A flower.

A generative matrix.

The knower, knowing and known.

One consciousness expressing itself through all three.

Abhinavagupta has somehow transformed a lesson in Sanskrit articulation into a metaphysics of existence.

And he's only just getting warmed up.
