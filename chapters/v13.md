# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XIII - The Pot, the Knower, and the Birth of Action

*Abhinavagupta tells everyone to stop wandering around in philosophical confusion, makes an ordinary clay pot reveal the structure of consciousness, explains why awareness cannot be manufactured by the things it knows, and finally introduces the four strangest vowels in Sanskrit, which apparently possess the generative capacity of roasted seeds.*

Commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series, pp. 170-174


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

We've reached the sixth vowel.

Six.

Thirteen installments into a reading of Abhinavagupta's commentary, and the man has got as far as **ऊ - ū**.

Ordinarily, a Sanskrit teacher could get through the entire alphabet in a few lessons.

Abhinavagupta has instead explained the origin of manifestation, the relationship between consciousness and time, the metaphysics of will, the nature of divine bliss, the Moon, the Sun, three Goddesses, five classes of consonants, several competing alphabetic systems, the nature of ritual purification, and whether somebody can speak, think and perceive simultaneously.

And now he's preparing to explain **क्रियाशक्ति - kriyāśakti**, the power of action.

But there's an objection.

Of course there's an objection.

There is always a bloody objection.

We learned that *icchāśakti*, the power of will, unfolds into more explicit forms.

And that *jñānaśakti*, the power of knowledge, likewise manifests through differentiated stages.

The Moon can symbolize fullness and repose.

The Sun can symbolize illumination, expansion and reabsorption.

And those relations can even be considered from opposite directions.

So the philosopher in the back row raises his hand.

"Excuse me, Ācārya."

"Yes?"

"If will can expand and contract, doesn't that require a further power governing its expansion?"

"And if that power expands, doesn't it require another?"

"And another?"

"And another?"

The man has discovered recursion.

And he's about to make it everybody's problem.

Abhinavagupta's answer is spectacular.

He effectively says:

**Stop wandering around in the bewilderment of external appearances. Enter the subtle path of reflexive awareness.**

Then he picks up the most ordinary object imaginable.

A pot.

And begins dismantling the relationship between the universe and consciousness.

We'll follow the Sanskrit continuously from the beginning of p. 170, through the pot example, the six divine vowel-powers, the transition into *kriyāśakti*, and the four vocalic vowels **ऋ ॠ ऌ ॡ**. The natural stopping point is p. 174, just before Abhinavagupta begins the next group of combined vowel-powers. I've checked the Sanskrit against both the Kashmir Series transcription and Somadeva Vasudeva's GRETIL transcription; I'll distinguish textual differences and bracketed editorial additions where they affect the interpretation. ; ; \]


## 1. उपसंहरत - Stop the bloody wandering

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · KASHMIR SERIES P. 170

**उपसंहरत बाह्यविभ्रमभ्रमभ्रमणं तावत्।**

**अनुप्रविशत सूक्ष्मां विमर्शपदवीम्।**

*Upasaṃharata bāhyavibhramabhramabhramaṇaṃ tāvat.*

*Anupraviśata sūkṣmāṃ vimarśapadavīm.*

A close rendering:

For now, withdraw from wandering through the bewilderment of outward appearances. Enter the subtle path of reflexive awareness.

The Kashmir Series transcription writes **बाह्यबिभ्रम** in the first phrase, while GRETIL has **bāhyavibhrama**. I've given the latter's normalized spelling in Devanāgarī. The substantive instruction is the same.

Now that is how you deal with a metaphysical objection.

No more endless hypothetical subdivisions.

No more inventing increasingly tiny agencies to explain other agencies.

Stop.

And examine the reality through which all those distinctions are being apprehended.

But before we turn this into a generic instruction to meditate, let's learn the Sanskrit properly.

### Upasaṃharata - उपसंहरत

**उपसंहरत - upasaṃharata**

Withdraw! Gather in! Bring to a close!

The verb belongs to:

**उप - upa**

Toward, near, in relation to.

**सम् - sam**

Together, completely.

**√हृ - √hṛ**

To take, carry, remove or gather.

The complete verbal formation is:

**उपसंहरति - upasaṃharati**

He or she withdraws or gathers together.

Now the imperative:

**उपसंहरत - upasaṃharata**

Second-person plural.

This is important.

It's not the ordinary singular imperative addressed to one person.

The plural form can address a group of listeners.

The master is telling everybody:

"Withdraw your attention from this proliferation."

An extremely useful verb, incidentally, because its nominal relative:

**संहार - saṃhāra**

Means gathering in, reabsorption, withdrawal.

And you've already encountered *saṃhāra* as one of the great cosmic activities of Śiva.

The same verbal family appears in both ordinary language and the technical theology.

### Bāhya - बाह्य

External, outward.

Related to:

**बहिस् - bahis**

Outside.

Now Abhinavagupta isn't saying external objects must literally be destroyed.

He's criticizing the tendency to remain wholly occupied with the differentiated appearances while neglecting the awareness through which those appearances are known.

### Vibhrama - विभ्रम

Confusion, bewilderment, wandering.

From:

**वि - vi**

Apart, variously, distinctly.

And √**भ्रम् - bhram**

To wander, turn, become confused.

Now the wonderfully alliterative sequence:

**विभ्रम - vibhrama**

Bewilderment.

**भ्रम - bhrama**

Wandering or confusion.

**भ्रमण - bhramaṇa**

The act of wandering.

And the compound:

**बाह्यविभ्रमभ्रमभ्रमणम्**

A kind of bewildered wandering within the bewilderment of the outward field.

The Sanskrit is deliberately dense.

And its repeated *bhram* sounds practically make you feel the mind spinning around.

The philosopher has become trapped in a conceptual washing machine.

### Tāvat - तावत्

For now, to that extent, meanwhile.

This is actually important.

Abhinavagupta is not announcing a permanent ban on philosophical reasoning.

He has been doing serious philosophical reasoning for hundreds of pages.

The instruction is more specific.

For the present inquiry, stop multiplying external distinctions and turn toward the fundamental conscious reality.

### Anupraviśata - अनुप्रविशत

Enter!

From:

**अनु - anu**

Along, into, following.

**प्र - pra**

Forward, forth.

**√विश् - √viś**

To enter.

The finite verb:

**अनुप्रविशति - anupraviśati**

He or she enters.

The imperative:

**अनुप्रविशत - anupraviśata**

Enter, second-person plural.

Now compare the two verbs.

**उपसंहरत - upasaṃharata**

Withdraw.

**अनुप्रविशत - anupraviśata**

Enter.

It's a perfectly structured instruction.

Withdraw from one orientation.

Enter another.

But enter what?

### Sūkṣmāṃ vimarśapadavīm - सूक्ष्मां विमर्शपदवीम्

**सूक्ष्म - sūkṣma**

Subtle, fine, delicate.

Feminine accusative singular:

**सूक्ष्माम् - sūkṣmām**.

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive awareness, self-apprehension, examination.

**पदवी - padavī**

Path, way, course.

Feminine accusative singular:

**पदवीम् - padavīm**.

Thus:

**सूक्ष्मां विमर्शपदवीम् - sūkṣmāṃ vimarśapadavīm**

The subtle path of reflexive awareness.

Now notice the grammar.

Both *sūkṣmām* and *padavīm* are feminine accusative singular.

The adjective agrees with its noun.

And *vimarśa* specifies the kind of path.

This isn't a mysterious collection of unrelated Sanskrit syllables.

It's an intelligible grammatical instruction.

**Enter the subtle path of self-apprehending consciousness.**

### Why this is more precise than “stop thinking”

Abhinavagupta is not simply saying:

"Thoughts bad. No thoughts good."

That would be a ridiculous simplification.

The distinction is between examining the endless proliferation of differentiated contents and examining the underlying reality through which differentiation is manifest.

You can intellectually contemplate the relationship between thoughts.

You can analyse memory.

You can study time.

You can construct mathematical models of cognition.

All useful activities.

But the present question is different.

**What is the nature of the awareness in which these activities appear?**

That's the path of *vimarśa*.

And now he demonstrates it.

With a pot.


## 2. घट - Ghaṭa: The Absolute goes shopping for kitchenware

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 170

**यावद्धि घटादावपि विज्ञानं जायते...**

*Yāvad dhi ghaṭādāv api vijñānaṃ jāyate...*

Whenever cognition arises even concerning a pot or similar object…

That's where he begins.

A pot.

After six divine vowels, lunar powers and the entire apparatus of Trika metaphysics.

An ordinary clay pot.

Beautiful.

Now why a pot?

Because **घट - ghaṭa** is a standard example in Indian epistemology.

Different philosophical schools use pots, cloths, jars, trees and other ordinary objects to analyse knowing, existence, causation and language.

A pot is recognizable.

It can be perceived.

Named.

Remembered.

Handled.

And distinguished from other things.

So the example is philosophically convenient.

### Ghaṭa - घट

Pot, jar.

The consonant is:

**घ - gha**

A voiced aspirated velar.

Then:

**ट - ṭa**

A retroflex unaspirated stop.

Notice the distinction from:

**ठ - ṭha**

The aspirated retroflex.

The word is:

**घट - ghaṭa**

Not *ghaṭha*.

Now in the actual phrase:

**घटादौ - ghaṭādau**

In a pot and similar objects.

Let's examine its formation.

**घट - ghaṭa**

Pot.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning, and so forth.

Compound:

**घटादि - ghaṭādi**

A pot and similar things.

Locative singular:

**घटादौ - ghaṭādau**.

Now here's a useful point.

The ending **-au** can mark the locative singular of an *-i* stem.

We've already seen:

**शक्तिः → शक्तौ**

*śaktiḥ → śaktau*.

And:

**आदिः → आदौ**

*ādiḥ → ādau*.

Same broad declensional pattern, though the nouns have different genders.

### Vijñānam - विज्ञानम्

Cognition, particularized knowing.

From:

**वि - vi**

Differentiating or specially.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

And:

**जायते - jāyate**

Arises, is born, comes into existence.

Third-person singular present middle voice of √**jan**, to be born or arise.

So the ordinary meaning is clear.

Cognition of a pot arises.

Now comes the real problem.

**What exactly arises when you know a pot?**

Does a completely unconscious object somehow manufacture consciousness?

Does consciousness first exist as a blank substance and then become a second substance called pot-consciousness?

Is there a separate cognition for the pot and yet another cognition responsible for knowing that cognition?

Or is the pot's appearance a differentiation of an already self-revealing field of awareness?

Abhinavagupta's answer is the last.

But he doesn't merely assert it.

He describes precisely how the known object and knowing appear together.


## 3. The cognition is coloured by the object, but illuminates itself

Now we arrive at the main philosophical claim.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 170

**ज्ञेयघटाद्यंशकर्बुरीकृतस्वयम्प्रथं ज्ञानं प्रथत एव।**

*Jñeyaghaṭādyaṃśakarburīkṛtasvayaṃprathaṃ jñānaṃ prathata eva.*

Knowledge, self-manifesting, appears variegated by the knowable aspect-the pot and so forth.

Now this is a spectacular sentence.

The entire argument is compressed into one enormous compound.

And if you understand it properly, you will understand something fundamental about Pratyabhijñā.

Let's dissect it.

### Jñeya - ज्ञेय

Knowable.

What is to be known.

From √**jñā**, to know, through a gerundive formation.

Remember the initial conjunct:

**ज्ञ = ज् + ञ**

The *jñ* sound.

And:

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

These are related but distinct forms.

One designates the knowable side.

The other the knowing side.

### Ghaṭādi - घटादि

Pot and similar objects.

We already know this compound.

### Aṃśa - अंश

Part, portion, aspect.

So:

**ज्ञेयघटाद्यंश - jñeyaghaṭādyaṃśa**

The knowable component, such as the pot.

Now comes the beautiful word.

### Karburī-kṛta - कर्बुरीकृत

Made variegated.

Coloured or marked by a differentiated quality.

**कर्बुर - karbura**

Variegated, spotted, mottled, multicoloured.

And:

**कृत - kṛta**

Made, done.

From √**kṛ**, to do or make.

Thus:

**कर्बुरीकृत - karburīkṛta**

Made variegated.

Now this is a perfect metaphor.

Imagine a clear surface through which different colours can appear.

Blue.

Red.

Green.

The appearance takes on a particular quality.

But the capacity for manifestation isn't created anew by each colour.

Abhinavagupta describes knowledge as variegated by the object-aspect.

The cognition appears as pot-cognition.

Not tree-cognition.

Not bell-cognition.

But here's the crucial distinction.

**Its self-manifesting nature is not produced by the pot.**

### Svayampratha - स्वयम्प्रथ

Self-manifesting, appearing of itself.

Now break this down.

**स्वयम् - svayam**

By oneself, of oneself.

And a form belonging to √**prath**, to spread, become manifest, be known.

The verb:

**प्रथते - prathate**

Appears, becomes manifest.

And the expression:

**स्वयम्प्रथ - svayampratha**

Self-appearing, self-manifesting.

Now put the whole thing together.

**ज्ञेयघटाद्यंशकर्बुरीकृतस्वयम्प्रथमज्ञानम्**

Knowledge that manifests of itself while appearing variegated through the knowable pot-aspect.

A marvellous Sanskrit compound.

But the philosophy matters more than the impressive length.

### The pot does not produce the luminosity of awareness

Imagine looking at a pot.

You see its shape.

Perhaps it's round.

Made of clay.

Dark brown.

You identify it as a pot.

Now the particular cognition has a definite content.

It's not the same as your cognition of a bird.

But what makes the cognition manifest as experience?

Within Abhinavagupta's system, the answer is not that the pot supplies the basic reality of awareness.

The pot is the known content.

The power of knowing is self-revealing consciousness.

Thus the pot can differentiate the appearance of cognition without being the ultimate source of the cognition's luminosity.

**The known object determines the form of the manifestation, not the existence of consciousness as such.**

This distinction is essential.

And it's not simply a theory about sense-organ mechanisms.

It's a metaphysical account of what knowing fundamentally is.

### The cosmic kitchenware problem

Imagine a pot sitting on a shelf.

Perfectly ordinary.

Then a philosopher announces:

"This pot has generated consciousness."

Interesting.

How?

"Because I am conscious of it."

But that only establishes that the pot is an object of consciousness.

It doesn't establish that the pot manufactures the consciousness through which it is known.

Now someone else replies:

"Then the pot must be unreal."

Not necessarily.

Abhinavagupta does not need to deny the manifestation of the pot.

He needs to distinguish the pot's appearance from the self-revealing power that makes appearance possible.

The object is manifested.

Its particularity is intelligible.

But its presentation does not establish a second independent ultimate reality outside consciousness.

And now the argument becomes subtler.

Because even the first emergence of the pot-as something not yet fully articulated-must itself be apprehensible.


## 4. सूक्ष्मोल्लास - The very first glimmer of the pot

The next statement concerns:

**घटादिप्रथमसूक्ष्मोल्लासः**

*Ghaṭādiprathamasūkṣmollāsaḥ.*

The first subtle emergence of the pot and similar objects.

Now let's learn it.

### Prathama - प्रथम

First, initial.

### Sūkṣma - सूक्ष्म

Subtle, fine.

### Ullāsa - उल्लास

Opening, blossoming, delight, emergence, shining forth.

Related to *ud-* and √**las**, to shine, play or manifest.

So:

**सूक्ष्मोल्लास - sūkṣmollāsa**

Subtle emergence.

And:

**प्रथमसूक्ष्मोल्लास - prathamasūkṣmollāsa**

The first subtle emergence.

Now, the idea is fascinating.

Before an object appears in fully articulated cognition, there can be a subtle emergence of the object-content.

That initial manifestation is also knowable.

It is not entirely outside awareness.

And this leads Abhinavagupta to reject the idea that object-appearance arises from an independent source completely foreign to consciousness.

### The first emergence need not be a fully formed object

Imagine looking toward a dimly lit shelf.

At first there's a shape.

Then a rounded outline.

Then you recognize the pot.

The experience seems to become progressively more determinate.

Now Abhinavagupta is not presenting an empirical theory of visual processing.

And his *sūkṣmollāsa* should not simply be equated with a modern account of early sensory processing.

But the analogy helps.

**An initially subtle appearance can become a determinate cognition without requiring awareness to be manufactured at the final stage.**

The pot becomes explicit.

The object-aspect becomes more clearly differentiated.

But the conscious manifestation is already relevant to the earliest emergence.

### The word prathate

**प्रथते - prathate**

Appears, becomes manifest.

And:

**प्रथमान - prathamāna**

Appearing, becoming manifest.

Both relate to √*prath*.

Now this root matters because it allows Abhinavagupta to discuss manifestation without presupposing that an object has independently emerged somewhere else and then been transported into awareness.

The object *appears*.

The appearance is known.

And the known appearance is not independent of the self-manifesting knowledge in which it occurs.

### A crucial philosophical caution

This argument is not merely:

"Everything I experience is mental, therefore there is no physical world."

That would be too crude.

Trika's metaphysical position concerns the ultimate non-independence of manifestation from universal consciousness.

It is not simply the assertion that the individual psychological mind invents its surroundings.

The pot is not merely an arbitrary fantasy of one person's ego.

Rather, the reality of manifestation itself is understood through the expressive consciousness of Bhairava.

That distinction is enormously important.

Otherwise we replace Kashmir Śaivism with an embarrassingly simplistic form of individual idealism.

Abhinavagupta would be furious.

And now he makes the decisive move.

**The initial emerging object is an expression of consciousness's sovereign freedom.**

Not merely a passive presentation imposed upon it.

And that means we have returned to *īśana*.

The fourth vowel-power.


## 5. भावोज्जिगमिषा - Consciousness's freedom to let an object emerge

Now comes the key phrase:

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 170

**संविद एव स्वातन्त्र्यं भावोज्जिगमिषात्मकमीशनम्।**

*Saṃvida eva svātantryaṃ bhāvojjigamiṣātmakam īśanam.*

The sovereign freedom of consciousness itself, characterized by the impulse for an entity to emerge, is *īśana*, governing power.

And Abhinavagupta adds:

**स्वसंवित्प्रमाणलब्धमेव।**

*Svasaṃvitpramāṇalabdham eva.*

It is established through one's own awareness as the means of knowledge. ; \]

Now this is extraordinary.

Because *īśana* isn't simply a title for a deity ruling somewhere outside cognition.

It's being identified with the power of consciousness to manifest an emerging object.

Let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Bhāva - भाव

Being, entity, becoming, manifested condition.

From √**bhū**, to be or become.

### Ujjigamiṣā - उज्जिगमिषा

The wish or impulse to emerge.

This is a remarkable word.

It belongs to a desiderative formation involving:

**उद् - ud**

Upward, forth.

And √**gam**

To go.

The desiderative introduces the sense of wanting or being disposed to perform the action.

So:

**उज्जिगमिषा - ujjigamiṣā**

The impulse toward coming forth or emerging.

Now notice the consonant sequence.

**ज्ज = ज् + ज**

The doubled palatal consonant.

And:

**ष - ṣa**

The retroflex sibilant in the desiderative suffix.

The complete compound:

**भावोज्जिगमिषा - bhāvojjigamiṣā**

The impulse for an entity or manifested state to emerge.

How does the first vowel combination work?

**भाव + उज्जिगमिषा**

*bhāva + ujjigamiṣā*.

With:

**a + u → o**.

Thus:

**bhāvojjigamiṣā**.

Now add:

**आत्मक - ātmaka**

Having the nature of.

So:

**भावोज्जिगमिषात्मक - bhāvojjigamiṣātmaka**

Having the nature of the impulse toward manifestation.

And this characterizes:

**ईशन - īśana**

Sovereign governing power.

### This is not ordinary craving

Now here's the crucial distinction.

The word *ujjigamiṣā* sounds like desire.

And it is a desiderative formation.

But it does not follow that Abhinavagupta is describing Śiva as a psychologically incomplete being who desperately wants a pot.

"There's nothing in the universe."

"I need a pot."

"Then I shall be complete."

No.

The *icchā* or sovereign freedom of consciousness is not treated as deficiency.

The pot's emergence is an expression of the freedom to manifest.

Not the satisfaction of a pre-existing lack.

And here's the most important point.

**The impulse toward manifestation is already within consciousness.**

It is not imposed by an external independent object.

### Svasaṃvit-pramāṇa - स्वसंवित्प्रमाण

Now the phrase:

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**संविद् - saṃvid**

Consciousness, awareness.

**प्रमाण - pramāṇa**

Means of valid knowing, evidence, instrument of knowledge.

And:

**लब्ध - labdha**

Obtained, established, acquired.

From √**labh**, to obtain.

Thus:

**स्वसंवित्प्रमाणलब्ध - svasaṃvitpramāṇalabdha**

Established through one's own awareness as the means of cognition.

Now, don't misunderstand the epistemic claim.

Abhinavagupta isn't saying every subjective opinion is automatically true.

The fact that you feel convinced about something doesn't make the object of that conviction infallible.

His claim concerns the immediate self-manifestation of consciousness and its power.

The object of a cognition may be mistaken.

But the occurrence of awareness is not established through an independent external apparatus in precisely the way an ordinary object is.

And that's the Pratyabhijñā distinction.

The pot may turn out to have been misperceived.

But the appearance was still an occurrence of knowing.

### The philosophical conclusion

Now we can see the relationship between the first six vowel-powers and ordinary cognition.

Anuttara isn't a remote concept waiting at the beginning of the universe.

Ānanda isn't a second substance added to consciousness.

Icchā isn't merely ordinary craving.

Īśana isn't merely cosmic administration.

Unmeṣa isn't just physical eye movement.

And the developing knowledge-power isn't generated by an independent external object.

**These are distinguishable expressions of the self-aware freedom through which objects become manifest.**

And the ordinary pot has revealed their relationship.

Excellent.

The pot is doing considerably more metaphysical work than most professional philosophers.


## 6. The subtle pot is still an appearance within consciousness

Now the argument takes a turn that's easy to miss if we rush through the Sanskrit.

Abhinavagupta has distinguished two things:

The self-revealing nature of knowledge.

And the object-aspect through which that knowledge becomes particularized.

But he hasn't therefore established two independently existing substances.

Quite the opposite.

The pot's first subtle emergence is itself apprehended.

Its later explicit appearance is apprehended.

And the power of cognition doesn't have to be generated afresh by the addition of an independently existing pot.

This is why his formulation immediately moves toward the fullness of consciousness.

The Sanskrit describes consciousness as:

**संविद्घना परिपूर्णा**

*Saṃvidghanā paripūrṇā.*

A compact fullness of consciousness, complete in itself.

Let's learn that.

### Saṃvidghana - संविद्घन

**संविद् - saṃvid**

Consciousness, awareness.

**घन - ghana**

Dense, compact, solid, an undivided mass.

Thus:

**संविद्घन - saṃvidghana**

A homogeneous or compact fullness of awareness.

Now don't picture an actual lump of consciousness.

The term doesn't imply that awareness is a solid object with mass and density.

The metaphor concerns its **non-fragmented nature**.

It is not a pile of independently existing cognitions that somebody has glued together.

### Paripūrṇā - परिपूर्णा

Completely full.

From:

**परि - pari**

All around, completely.

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa**

Full, complete.

The final **-ā** is feminine nominative singular.

And here it agrees with the feminine *saṃvid*, consciousness.

Now why insist upon fullness?

Because otherwise we risk thinking that the object contributes a new portion of consciousness each time it becomes known.

You see a pot.

Consciousness acquires one extra unit.

You see a tree.

Another unit.

You see a gecko.

Three consciousness points.

After a particularly busy day, you're apparently a more conscious substance because you've accumulated an enormous inventory of things.

No.

Abhinavagupta rejects that sort of additive model of supreme awareness.

The particular contents distinguish the manifested cognitions.

But the self-revealing character of consciousness isn't simply the sum of those contents.

### A textual caution

The phrase surrounding this assertion contains a compound transmitted as **तद्भावानुचयरूपा - tadbhāvānucayarūpā** in the Sanskrit transcriptions. A modern interpretive translation takes the intended sense as freedom from the addition of manifested entities. Because the exact compound and its segmentation are not transparent, I'm not presenting that interpretation as an uncontested word-for-word translation.

What is secure is the surrounding argument: consciousness is *saṃvidghana*, *paripūrṇā*, and characterized by its own *svātantrya*, not by dependence upon an externally supplied object. ; \]

### The difference between an object's appearance and awareness itself

Let's make this very precise.

Suppose a pot appears in experience.

One question is:

**What is being known?**

The answer: the pot.

Another question is:

**What makes the pot's appearance an occurrence of knowing?**

Abhinavagupta's answer: self-revealing consciousness.

Now, those questions concern distinguishable aspects of the event.

But the pot's appearance doesn't require a second independently existing ultimate awareness to mediate between the pot and consciousness.

Its appearance occurs within the field of manifestation.

And the same freedom that makes consciousness self-revealing permits it to manifest determinate forms.

That's why the text identifies the impulse toward manifestation with *īśana*.

The pot isn't creating the Lord's power of knowing.

The power of knowing is manifesting as pot-cognition.

Now that is an extremely strong metaphysical claim.

It cannot be established simply by examining the clay from which a pot is made.

And it should not be confused with a scientific theory of perception.

The argument belongs to Pratyabhijñā's ontology of self-luminous consciousness.

But within that framework, the logic is coherent.

And now Abhinavagupta makes the next move.

**If consciousness is complete and sovereign, why must we also acknowledge bliss?**

Because, apparently, the pot has not yet finished its theological duties.


## 7. आनन्दोऽपि नापह्नवनीयः - You cannot simply delete bliss

Here is the crucial line:

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 170

**इत्यनन्दोऽपि नापह्नवनीयः।**

*Ity ānando 'pi nāpahnavanīyaḥ.*

Thus bliss too cannot be denied.

Now, the Sanskrit deserves proper attention.

### Ānanda - आनन्द

Bliss, delight, joy.

We've encountered this repeatedly.

But it isn't merely the pleasure of encountering a desirable object.

In the present passage, it is connected with consciousness's repose in its own freedom and fullness.

### Apahnavanīya - अपह्नवनीय

Something that may be denied, rejected or repudiated.

The word belongs to:

**अप - apa**

Away, apart.

And √**ह्नु - hnu**

To conceal, deny or disown, in the relevant verbal formation.

The full negative statement:

**न अपह्नवनीयः - na apahnavanīyaḥ**

Not to be denied.

Through sandhi, the text gives:

**नापह्नवनीयः - nāpahnavanīyaḥ**.

Now notice:

**na + a → nā**.

The vowel coalesces.

And:

**आनन्दोऽपि - ānando 'pi**

Bliss too.

That's visarga sandhi with avagraha.

Underlying:

**आनन्दः + अपि - ānandaḥ + api**.

The result is:

**आनन्दोऽपि - ānando 'pi**.

Now the philosophy.

If supreme consciousness were merely a passive capacity for receiving objects, its sovereign freedom and self-revealing fullness would remain unexplained.

Abhinavagupta insists that the self-apprehending reality possesses a positive character of delight.

Not because it has obtained something previously lacking.

But because its fullness is intrinsically self-aware and free.

### Ānandaghana - आनन्दघन

**आनन्द - ānanda**

Bliss.

**घन - ghana**

Compact mass, fullness.

So:

**आनन्दघन - ānandaghana**

An undivided fullness of bliss.

Now this does not mean somebody must be experiencing a constant pleasant emotion in order to possess consciousness.

The technical *ānanda* is not simply identical with everyday psychological happiness.

It's a description of the intrinsic self-repose or delight of universal consciousness.

### The difference between psychological pleasure and divine bliss

Imagine drinking excellent tea.

The taste is pleasurable.

Perhaps you're relaxed.

The experience has a definite object, bodily conditions and a temporal duration.

Now, that can be wonderful.

But the experience isn't therefore identical, in every respect, with the unsurpassable freedom of Bhairava.

Its pleasure is a conditioned manifestation.

The metaphysical *ānanda* being discussed is more fundamental.

It is the positive self-revelation of consciousness, not merely one pleasant sensation among others.

So we shouldn't use the existence of pleasurable experiences as a simplistic proof of the doctrine.

Nor should we assume the absence of pleasure disproves it.

The categories are different.

And now comes the important transition.

If consciousness possesses sovereign freedom and bliss, then the supreme reality cannot be merely an impersonal, inert object.

It must possess the power of agency.

And that's precisely what Abhinavagupta says.


## 8. Bhairava is the doer - But not the personal ego wearing a crown

The text identifies Bhairava as:

**अनुत्तरश्च शक्तिमान्**

*Anuttaraś ca śaktimān.*

Anuttara, possessing Śakti.

And describes him as:

**अव्यपदेश्यपरचमत्कारसारः**

*Avyapadeśyaparacamatkārasāraḥ.*

Having the ineffable supreme wonder or delight as his essence.

Finally:

**सर्वत्र कर्तृत्वेन भासत एव।**

*Sarvatra kartṛtvena bhāsata eva.*

He manifests everywhere in the capacity of agency.

That's a very powerful statement.

Let's break down its vocabulary.

### Śaktimān - शक्तिमान्

Possessing power.

The noun:

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

And a possessive formation of the *-mat/-mant* type.

**शक्तिमत् - śaktimat**

Possessing power, as a stem or adjectival form.

Masculine nominative singular:

**शक्तिमान् - śaktimān**.

Now here's an important distinction.

Śiva and Śakti can be distinguished as the possessor of power and power.

But within Trika, that distinction does not create independent ultimate substances.

The power isn't an accessory kept in a cupboard.

It belongs intrinsically to the supreme reality.

### Avyapadeśya - अव्यपदेश्य

Not adequately expressible or definable.

From:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**व्यपदेश्य - vyapadeśya**

That which can be specified, designated or described.

So:

**अव्यपदेश्य - avyapadeśya**

Beyond adequate designation.

And:

### Camatkāra - चमत्कार

Wonder, astonishment, delighted awareness.

This word is tremendously important in Abhinavagupta's aesthetics and metaphysics.

But its technical significance depends upon context.

In aesthetic theory, it can describe the extraordinary relish or wonder of aesthetic experience.

In the present metaphysical passage, it characterizes the supreme self-revelation of consciousness.

And:

**सार - sāra**

Essence, substance, core.

Thus the expression identifies Bhairava's essential character with supreme, ineffable *camatkāra*.

Now that is beautiful.

The supreme reality is not merely a passive substance containing objects.

It is self-aware, free, and intrinsically characterized by the wonder of manifestation.

### Kartṛtva - कर्तृत्व

Agency, doership.

From:

**कर्तृ - kartṛ**

Doer.

And:

**-त्व - -tva**

Abstract condition.

So:

**कर्तृत्व - kartṛtva**

The condition of being an agent.

Now the instrumental singular:

**कर्तृत्वेन - kartṛtvena**

In the capacity of agency.

And the verb:

**भासते - bhāsate**

Shines forth, appears, manifests.

From √**bhās**, to shine.

Thus:

**सर्वत्र कर्तृत्वेन भासत एव**

Bhairava manifests everywhere as the agent.

### Does that mean my ego is omnipotent?

No.

Absolutely not.

The finite ego-function, *ahaṃkāra*, is a limited manifestation.

It is not identical in its ordinary psychological capacities with the universal agency of Bhairava.

The statement concerns the **metaphysical ground of agency**.

Whatever limited activity becomes manifest, its ultimate reality is not outside the supreme consciousness.

But the differentiated agent remains subject to the practical conditions of its manifested existence.

You cannot decide to fly merely because you've memorized the word *svātantrya*.

And you cannot claim absolute authority over other people by declaring yourself Śiva.

That would be a confusion between universal subjectivity and the limited personal ego.

Abhinavagupta's system is much more precise than that.

Now comes the wonderful culmination of this first argumentative movement.

He returns to the six vowel-powers.

And explains why their apparent distinctions are not fundamentally separate.


## 9. The six vowel-powers converge into one consciousness

Here is the movement across pp. 170-171.

The six powers we've been studying are the symbolic stages beginning with:

**अ - a:** Anuttara.

**आ - ā:** Ānanda.

**इ - i:** Icchā.

**ई - ī:** Īśana.

**उ - u:** Unmeṣa.

**ऊ - ū:** the further differentiated manifestation of knowledge.

The Sanskrit identifies these as divine conscious powers that, upon examination of their own natures, converge without being independently different from consciousness.

Now the crucial phrase:

**ता एव भगवत्यः संविच्छक्तयः समापतन्त्यनन्या एव स्वयं संविदः।**

*Tā eva bhagavatyaḥ saṃvicchaktayaḥ samāpatanty ananyā eva svayaṃ saṃvidaḥ.*

Those very venerable powers of consciousness come together as not other than consciousness itself.

Let's learn the grammar.

### Bhagavatyaḥ - भगवत्यः

Venerable goddesses, divine powers.

Feminine nominative plural.

Singular:

**भगवती - bhagavatī**

The venerable Goddess.

Plural:

**भगवत्यः - bhagavatyaḥ**.

Notice the glide.

**-ī → -yaḥ** in this inflectional formation.

### Saṃvicchaktayaḥ - संविच्छक्तयः

Powers of consciousness.

Start with:

**संविद् - saṃvid**

Consciousness.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

The compound undergoes consonant adjustment at the boundary:

**saṃvid + śakti → saṃvicchakti**.

Now the feminine nominative plural:

**संविच्छक्तयः - saṃvicchaktayaḥ**.

This is a particularly beautiful form.

It refers to the plural divine powers without making them independent ultimate realities.

### Samāpatanti - समापतन्ति

They come together, converge, arrive or become united.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Together.

**आ - ā**

Toward.

**√पत् - √pat**

To fall, move or arrive, in the relevant formation.

Third-person plural present:

**समापतन्ति - samāpatanti**.

Now:

### Ananyāḥ - अनन्याः

Not other, not different.

From:

**अन् - an-**

Negative prefix.

**अन्य - anya**

Other.

So:

**अनन्य - ananya**

Not other.

Feminine nominative plural:

**अनन्याः - ananyāḥ**.

And:

**स्वयं संविदः - svayaṃ saṃvidaḥ**

From consciousness itself, or in relation to consciousness itself, depending upon the syntactic construal.

The philosophical meaning is clear.

The six divine powers, although distinguishable in the symbolic exposition, do not ultimately possess independent conscious natures.

They are manifestations of the same self-aware reality.

### Why they nevertheless appear different

Abhinavagupta gives the reason.

The **saṃvedyopādhi**, the qualifying condition of what is cognized, introduces the appearance of difference.

Let's dissect that.

**संवेद्य - saṃvedya**

What is cognized, the apprehended object.

Related to *saṃ-√vid*, to know.

**उपाधि - upādhi**

Condition, qualification, limiting adjunct.

And:

**भेदकत्व - bhedakatva**

The condition of differentiating.

From:

**भेदक - bhedaka**

That which distinguishes.

And:

**-त्व - -tva**

Abstract condition.

Thus the object-qualification functions as a differentiator.

Now think about it.

Pot-cognition appears different from tree-cognition.

And tree-cognition appears different from the cognition of sound.

But the differentiation concerns the particular manifested contents.

It does not establish independently existing consciousnesses for each object.

**The content distinguishes the cognition; the supreme conscious ground does not become a collection of unrelated substances.**

And now, having established this, Abhinavagupta cites a wonderful verse involving your own shadow.

This is where the philosophical argument becomes unexpectedly funny.


## 10. Try jumping over the shadow of your own head

Abhinavagupta introduces an illustration attributed in the commentary to **Śrītantrasāra**.

VERSE QUOTED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA · P. 171

**स्वपदा स्वशिरश्छायां यद्वल्लङ्घितुमीहते।**

**पादोद्देशे शिरो न स्यात्तथेयं बैन्दवी कला॥**

*Svapadā svaśiraśchāyāṃ yadval laṅghitum īhate.*

*Pādoddeśe śiro na syāt tatheyaṃ baindavī kalā.*

The verse compares the relationship with the Baindavī power to trying to step across the shadow of one's own head: when one moves, the relationship between one's body and its shadow does not remain as though the shadow were an independently fixed object.

The Devanāgarī transcription has an anomalous spelling around *laṅghitum*; the GRETIL witness supports the normalized form **लङ्घितुम्**. The explanatory paragraphs following the verse in square brackets belong to the editor's apparatus, not to Abhinavagupta's uninterrupted prose.

Now this is a magnificent image.

Imagine standing in sunlight.

Your head casts a shadow.

You decide:

"I shall leap over the shadow of my own head."

So you jump.

But the shadow's position is related to your body and the illumination.

You cannot treat it as a completely independent object fixed in place while its originating conditions remain unrelated.

Now, an ordinary physical shadow can of course change in different ways according to the source of light and your movement.

The analogy is not a formal theorem of optics.

Its purpose is to illustrate a relationship of dependence.

### Svaśiras - स्वशिरस्

One's own head.

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**शिरस् - śiras**

Head.

Now:

**स्वशिरश्छाया - svaśiraśchāyā**

The shadow of one's own head.

The consonant joining is worth observing.

The *-s* of *śiras* combines with the following *chāyā*, producing the visible **श्छ** sequence.

### Chāyā - छाया

Shadow, reflection, image.

A feminine noun.

Accusative singular:

**छायाम् - chāyām**.

### Laṅghitum - लङ्घितुम्

To leap over, cross, transgress.

From √**laṅgh**, to leap or cross.

The ending **-tum** is the infinitive formation.

So:

**लङ्घितुम् - laṅghitum**

To leap over.

### Īhate - ईहते

Attempts, strives, wishes.

From √**īh**, to desire or strive.

Third-person singular present middle.

Thus the verse describes someone trying to leap across the shadow.

### Baindavī Kalā - बैन्दवी कला

This is particularly important.

**बिन्दु - bindu**

Point, drop, dot.

The derivative:

**बैन्दव - baindava**

Pertaining to Bindu.

Feminine:

**बैन्दवी - baindavī**.

Then:

**कला - kalā**

Power, part, aspect.

So:

**बैन्दवी कला - baindavī kalā**

The power associated with Bindu.

Now the philosophical application.

The self-revealing power of consciousness cannot be treated as though it depended for its very nature upon an independently existing known object.

The object is apprehended within consciousness.

It is not an external metaphysical master standing over consciousness.

And, just as the shadow analogy challenges the attempt to establish a permanently independent position for the shadow relative to its source, the passage challenges the reification of the known object as the source of the knowing power.

### What the editor adds

The bracketed gloss following the verse explicitly interprets it as a warning against thinking that *jñānaśakti* depends upon the existence of knowable objects.

It contrasts ordinary, gross apprehension with the subtler recognition of self-luminous knowledge.

That's a useful interpretation.

But it is an editorial explanation following the cited verse.

The important distinction is that **Abhinavagupta supplies the verse and then proceeds directly to his conclusion about the six powers**. We should not attribute every sentence of the bracketed philosophical expansion to him. ; \]

### The actual conclusion

Knowledge can appear with particularized contents.

The contents can differ.

But the self-revealing character of consciousness isn't simply made by those contents.

And that's why the six vowel-powers can be distinguished in the sacred exposition without becoming ultimately separate conscious substances.

Now we've reached the next boundary.

Abhinavagupta has finished explaining the initial six.

And he finally announces the arrival of the power of action.

The next sentence is so direct that it's almost comical.

After all that metaphysics:

"Right. Six completed. Now action."


## 11. तदेवं षट्कं प्रवृत्तम् - Six done. Now the Goddess starts doing things

Here's the decisive sentence.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · PP. 171-172

**तदेवं षट्कं प्रवृत्तं ज्ञानशक्त्यन्तम्।**

**क्रियाशक्तिस्तु प्रसरन्ती विचार्यते।**

*Tad evaṃ ṣaṭkaṃ pravṛttaṃ jñānaśaktyantam.*

*Kriyāśaktis tu prasarantī vicāryate.*

Thus the sixfold sequence has unfolded, ending with the power of knowledge. Now the power of action, as it expands, is to be examined.

Well.

Here we are.

Six vowels.

One enormous philosophical argument.

And now Abhinavagupta says it's time for action.

### Ṣaṭka - षट्क

A group of six.

From:

**षट् - ṣaṭ**

Six.

And the collective suffix:

**-क - -ka**.

Thus:

**षट्क - ṣaṭka**

A set of six.

Now:

### Pravṛttam - प्रवृत्तम्

Has unfolded, proceeded, become active.

From:

**प्र - pra**

Forth.

**√वृत् - √vṛt**

To turn, occur, proceed.

The past participial form is:

**प्रवृत्त - pravṛtta**.

Neuter nominative singular:

**प्रवृत्तम् - pravṛttam**.

It agrees with:

**षट्कम् - ṣaṭkam**.

And:

### Jñānaśaktyantam - ज्ञानशक्त्यन्तम्

Ending in Jñānaśakti.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

**अन्त - anta**

End, limit.

So:

**jñānaśakti + anta → jñānaśaktyanta**.

The final *i* of *śakti* becomes the semivowel *y* before the following vowel.

**i + a → ya** in this sandhi environment.

Thus:

**ज्ञानशक्त्यन्त - jñānaśaktyanta**

Ending in the power of knowledge.

Now the second statement.

### Kriyāśakti - क्रियाशक्ति

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action, doing, activity.

From √**कृ - kṛ**, to do or make, through its established nominal formation.

And:

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

So:

**क्रियाशक्ति - kriyāśakti**

The power of action.

Now:

**प्रसरन्ती - prasarantī**

Expanding, spreading forth.

Feminine nominative singular present participle.

And:

**विचार्यते - vicāryate**

Is to be examined, is considered.

A passive verbal form associated with *vi-√car*, to examine or investigate.

Now notice the sentence's structure.

The feminine subject is *kriyāśaktiḥ*.

The participle *prasarantī* agrees with it.

And the verb *vicāryate* announces the new investigation.

Abhinavagupta is giving us the next topic.

Not an ornamental theological aside.

**The next phonemic group expresses the emergence of action.**

### The important distinction

Don't interpret this as saying Śiva suddenly acquires the ability to act after he has acquired knowledge.

That would contradict everything we've learned about supreme freedom.

The sequence belongs to the articulated teaching.

The powers are intrinsically inseparable in consciousness.

But their manifestations can be distinguished.

Will.

Knowledge.

Action.

And now we're studying action as the specific form in which **the already present powers of will and knowledge become ordered, interwoven and expressed**.

That is Abhinavagupta's next point.

And it's exceptionally important.

Because action isn't introduced as a completely foreign third substance.

It emerges through the relationship between the first two powers.


## 12. क्रिया - What is action, really?

The commentary's next prose is a glorious Sanskrit compound.

Rather than pretend it's a straightforward one-line formula, we'll divide its actual components.

Abhinavagupta describes *kriyā* as:

**इच्छाज्ञाने एव ... संरम्भसारा क्रिया।**

*Icchājñāne eva ... saṃrambhasārā kriyā.*

Will and knowledge themselves, through the wondrous intermixture and ordering of their natures, constitute action, whose essence is active exertion.

Now here's the key.

**Action is not independent of will and knowledge.**

The divine powers become related through differentiated manifestation.

And their interrelationship is experienced as activity.

Let's learn the compounds.

### Icchājñāne - इच्छाज्ञाने

Will and knowledge.

Start with:

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

The compound is a dual *dvandva*, joining two terms.

Because the final member is the neuter noun *jñāna*, the dual form ends in **-e**:

**इच्छाज्ञाने - icchājñāne**.

Not to be mistaken for a locative singular here.

The grammar conveys two powers considered together.

### Paraspara - परस्पर

Mutual, reciprocal, one another.

You'll encounter this constantly in philosophical Sanskrit.

**परस्परसम्बन्ध - parasparasaṃbandha**

Mutual relationship.

And:

**परस्परस्वरूप - parasparasvarūpa**

The respective natures of one another.

### Sāṅkarya - साङ्कर्य

Mixture, intermingling, confusion of categories or mutual blending, depending on context.

Here the positive meaning of **interpenetration** is central.

The will-power and knowledge-power aren't simply two sealed objects placed next to each other.

Their manifested relationship produces diverse forms.

Now:

### Vaicitrya - वैचित्र्य

Variety, diversity, variegation.

Related to:

**चित्र - citra**

Variegated, many-coloured.

**विचित्र - vicitra**

Diverse, remarkable, extraordinary.

Then:

**वैचित्र्य - vaicitrya**

The condition of diversity.

### Camatkāra - चमत्कार

Wonder, delight, astonishment.

Again.

The word is not an irrelevant decorative flourish.

It describes the expressive wonder of consciousness's own manifestation.

Now combine the ideas.

Mutuality.

Intermingling.

Variety.

Wonder.

And the reception of a form involving before and after.

### Pūrva-aparībhūta - पूर्वापरीभूत

Having taken the form of prior and subsequent.

**पूर्व - pūrva**

Former.

**अपर - apara**

Later, subsequent.

**भूत - bhūta**

Become.

So the expression concerns the acquisition of a differentiated sequential form.

This is the heart of the argument.

**Action becomes intelligible through an ordered relation among distinguishable aspects.**

Now think about something ordinary.

You make tea.

You take the leaves.

Heat water.

Pour.

Wait.

Taste.

There is an intention.

There is knowledge of the procedure.

And there is an unfolding sequence of activity.

But we shouldn't reduce *kriyāśakti* to ordinary kitchen behaviour.

The example illustrates the structural relation.

Within the divine manifestation, the powers of will and knowledge can be expressed through ordered differentiation.

And that differentiated expression is called *kriyā*.

### Saṃrambha - संरम्भ

Exertion, energetic undertaking, excitement, determined activity.

From a formation involving *sam-* and √**rabh**, to grasp, undertake or begin.

The compound:

**संरम्भसारा - saṃrambhasārā**

Having exertion or energetic engagement as its essence.

And this qualifies:

**क्रिया - kriyā**.

Now that's a lovely definition.

Action isn't merely a sequence of inert mechanical positions.

In the Tantric interpretation, it is an **active expression of the freedom of consciousness**.

It manifests through the mutual involvement of will and knowledge.

But their shared ground remains the same.

### The cosmic operations department

Imagine Śiva's powers as three departments.

Will says:

"We shall manifest."

Knowledge says:

"Here is the intelligible structure of what becomes manifest."

Action says:

"Right. I'll handle the actual unfolding."

Now the departments aren't genuinely separate organizations.

They're distinguishable functions of one divine freedom.

And if the first two powers were entirely unrelated, action wouldn't be a coherent expression of their relation.

But if action were simply identical with will in every respect, the distinction between intention, intelligibility and manifestation would disappear.

Abhinavagupta wants both distinctions.

**One consciousness, three inseparable but analytically distinguishable powers.**

This is why the phonemic system is so useful to him.

It can represent the powers separately while retaining their underlying unity.

### The editorial Bhartṛhari quotations

Immediately after this statement, the Kashmir Series includes square-bracketed verses attributed to **Hari**, commonly understood in this philosophical context as Bhartṛhari.

Those verses explain *kriyā* through the conceptual construction of sequentially arising parts.

Their relevance is clear.

Action can be understood through a set of events or parts that the intellect comprehends as one process.

But those verses are **inside the bracketed editorial explanation**.

So we should not present them as additional lines of Abhinavagupta's own prose at p. 172.

His unbracketed argument resumes by describing the mutual intermixture of the powers.

And the next analogy is rather wonderful.

It involves a frog.

Because apparently the pot wasn't enough.


## 13. The frog leaps - Even divine action needs a point of departure

Now Abhinavagupta makes a comparison involving:

**भेक - bheka**

Frog.

And:

**प्लव - plava**

Floating, swimming, a raft or a leap, depending on context.

The main prose contains the expression:

**प्लवानामिव भेकादिः**

*Plavānām iva bhekādiḥ.*

Like a frog or similar creature in relation to a leap or floating movement.

Now this is a compact simile whose exact force depends on the surrounding description.

The broader argument concerns how one differentiated state comes into relation with another, requiring a movement through a condition that can be compared to emptiness or an unobjectified interval.

Let's examine the important terms.

### Bheka - भेक

Frog.

A perfectly ordinary Sanskrit noun.

And:

### Plava - प्लव

Something floating, a floating motion, a leap or a raft in different contexts.

The derivational family belongs to √**plu**, to float, swim or leap.

Now picture a frog leaping.

It begins from one position.

Moves.

And arrives elsewhere.

We can distinguish its departure.

Its motion.

Its arrival.

But the movement isn't simply a collection of three completely independent objects.

It is one intelligible action with distinguishable phases.

Now Abhinavagupta connects such relational transformation with an apparently empty or nonobjectified condition.

The important phrase is:

**शून्यप्रायस्वरूप - śūnyaprāyasvarūpa**

Having a nature resembling emptiness.

Let's break that down.

### Śūnya - शून्य

Empty, void, vacant.

In different Indian philosophical contexts, the term has quite different implications.

And we must be careful not to import Buddhist *śūnyatā* wholesale into every Śaiva use of the word.

Here it participates in the account of the subtle or unobjectified condition entered as differentiated powers are related.

### Prāya - प्राय

Mostly, resembling, approximately.

So:

**शून्यप्राय - śūnyaprāya**

Almost empty, void-like.

And:

**स्वरूप - svarūpa**

Own nature, intrinsic form.

Thus:

**śūnyaprāyasvarūpa**

Having a void-like nature.

Now the philosophical point.

Between differentiated manifestations, the underlying consciousness isn't another ordinary object occupying one of the differentiated positions.

The relationship can be expressed through an entry into a more subtle, nonobjectified ground.

But this ground is not the complete annihilation of awareness.

It is the condition through which the differentiated manifestation remains possible.

### Anāmarśanīya - अनामर्शनीय

Not ordinarily graspable or apprehensible.

From a negative prefix and a formation related to √*mṛś*, to apprehend or examine.

Now, here's the important distinction.

If the ground is *anāmarśanīya* in a particular context, that doesn't mean it is completely unconscious.

The statement concerns the limits of determinate objectification.

The supreme *vimarśa* is not equivalent to an ordinary object being grasped by a mental act.

And that's why Abhinavagupta can distinguish the unobjectified ground from the particular expressions arising within it.

### The frog and the Absolute

Imagine a frog leaping from one lily pad to another.

The first pad is distinct from the second.

The frog's movement is distinguishable from both.

But the movement doesn't require us to invent a completely separate universe for the interval between the pads.

Likewise, the transformation of the divine powers into ordered action need not establish a second ultimate consciousness standing between them.

The analogy is imperfect.

But its purpose is to illuminate **continuity through differentiation**.

And Abhinavagupta immediately guards against a crucial misunderstanding.

When these powers enter the more subtle ground, Anuttara and Ānanda do not disappear.

That's the next point.


## 14. Anuttara and Ānanda never leave the room

Here is the decisive claim.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 172

*Tatrānuttarānandātmakaṃ vapur na vyapasarati.*

There, the nature consisting of Anuttara and Ānanda does not depart.

Now that is a magnificent statement.

Because it's precisely what we need to avoid the idea that the Absolute becomes something else and loses itself during manifestation.

### Anuttara - अनुत्तर

Unsurpassable.

### Ānanda - आनन्द

Bliss.

### Ātmaka - आत्मक

### Vapus - वपुस्

### Vyapasarati - व्यपसरति

### The two different sorts of “before”

So the emergence of *kriyāśakti* doesn't mean Anuttara and Ānanda have been used up.

### Sarvādhāra - सर्वाधार

Support of everything.

**सर्व - sarva**

All.

**आधार - ādhāra**

Support, basis, foundation.

Thus:

**सर्वाधार - sarvādhāra**

The support of all.

And:

**पर्यन्तभित्ति - paryantabhitti**

Final supporting wall or ultimate boundary.

**पर्यन्त - paryanta**

Limit, end, ultimate extent.

**भित्ति - bhitti**

Wall, support, surface.

Now the important distinction.

And that's exactly why the next phonemes are interpreted through entry into a subtle, apparently void-like condition.

Because action is now moving toward its more explicit manifestation.

**ऋ - ṛ.**


## 15. The emergence of the vocalic ṛ - A sound that looks like a consonant pretending to be a vowel

We've finally reached Kashmir Series p. 173.

And now Abhinavagupta introduces the next sound-pair.

# ऋ - ॠ

ṛ - ṝ

Now these are extraordinary Sanskrit vowels.

Because their historical phonetic relationship involves the rhotic sound *r*, yet their grammatical role in the traditional alphabet is **vocalic**.

They are not simply ordinary combinations *ri* and *rī*.

And Abhinavagupta uses that structure for an esoteric explanation of the power of action.

Here is his statement:

**भास्वरं रूपं तेजोमयमिव प्रथमं गाहते ऋ-ॠ इति।**

*Bhāsvaraṃ rūpaṃ tejomayam iva prathamaṃ gāhate ṛ-ṝ iti.*

She first enters a luminous, almost fiery form, designated by *ṛ* and *ṝ*.

Now let's learn it.

### Bhāsvara - भास्वर

Shining, luminous, brilliant.

Related to √**bhās**, to shine or appear.

Remember:

**भासते - bhāsate**

Shines, appears.

And:

**आभास - ābhāsa**

Appearance, manifestation.

Same broad root-family.

### Tejomaya - तेजोमय

Made of light, brilliance or fiery energy.

**तेजस् - tejas**

Light, splendour, heat, brilliance.

**मय - maya**

Consisting of, made of.

So:

**तेजोमय - tejomaya**

Consisting of brilliance.

Now the sandhi.

The stem *tejas* appears in the compound as:

**तेजो - tejo**.

Thus:

**तेजोमय - tejomaya**.

And:

### Prathamam - प्रथमम्

First, initially.

An adverbial accusative use.

### Gāhate - गाहते

Enters, plunges into, immerses itself.

From √**gāh**, to enter deeply, plunge or penetrate.

Now the image becomes clear.

The will-power, capable of ordered manifestation, enters a luminous form.

And that entry is represented through the vocalic *ṛ* and *ṝ*.

### Why the sound ṛ?

Abhinavagupta connects the vowel with:

**रेफ - repha**

The *r* sound or the name used for the phoneme *r* in grammatical and phonemic discussion.

Now here's the ordinary phonology.

The consonant:

**र - ra**

And the vocalic vowel:

**ऋ - ṛ**

Are related in the traditional Sanskrit phonological system but remain distinct phonemes.

The dependent sign of vocalic *ṛ* is:

**ृ**

For example:

**कृ - kṛ**.

Now compare:

**क्रि - kri**.

These are not the same.

**कृ - kṛ** uses the vocalic *ṛ* sign.

**क्रि - kri** uses the consonant cluster **क्र** with the vowel sign **ि**.

That difference matters.

And Abhinavagupta's esoteric explanation explicitly uses the resemblance to *r* as part of the metaphysical symbolism.

He connects the initial will-powers **इ - i** and **ई - ī** with the luminous *repha* sound.

Now that is **not** an ordinary Sanskrit sandhi rule that mechanically generates the historical phoneme *ṛ* from *i* plus *r*.

It's Tantric phonemic interpretation.

The linguistic relationship provides the symbolic material.

The theological interpretation supplies the meaning.

### The statement about Puṣpadanta

Abhinavagupta cites **Bhagavān Puṣpadanta** and the fragment:

**रश्रुतिसामान्याद्वा सिद्धम्।**

*Raśrutisāmānyād vā siddham.*

Something is established through the shared audible character of *r*.

The citation supports his appeal to the sound relationship.

But the passage gives only this fragment. I'm not reconstructing an entire grammatical treatise or attributing a larger argument to Puṣpadanta from the fragment alone. ; \]

Now the philosophy.

The power of will enters the subtle ground and begins assuming a luminous form.

The movement is toward manifestation.

But the underlying Anuttara-Ānanda remains.

The *ṛ* vowels therefore symbolize a further articulation of the divine powers through their relation to luminous, active expression.

And naturally, the next question is:

## 16. ऌ - Ḷ: The Goddess enters the motionless ground

### Niścala - निश्चल

## 17. The strange status of ॡ - Is it long, or is it pluta?

Here's the important textual distinction.

The Kashmir Series Devanāgarī transcription represents the pair as:

**ऌ - ॡ**

But the GRETIL transcription gives the second member as **ḷ3**, a protracted vocalic *ḷ*.

And Abhinavagupta's immediately following discussion concerns **plutatva**, protracted duration.

So we mustn't simply announce that *ॡ* here is an ordinary, unproblematic long vowel with precisely the same status as *आ*.

The text itself is more subtle. ; \]

### Hrasva - ह्रस्व

Short.

Traditionally one *mātrā* in vowel quantity.

### Dīrgha - दीर्घ

Long.

Traditionally two *mātrās*.

### Pluta - प्लुत

Protracted.

Traditionally three *mātrās*.

Now the Sanskrit example:

**अ - a**

Short.

**आ - ā**

Long.

And a protracted *a* can be indicated in grammatical notation as:

**अ३ - a3**.

The numeral conventionally marks protracted length.

Now here's the issue.

The long vocalic *ḷ* has an unusual status.

Abhinavagupta invokes the principle:

**ऌवर्णस्य दीर्घा न सन्ति।**

*Ḷvarṇasya dīrghā na santi.*

There are no long forms of the *ḷ* vowel.

This is the grammatical maxim he's invoking, and the subsequent explanation concerns treating the relevant extension through protraction.

Now, the exact form transmitted as **ॡ** in one edition and **ḷ3** in another shouldn't be silently equated.

They're distinct ways of presenting or understanding the extended vocalic element.

The Sanskrit argument explicitly discusses the difference between long and protracted quantity.

### The actual phonological issue

Ordinary Sanskrit phonology distinguishes three quantities:

Short.

Long.

Protracted.

But not every vowel has the same ordinary distribution across all three categories in actual linguistic usage.

And the vocalic *ḷ* is particularly exceptional.

The traditionally listed *ḹ* is exceedingly rare as an independent lexical vowel.

Abhinavagupta therefore uses an inherited grammatical observation about this sound when explaining the sacred fourfold group.

Now this is important because the text's Tantric symbolic interpretation is **not simply identical with ordinary historical phonology**.

Abhinavagupta draws upon phonological distinctions and grammatical discussions, but he interprets them through his metaphysics of Śakti.

The phonetic discussion supplies a framework.

The symbolic account explains the entry into the motionless and unobjectified condition.

**दीर्घतर - dīrghatara**

From:

**दीर्घ - dīrgha**

Long.

Comparative suffix.

Thus:

**dīrghatara**

Longer.

The active power enters a more subtle condition and is described as extending beyond an ordinary determinate phonemic duration.

## 18. दग्धबीज - The four vowels are like roasted seeds

Now we're at Kashmir Series p. 174.

Abhinavagupta describes the fourfold group through:

**दग्धबीजमिव षण्ठरूपम्।**

*Dagdhabījam iva ṣaṇṭharūpam.*

Like a burnt seed, having a non-generative form.

Now, this requires care.

The traditional adjective *ṣaṇṭha* can refer to a person lacking generative capacity and has historical associations with eunuchhood.

Here its function is **metaphorical**: the four vocalic powers are being described as relatively non-generative in a particular symbolic relation.

We're not making a statement about human worth or physiology.

And the interpretation is much more subtle than simply saying the sounds are useless.

### Dagdha - दग्ध

Burnt, scorched.

From √**dah**, to burn.

The past participle has the familiar form:

**दग्ध - dagdha**.

Notice the conjunct:

**ग्ध = ग् + ध**.

Now:

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

Thus:

**दग्धबीज - dagdhabīja**

Burnt seed.

And:

**इव - iva**

Like, as if.

So:

**दग्धबीजमिव - dagdhabījam iva**

Like a burnt seed.

Now imagine an ordinary seed.

Under appropriate conditions, it can germinate.

But if the seed has been thoroughly roasted, its capacity to germinate is ordinarily destroyed.

It may retain its outward appearance.

It still looks like a seed.

But its generative function has been transformed.

That's the analogy.

### Why the four vowels are compared with burnt seeds

The four vocalic powers have entered a condition described through *śūnyarūpatā*, the form of emptiness.

They therefore lack a certain active generative function associated with other seed-powers.

But Abhinavagupta explicitly refuses to conclude that they have ceased to be seeds altogether.

This is very important.

The Sanskrit immediately qualifies the metaphor.

**न तु सर्वथा बीजरूपत्वाभावात्।**

*Na tu sarvathā bījarūpatvābhāvāt.*

Not because their seed-nature is entirely absent.

Now learn it.

### Sarvathā - सर्वथा

In every way, altogether, completely.

### Bījarūpatva - बीजरूपत्व

The condition of having the nature of a seed.

Break it down:

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form.

**त्व - tva**

Condition.

So:

**bījarūpatva**

Seed-nature.

### Abhāva - अभाव

Absence, nonexistence, lack.

From:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**भाव - bhāva**

Being, existence.

Thus:

**अभाव - abhāva**

Nonexistence or absence.

Now the compound:

**बीजरूपत्वाभाव - bījarūpatvābhāva**

Absence of the condition of being a seed.

And the ablative ending:

**-आत् - -āt**

Gives:

**बीजरूपत्वाभावात् - bījarūpatvābhāvāt**.

Because of the absence of seed-nature.

But the statement denies that such complete absence applies.

The four vowels are not utterly excluded from the seed principle.

### Why can't there be an entirely third category?

Abhinavagupta gives the reason.

The entire symbolic system is grounded in the dual-aspect structure of:

**बीज - bīja**

Seed, associated with Śiva.

And:

**योनि - yoni**

Generative matrix, associated with Śakti.

Those two are not independent ultimate substances.

They're distinguishable generative powers of one reality.

The four vocalic sounds cannot simply be declared a third completely unrelated principle standing outside Śiva and Śakti.

So even when they are described as relatively non-generative, they remain within the underlying seed-matrix structure.

**They are like burnt seeds in one functional respect, not absolutely devoid of divine phonemic nature.**

That is the subtlety.

### The editorial explanation

The Kashmir Series bracketed gloss develops the distinction between being affected or stirred and actively producing such stirring.

It's a useful interpretation of the metaphor.

But the main prose itself establishes the two essential points:

The four vowels are compared with burnt seeds.

Their seed-character is **not wholly abolished**.

That's the argument we can securely attribute to Abhinavagupta here. ; \]

### A beautiful practical analogy

Imagine a capacity that is present but no longer operating in its ordinary productive manner.

It's not simply nonexistent.

But its function has changed.

Similarly, these four phonemic powers retain their place within the universal divine system while being associated with repose in an unobjectified condition.

That's why the text uses the burnt-seed image.

And it prepares the next designation.

**Amṛtabīja.**

The immortal or nectar-bearing seeds.


## 19. अमृतबीजचतुष्क - The four nectar-seeds

Now Abhinavagupta identifies the group as:

**अमृतबीजचतुष्कम्**

*Amṛtabījacatuṣkam.*

The fourfold group of immortal or nectar-seeds.

Let's dismantle it.

### Amṛta - अमृत

Deathless, immortal, nectar.

From:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**मृत - mṛta**

Dead.

The past participle of √**mṛ**, to die.

Thus:

**अमृत - amṛta**

Not dead, deathless, immortal.

And in many religious contexts:

Nectar of immortality.

### Bīja - बीज

Seed.

### Catuṣka - चतुष्क

Group of four.

From:

**चतुर् - catur**

Four.

Plus a collective suffix.

Thus:

**चतुष्क - catuṣka**

A set of four.

So:

**अमृतबीजचतुष्क - amṛtabījacatuṣka**

The group of four immortal seeds.

Now this is an extraordinary name for the very sounds that were just compared with burnt seeds.

Why?

Because their relative non-generativity doesn't mean they are spiritually empty or insignificant.

Their relation to the void-like condition is associated with repose.

And that repose is interpreted through *ānanda*, bliss.

The text explicitly brings the discussion back to ordinary experiences of happiness and repose as an analogy.

### Viśrānti - विश्रान्ति

Rest, repose, settling.

From *vi-√śram*, to rest or become weary, through its established formation.

The important philosophical sense here is **repose**.

Consciousness resting in its own nature.

Not merely physical sleep.

And not unconscious blankness.

### Why repose is blissful

In the Trika interpretation, the complete absence of restlessness or external striving can become an occasion for recognizing the fullness of consciousness.

The analogy with ordinary pleasure suggests that the cessation of a particular agitation can be associated with a state of repose.

But this does not make every pleasurable rest equivalent to ultimate liberation.

The technical *ānanda* belongs to the supreme self-revealing consciousness.

The ordinary experience offers a limited analogy.

And now the four vowel-powers become intelligible.

They are associated with an entry into a subtle, void-like condition.

Their outward generative activity is comparatively inhibited.

Yet their divine seed-character isn't destroyed.

And their repose is related to bliss.

So the name:

**Amṛtabījacatuṣka**.

The four nectar-seeds.

### The ultimate irony

We've now gone from:

"The Absolute possesses unlimited creative power."

To:

"Action emerges from the intermixture of will and knowledge."

To:

"The first four new vowel-powers are like burnt seeds."

And finally:

"They are the four nectar-seeds."

This is the sort of passage that sounds completely insane if you simply recite the conclusions without following the argument.

But once the sequence is understood, the distinctions become coherent within the system.

Generative capacity.

Entry into stillness.

Relative suspension of outward production.

Retention of the underlying seed-character.

Repose in consciousness.

All belong to the same theological account.

And Abhinavagupta's prose has preserved those distinctions.

Now we have reached the next natural boundary.

Because the four vocalic powers have been explained.

The next passage asks how the earlier vowel-powers **अ आ इ ई** combine to form **ए - e**, and how the related combinations yield **ऐ, ओ and औ**.

That's a major transition.

We're about to encounter ordinary Sanskrit vowel sandhi transformed into an elaborate Tantric account of the union of Śiva's powers.

That belongs to Part XIV.


## 20. The whole argument, in the exact sequence we've followed

Now, the philosophical point I particularly want to retain is this:

**The known object differentiates consciousness's appearance, but it does not establish the basic existence of self-revealing awareness.**

Everything else follows from that.

The pot becomes intelligible as a manifestation.

The power of knowing is not produced by the pot.

The free emergence of objects can be understood through *īśana*.

The fullness of consciousness remains.

And its powers of will, knowledge and action can be distinguished without becoming independent substances.

That is a remarkable metaphysical structure.

And the Sanskrit phonemes give it a symbolic expression.

## 22. Where we stop in the original commentary

We've followed Kashmir Series pp. 170-174 continuously from the instruction to enter the subtle path of *vimarśa*.

We covered the cognition of a pot, the first subtle emergence of the known, the sovereign freedom through which manifestation becomes possible, the fullness and bliss of consciousness, the agency of Bhairava, and the convergence of the six vowel-powers.

We then followed Abhinavagupta's explicit introduction of *kriyāśakti*, his account of action through the intermingling of will and knowledge, the relation to an unobjectified ground, the persistence of Anuttara-Ānanda, and the symbolic interpretation of **ऋ ॠ ऌ ॡ**.

We reached the four *amṛtabījas* and the explanation of their apparent non-generativity.

**The next sentence begins the construction of compound vowels**, including the relationship between *a/ā* and *i/ī* producing *e*, and the related transitions toward *ai, o* and *au*.

The commentary then develops the role of Bindu, Visarga and the completion of *kriyāśakti*.

That is the natural next installment.

### The final insight - The pot never manufactured consciousness

After all the phonemes, the most important image remains the pot.

An ordinary object appears.

It has a particular form.

We can describe it.

Touch it.

Recognize it.

Remember it.

And distinguish it from other things.

But Abhinavagupta asks what makes that occurrence of recognition possible.

His answer is that the object does not manufacture the self-revealing nature of awareness.

Rather, the pot's particular form is manifest within consciousness.

And consciousness's freedom includes the capacity to display such forms.

So the pot doesn't create a new Absolute.

And action doesn't create a new independent power unrelated to will and knowledge.

The divine manifestation is the articulation of a reality already complete.

**Anuttara remains Anuttara even while the world appears.**

That's why the powers can differentiate without abandoning their ground.

And why the sequence of sounds can express divine action without becoming an external temporal law imposed upon Śiva.

The supreme consciousness is not a passive blank.

Its freedom is expressive.

Its expressions are distinguishable.

And their ultimate reality remains one.

Now the vowels are about to combine.

And the sacred alphabet is about to become an extraordinarily complicated account of how consciousness folds into itself.

After thirteen installments, we've finally found something more dangerous than the pot.

**Vowel sandhi.**
