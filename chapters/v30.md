# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa - Part XXX

Abhinavagupta's Absolutely Unhinged Guide to the Universe

## “The Guru, the Cosmic Magnet, and the Man Who Discovered That Time Is Basically a Terrible Administrative Arrangement”

*Kashmir Series of Texts and Studies XVIII, pp. 241-249 approximately; parallel to Gnoli's pagination, pp. 269-273.*

Previously, on *Abhinavagupta Explains the Universe and Somehow Makes Sanskrit Grammar Everyone's Problem*:

We discovered that the phoneme **स - sa** is associated with the nectar of supreme bliss; that linguistic meaning can be conveyed through astonishingly small units; that Patañjali and Bhartṛhari are unexpectedly relevant to the metaphysics of mantra; and that the supreme heart-mantra **सौः - sauḥ** embraces the differentiated cosmic order.

And just when you're expecting another six hundred pages explaining the difference between a vowel and the absolute, Abhinavagupta changes the subject.

To the guru.

Not just the guru's teachings.

The guru's *satisfaction*.

Apparently we have spent the entire afternoon establishing that ultimate reality is infinite, omnipresent, unbounded, and eternally self-luminous, only to discover that the cosmic mystery comes with a requirement that somebody be pleased with your behaviour.

Śiva is absolutely free.

Consciousness is not limited by space or time.

The world is the self-expression of divine sovereignty.

But the disciple may nevertheless need to sort out his conduct before receiving the mantra.

The theological equivalent of discovering that the doorway to infinity has an exceptionally particular admissions officer.

And Abhinavagupta is not embarrassed by this.

Because the apparent contradiction leads directly to one of the deepest questions of Trika:

**If liberation is the recognition of what you already are, what exactly is the guru giving you?**

More importantly, if Śiva alone is the ultimate agent, who initiates whom?

And why do the scriptures insist on transmission if consciousness is already present in everyone?

This chapter will become considerably stranger before we finish.

### The textual ground rules

We are proceeding through the Sanskrit in its actual order, from the *Mālinīvijayottaratantra* 3.57-58 quotation, through Abhinavagupta's explanations of the heart, mantra, mudrā, sudden recognition, time, perception, memory, the Mātṛkā goddesses, and the siddhis, ending immediately before his explicit introduction of *Parātriṃśikā* verse 18.

I compared three distinct kinds of witness:

- **1918 KSTS XVIII**, Mukunda Rāma Śāstrī's edition, represented by the digitised Devanāgarī text, including its manuscript notes and editorial explanations.
- **Somadeva Vasudeva's GRETIL transcription** of the *Parātriṃśikāvivaraṇa*, with Gnoli's 1985 page markers.
- **The independent *Mālinīvijayottaratantra***, chapters 3-4, in Vasudeva's critically edited text, alongside independently transmitted passages from the *Spandakārikā*.

The 1918 digital transcription contains apparent recognition and transcription errors. Where those cannot be separated confidently from genuine printed readings, I'll say so. The root *Mālinīvijayottara* transcription also lacks its full critical apparatus, so agreement between sources does not establish the complete manuscript history. ; ; \]


## I. The Guru Must Be Satisfied. Congratulations, Divine Freedom Has a Performance Review.

### Kashmir Series p. 241 - Gnoli p. 269

We resume at the exact word:

**तथा - tathā**

“And likewise.”

This is not a new theoretical proposition.

It introduces another scriptural quotation supporting the importance and mode of transmission of the supreme mantra.

The first verse, *Mālinīvijayottaratantra* 3.57, reads in the critically edited tantra:

**शिष्येणापि तदा ग्राह्या यदा संतोषितो गुरुः ।**

*śiṣyeṇāpi tadā grāhyā yadā saṃtoṣito guruḥ.*

**शरीरद्रव्यविज्ञानशुद्धिकर्मगुणादिभिः ॥**

*śarīradravyavijñānaśuddhikarmaguṇādibhiḥ.*

My literal translation:

“She is to be received by the disciple only when the guru has been satisfied through bodily service, resources, purification of understanding, actions, good qualities, and the like.”

“She” refers to the feminine mantra or sacred teaching discussed in the surrounding tantra.

And the second verse, 3.58:

**बोधिता तु यदा तेन गुरुणा हृष्टचेतसा ।**

*bodhitā tu yadā tena guruṇā hṛṣṭacetasā.*

**तदा सिद्धिप्रदा ज्ञेया नान्यथा वीरवन्दिते ॥**

*tadā siddhipradā jñeyā nānyathā vīravandite.*

“When she has been made known by that guru whose mind is delighted, then she should be understood as bestowing accomplishment-not otherwise, O you who are honoured by the heroes.”

These readings are supported by the independent *Mālinīvijayottara* text, although the GRETIL *Parātriṃśikāvivaraṇa* has a small initial spelling defect, *siṣyeṇāpi*, for *śiṣyeṇāpi*. ; ; \]

Now.

What the hell does this mean?

Have we just discovered that spiritual liberation operates according to a medieval customer-satisfaction questionnaire?

“On a scale of one to ten, how pleased is your guru with the quality of your devotional services?”

“Have you demonstrated sufficient metaphysical understanding, acceptable conduct, and the correct quantity of incense?”

“Thank you. Your application to transcend the thirty-six tattvas has been forwarded to the appropriate department.”

No.

The verses describe a traditional relationship of preparation, instruction, and transmission.

The teacher must be satisfied that the student has the requisite readiness.

But the really important language is in verse 58.

**bodhitā**.

Made known. Awakened. Brought to understanding.

The transmission is not described merely as the transfer of a physical object.

It is a disclosure.

Something becomes manifest.

And that opens the distinction between a mantra as a sequence of phonemes and a mantra as spiritually effective knowledge.

The student may hear the phonemes.

They may memorise the syllables.

They may reproduce the sound perfectly.

But the tradition distinguishes that from receiving the mantra in its fuller, realised significance.

This difference will become central in just a few lines.

### The grammar of receiving

**शिष्येणापि - śiṣyeṇāpi**

Split it:

*śiṣyeṇa + api.*

*Śiṣya* means pupil or disciple, related to √*śās*, to instruct or discipline.

**Śiṣyeṇa** is instrumental singular masculine.

The instrumental matters.

The verse does not say that the disciple is a passive object into which somebody mechanically inserts liberation.

The disciple is an agent participating in the acquisition of the teaching.

**api** contributes an inclusive or emphatic sense, here understood in the contextual construction.

External sandhi:

*a + a → ā.*

So:

*śiṣyeṇa api → śiṣyeṇāpi.*

In Devanāgarī:

शिष्येण + अपि → शिष्येणापि

Look carefully at **ष्य**.

This represents ष् + य.

The consonant ṣ does not receive its ordinary inherent *a* before *ya*.

You pronounce *śiṣ-ye-ṇa*, not *śi-ṣa-ye-ṇa*.

Then:

**ग्राह्या - grāhyā**

A feminine nominative singular gerundive associated with √*grah*, to seize, grasp, take, or understand.

It has a passive sense of obligation or suitability: “to be grasped,” “to be received.”

Because the referent is feminine, the ending is **-ā**.

The mantra is not just something the pupil happens to hear.

It is something to be properly received.

**संतोषितः - saṃtoṣitaḥ**

From *sam-* \+ √*tuṣ*, to be satisfied or pleased, with a causative-passive formation.

It means “having been satisfied,” “having been pleased.”

The form is masculine nominative singular, agreeing with **guruḥ**.

The guru is the one who has been satisfied.

The grammatical construction is straightforward.

The philosophical implications are anything but.

### The long compound: body, resources, knowledge, purification, action, qualities

Now look at:

**शरीरद्रव्यविज्ञानशुद्धिकर्मगुणादिभिः**

*śarīra-dravya-vijñāna-śuddhi-karma-guṇa-ādibhiḥ*

This is a long compound whose final member bears the instrumental plural ending **-bhiḥ**.

The listed elements are means by which the teacher is pleased.

**śarīra** - body.

**dravya** - substance, property, material resources.

**vijñāna** - discriminative knowledge or understanding, from *vi-* \+ √*jñā*, to know.

**śuddhi** - purification, cleansing, connected with √*śudh*.

**karman** - action, deed, activity, from √*kṛ*, to do.

**guṇa** - quality, characteristic, excellence.

**ādi** - beginning with, and other such things.

The compound does not necessarily require that *vijñāna* and *śuddhi* be treated as entirely separate items. In context, purification of knowledge is a natural interpretation.

But the Sanskrit supports a range of grammatical relations, and the exact significance of the compound must be understood within the ritual context.

Most importantly, the verse does not teach that giving the teacher money automatically buys initiation.

*Dravya* may include material offerings or resources, but the full compound also stresses knowledge, proper conduct, and qualities.

The teacher's satisfaction is related to the student's preparedness.

Whether every historical guru lived up to that ideal is another matter entirely.

The Sanskrit does not provide a blanket endorsement of whatever demands any individual teacher chooses to make.

### What does hṛṣṭacetasā mean?

**हृष्टचेतसा - hṛṣṭacetasā**

Two components:

*hṛṣṭa* - delighted, joyful, pleased.

*cetas* - mind, consciousness, thought, inward disposition.

The compound is instrumental singular masculine, agreeing with **guruṇā**.

So:

*tena guruṇā hṛṣṭacetasā* -

“By that guru whose mind is pleased.”

The participle *hṛṣṭa* is connected with √*hṛṣ*, to rejoice, bristle, or become excited.

The semantic range includes the familiar bodily expressions of exhilaration.

And recall the previous chapter.

We have just discussed *sītkāra*, bodily trembling, and expressions of bliss.

Now the guru is described as *hṛṣṭacetas*, delighted in mind.

The verses are not asserting that the guru must be in a permanent physiological state of excitement.

But there is a beautiful continuity in their vocabulary.

The teaching is transmitted through a relation of understanding, satisfaction, and awakened attention.

Not merely through the mechanical movement of sounds.

### Vīravandite

**वीरवन्दिते - vīravandite**

*Vīra* - hero, here especially the heroic or initiated figure of the tantric religious world.

*Vandita* - honoured, praised, saluted, from √*vand*.

The ending **-e** is feminine vocative singular of the relevant *-ā*-stem adjective.

The speaker addresses the goddess:

“O one honoured by the heroes.”

This is significant because it reminds us that the passage comes from a dialogue within a tantra.

It is not Abhinavagupta himself addressing the reader as *vīravandite*.

He is quoting another scripture.

Do not erase the literary framing.

And do not invent a new initiation dialogue in its place.


## II. Three Readings, Three Very Different Problems

The 1918 KSTS transcription does not reproduce exactly the same text as the critically edited *Mālinīvijayottara*.

Here are the important differences.

| Location | 1918 digital KSTS | Independent tantra |
|--------|--------|--------|
| 3.57cd | *vijñāna-jāti-karma* | *vijñāna-śuddhi-karma* |
| 3.58ab | *bheditā* | *bodhitā* |
| 3.58cd | *siddhimadā* | *siddhipradā* |

The KSTS apparatus itself records **bodhitā** as a reading of manuscript ग. That is particularly important evidence: the editor was aware of a form agreeing with the independently transmitted tantra. The electronic KSTS transcription gives *siddhimadā*, but I cannot establish from this transcription alone whether that is a faithful printed reading or a digital error; it should remain flagged rather than promoted into a secure manuscript variant. ; \]

Let's consider them.

### Jāti versus śuddhi

**जाति - jāti** means birth, kind, class, or caste, among other related senses.

**शुद्धि - śuddhi** means purification.

These generate different interpretations of the instrumental compound.

With *jāti*, the verse may incorporate birth or social classification into the qualifications and means of pleasing the teacher.

With *śuddhi*, it foregrounds purification.

The independent critical edition has *śuddhi*.

One must not silently replace *jāti* in a presentation of the 1918 text, nor explain the critical reading as if both editions agreed.

This isn't a tiny typographic difference.

It changes the substance of the passage.

### Bheditā versus bodhitā

**भेदिता - bheditā**

From the causative formation associated with √*bhid*, to split, divide, distinguish, or penetrate.

Depending on context, a passive participial form can convey having been differentiated or penetrated.

**बोधिता - bodhitā**

From the causative of √*budh*, to wake, know, understand.

“Made known,” “awakened,” “instructed.”

In the verse's context of initiation and transmission, *bodhitā* makes excellent sense.

The fact that KSTS reports it as a manuscript reading, and that it appears in the independent tantra, is substantial evidence in its favour.

But it is still important to preserve the other reading as transmitted.

### Siddhimadā versus siddhipradā

**सिद्धिप्रदा - siddhipradā**

A feminine compound meaning “giver of accomplishment.”

*Siddhi* is accomplishment, attainment, success, or a special power depending on context.

*Pra-da* derives from *pra-* \+ √*dā*, to give.

The feminine ending agrees with the mantra.

The word therefore means “bestowing siddhi.”

The electronic KSTS *siddhimadā* is more difficult. Without inspecting the original print at this precise point, we should not confidently emend it into something else or invent a special tantric meaning for it.

Both the independent tantra and GRETIL give **siddhipradā**.

For explaining the verse, that is the better-supported reading.

### And the metre?

Both verses are **anuṣṭubh**, four pādas of eight syllables.

For 3.57:

| Pāda | Syllables | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *śiṣyeṇāpi tadā grāhyā* | śiṣ-ye-ṇā-pi-ta-dā-grā-hyā | 8 |
| *yadā saṃtoṣito guruḥ* | ya-dā-saṃ-to-ṣi-to-gu-ruḥ | 8 |
| *śarīradravyavijñāna-* | śa-rī-ra-dra-vya-vi-jñā-na | 8 |
| *śuddhikarmaguṇādibhiḥ* | śud-dhi-kar-ma-gu-ṇā-di-bhiḥ | 8 |

Here *dra-vya*, *jñā-na*, and the consonant groups are counted according to spoken Sanskrit syllables, not by counting the visible Devanāgarī components.

For 3.58:

| Pāda | Syllables | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *bodhitā tu yadā tena* | bo-dhi-tā-tu-ya-dā-te-na | 8 |
| *guruṇā hṛṣṭacetasā* | gu-ru-ṇā-hṛṣ-ṭa-ce-ta-sā | 8 |
| *tadā siddhipradā jñeyā* | ta-dā-sid-dhi-pra-dā-jñey-ā | 8 |
| *nānyathā vīravandite* | nā-nya-thā-vī-ra-van-di-te | 8 |

The alternative *bheditā* also scans properly.

So does *jāti* in place of *śuddhi*.

Therefore **metre does not settle these variants**.

The verse forms are metrically compatible.

For the individual *guruṇā hṛṣṭacetasā* pāda, note that *hṛṣṭa* contains the conjunct ष्ट, while *cetasā* has a long final ā.

And in **सिद्धिप्रदा**, the sequence द्ध represents द् + ध; प्र represents प् + र. The apparent four-member compound still fits comfortably into the metrical pattern.

The phonological structure, not the sheer visual complexity of the script, determines the count.

Now comes the philosophical question.

If the guru truly awakens the mantra in the disciple, how can the heart be the universal heart of Bhairava?

And why would the text insist that the mantra cannot simply be learned from a book?


## III. “Do Not Write This in a Book.” Says the Text We Are Literally Reading in a Book.

### Kashmir Series pp. 242-243 - Gnoli p. 269

The *Mālinīvijayottara* citation ends.

Abhinavagupta continues:

**इति । अन्यत्रापि -**

*iti. anyatrāpi -*

“Thus. And elsewhere, too-”

He introduces another quotation, beginning:

**एकं सृष्टिमयं बीजम्**

*ekaṃ sṛṣṭimayaṃ bījam*

“One seed consisting of creation…”

And then the quotation is truncated.

This is important. We must not silently import a complete verse from a related source and pretend that Abhinavagupta has quoted the whole thing here.

The 1918 editor's footnote supplies an expanded version concerning the single creative seed and Khecarī mudrā. A closely related formulation appears in *Tantrāloka* 32.64, where Abhinavagupta identifies the one creative seed as the power underlying all mantras and Khecarī as the mudrā animating the others. Related but different formulations also occur in later Haṭhayoga literature. ; ; \]

The common idea is clear.

There is a supreme seed that grounds the multitude of differentiated mantras.

And there is a supreme mode of conscious power through which the many *mudrās* acquire their significance.

One underlying principle.

Many manifestations.

Not a thousand independent magical syllables competing for control of the universe like rival telecommunications providers.

### Then comes the scandalous sentence

**अत एवालेख्यं पुस्तके इति नियमः**

*ata evālekhyaṃ pustake iti niyamaḥ.*

“Therefore there is the rule that it is not to be written in a book.”

Well.

Fucking fantastic.

We have now reached the part of the manuscript where the manuscript explains why the thing being explained should not be in a manuscript.

The universe is a manifestation of consciousness.

Speech is the expression of supreme freedom.

The mantra is the very heart of Bhairava.

And apparently Abhinavagupta has just placed a warning label on the entire publishing industry.

But don't overlook the actual issue.

### What is prohibited?

**alekhya** means “not to be written,” “not suitable for writing down,” or “not to be inscribed.”

The form contains the negative prefix *a-* and a gerundive formation related to √*likh*, to scratch, inscribe, or write.

**pustake** is locative singular of *pustaka*, a book or manuscript.

**niyamaḥ** means a rule, restriction, or regulation.

The sentence refers to a rule concerning the writing down of the sacred material.

It does not say that absolutely no philosophical discussion of the mantra can ever appear in writing.

If it did, the present commentary would be an exceptionally elaborate demonstration of noncompliance.

We must distinguish:

A transmitted instruction concerning the handling and disclosure of a powerful mantra.

The publicly accessible discussion of its metaphysical principles.

The complete ritual, initiatory, and experiential setting in which the mantra is understood to become efficacious.

These are not necessarily identical.

That difference is central to the passage.

### The deeper problem: Does the manuscript contain the mantra?

At one level, yes.

A manuscript can contain written phonemes.

But can it convey the entire reality of the mantra merely by displaying those phonemes?

That is precisely what Abhinavagupta's theory calls into question.

In Part XXIX, we distinguished the written character, the phoneme, its expressive capacity, and the supreme consciousness in which expression is grounded.

Now the distinction has become practical.

A written sequence can be copied.

But the act of copying does not necessarily reproduce understanding.

Think about a musical score.

The score contains the notation of a composition.

A trained musician can interpret it.

But the paper is not the performance.

Nor does reading the score guarantee that someone understands the emotional, structural, and aesthetic possibilities of the work.

The analogy is imperfect, but useful.

The text can preserve the sequence.

The teacher can provide instruction.

Practice can develop familiarity.

Yet the heart of the issue is the transformation of understanding.

In Trika, that transformation ultimately concerns *pratyabhijñā*: recognition.

Not the mechanical transfer of an object.

### A point about traditional secrecy

It is tempting to reconstruct the entire restriction as a friendly modern teaching about experiential learning.

But that would distort the historical context.

The tantra also participates in a culture of restricted transmission, initiatory qualifications, and specialised ritual authority.

The rule concerning writing has a genuinely esoteric dimension.

We should not erase that.

Nor should we conclude that a modern teacher's arbitrary demands are justified simply because a medieval scripture emphasises the importance of the guru.

The historical doctrine describes an initiation system.

It does not prove that any particular guru is infallible, immune to criticism, or entitled to exploit disciples.

The philosophical question remains:

**What is the difference between possessing sacred information and possessing the understanding to which that information points?**

And suddenly the apparent contradiction makes more sense.

The word can be printed.

The word can be memorised.

But the meaning that transforms the subject cannot be reduced to ink.


## IV. The Left Thigh, the Life-Principle, and the Sanskrit Version of an Encrypted Password

Still on Gnoli p. 269, Abhinavagupta cites the *Śrīpūrvaśāstra* again.

The fragment is:

**वामजङ्घान्वितो जीवः पारम्पर्यक्रमागतः ।**

*vāmajaṅghānvito jīvaḥ pāramparyakramāgataḥ.*

This is independently found as the second half of *Mālinīvijayottaratantra* 3.54. It is not the entirety of verse 3.54, and its immediate source context belongs to the tantra's discussion of mantra. ; \]

A cautious literal translation:

“The *jīva*, joined with the left thigh, has come through the succession of transmission.”

What the fuck?

We've gone from the guru's satisfaction to the guru's left thigh.

Is this an initiation or an orthopaedic examination?

Neither.

The fragment uses the characteristic coded language of tantric mantra instruction.

Words denoting bodily parts, directions, and principles can function as technical designators for phonemic components or elements in a ritual sequence.

**We cannot decode the exact mantra merely from the ordinary dictionary meanings of this isolated fragment.**

That would require the technical conventions and source context governing the expression.

But we can understand the grammar.

### Vāmajaṅghānvitaḥ

**vāma** - left, or in other contexts favourable, beautiful, or associated with particular tantric usages.

**jaṅghā** - leg or shank, especially the region below the knee; not simply “thigh” in all contexts.

**anvita** - connected with, accompanied by, endowed with, from *anu-* \+ √*i*, to go or follow, through a participial formation.

The compound describes something connected with the *vāmajaṅghā*.

In the transmitted line:

*vāmajaṅghānvitaḥ + jīvaḥ*

External sandhi yields:

**vāmajaṅghānvito jīvaḥ**

The final *-aḥ* becomes *-o* before the voiced initial *j*.

This is a standard Sanskrit sandhi phenomenon, not a special mantric pronunciation.

The consonant cluster **ङ्घ** in जङ्घा represents the velar nasal followed by the voiced aspirated velar stop.

Pronounce the nasal and following stop as one connected sequence.

In IAST:

**jaṅghā**, not *janga* and not *jangha* without marking the nasal if we want precise transliteration.

### Pāramparyakramāgataḥ

This is the more significant expression for our argument.

**paramparā** - succession, lineage, transmission from one to another.

**pāramparya** - belonging to a succession or transmitted tradition.

**krama** - sequence, progression, order.

**āgata** - arrived, come, received through a process, from *ā-* \+ √*gam*.

The compound thus expresses coming through a successive, transmitted order.

It does not necessarily mean that every component of the teaching can only be preserved orally.

But it does place the present mantra within a tradition of transmission.

And that's the connection to the guru verses.

The mantra has a technical structure.

The tradition claims that the structure must be understood through the proper mode of transmission.

Its significance is not reducible to reading a list of phonetic ingredients.

Now Abhinavagupta changes authority once more.

**इहापि वक्ष्यते - ihāpi vakṣyate**

“And here too it will be said…”

He begins the line:

**यथा न्यग्रोधबीजस्थः**

*yathā nyagrodhabījasthaḥ*

“Just as $$what is$$ situated within the seed of a banyan tree…”

And stops.

This anticipates the famous banyan-seed analogy later in the *Parātriṃśikā*.

We must not read forward and insert the whole later verse into the current quotation.

For now, the point is anticipatory.

The manifest world is related to the supreme heart as the potential of a great tree is related to its seed.

Not by being an externally added collection of independent pieces.

But by unfolding from a condition in which its differentiated form has not yet become manifest.

The universe in a seed.

The seed in consciousness.

And the consciousness not located in a particular point of space like a small object.

This brings us to the heart itself.


## V. The Heart of Bhairava: How a Mantra Becomes a Person and the Person Becomes Rudra

### Kashmir Series p. 243 - Gnoli p. 269

Abhinavagupta returns to his own prose:

**तदेतद्भैरवात्मनो हृदयं**

*tad etad bhairavātmano hṛdayam.*

“This, then, is the heart of the one whose nature is Bhairava.”

Pause here.

**Hṛdaya** does not simply mean the anatomical heart.

Nor is it merely a decorative synonym for compassion.

It means the central essence or innermost reality of Bhairava.

This is why the mantra is called a heart-mantra.

The heart is the locus, in a metaphysical sense, of the unity underlying all differentiated powers.

The heart of the universe is not an additional object hidden behind the universe.

It is the self-manifesting consciousness through which the universe appears at all.

But Abhinavagupta now does something extremely technical.

He interprets the passage's reference to the qualifying practitioner through different phonemic systems.

He says, in the transmitted reading:

**मालिन्यपेक्षया नकारात्**

*mālinyapekṣayā nakārāt.*

“With reference to Mālinī, from the phoneme *na*.”

And then:

**वस्तुतस्त्वकाराद्योगिन्याश्च विसर्गशक्तेर्जातः**

*vastutas tv akārād yoginyāś ca visargaśakter jātaḥ.*

“But in reality, born from the phoneme *a* and from the Yoginī, the power of visarga.”

Right.

We're back to the alphabet.

Of course we are.

The teacher has barely finished discussing initiation before Abhinavagupta decides to reinterpret the human practitioner through the origin of phonemes.

Apparently no noun is safe.

### Why Mālinī begins with na

You know from our previous readings that the **Mālinī arrangement** of Sanskrit phonemes differs from the ordinary Mātṛkā order.

The ordinary Mātṛkā arrangement begins with the vowels, headed by **a**.

The Mālinī arrangement is a distinct tantric ordering, traditionally beginning with **na**.

This is not just a different way of learning an alphabet.

Within the relevant system, the rearrangement expresses a particular configuration of divine powers.

And its initial phoneme provides the basis for a different esoteric interpretation.

So Abhinavagupta distinguishes:

- *mālinyapekṣayā*: viewed in relation to the Mālinī arrangement.
- *vastutaḥ*: in reality, in the ultimate sense.

The distinction is important.

He does not simply say that the Mālinī sequence is the only ultimately real alphabet.

He moves from a specific technical ordering to a more fundamental interpretation involving **akāra**, the initial *a*, and **visargaśakti**, the power of emission.

### Akāra - अ

The simplest Sanskrit vowel.

No consonantal articulation.

An open central or back vowel in the conventional phonetic account.

Its exact phonetic quality varies with recitational tradition and historical period.

In Abhinavagupta's metaphysics, however, *akāra* has an extraordinary symbolic status.

It belongs to the representation of *anuttara*, the unsurpassable.

And in the broader system of phonemic emission, the first vowel is associated with the original undivided ground from which the differentiated powers of speech unfold.

But be precise.

The claim is **not** that the acoustic waveform of /a/ is somehow the scientifically measurable origin of all matter.

Rather, the phoneme is used as a privileged symbolic and contemplative articulation of the supreme reality.

Its significance is doctrinal, not merely acoustic.

### Visargaśakti - विसर्गशक्ति

Split it:

**visarga** - emission, release, sending forth, from *vi-* \+ √*sṛj*, to emit or send out.

**śakti** - power, capacity, energy.

**visargaśakti** - the power of emission.

The final form **visargaśakteḥ** is genitive singular feminine.

The compound refers to the emitting or manifesting power.

This is one of the great conceptual centres of Trika.

Consciousness is not merely self-luminous.

It is intrinsically free.

That freedom includes the capacity for self-expression.

The manifest world is not added to consciousness as a wholly separate substance.

It is an expression of consciousness's own *svātantrya*.

And visarga names a central dimension of that expressive process.

The mantra is therefore not just a static representation of God.

It is interpreted through the dynamic relationship between self-awareness and manifestation.

### Yoginī - योगिनी

Here *yoginī* is not simply an ordinary female human practitioner.

In the phrase under discussion, it is associated with the power of visarga.

The word derives from *yoga*, with the feminine formation *-inī*.

Depending on context, it can refer to a female yogic practitioner, a goddess, or a class of divine powers.

In this sentence, Abhinavagupta's immediate explanation associates the Yoginī with the emitting Śakti.

The practitioner is being understood as one who has arisen through, and participates in, that power.

### The birth of Rudra

He continues:

**प्रादुर्भूतप्रमातृभावो रुद्रः**

*prādurbhūtapramātṛbhāvo rudraḥ.*

“Rudra is the one in whom the condition of being a knowing subject has become manifest.”

Here:

**prādurbhūta** - manifested, appeared, come forth.

**pramātṛ** - cognising subject, knower.

**bhāva** - state, condition, being.

The phrase connects the emergence of the knower with Rudra.

And now comes the extraordinary interpretation.

**रोधको द्रावकश्च पाशानाम्**

*rodhako drāvakaś ca pāśānām.*

“One who obstructs and dissolves the bonds.”

**rodhaka** - one who obstructs or restrains, from √*rudh*, to obstruct.

**drāvaka** - one who melts, dissolves, causes to flow, related to the causative of √*dru*, to run or flow.

**pāśa** - bond, fetter, noose.

The genitive plural **pāśānām** tells us what is being obstructed or dissolved.

Rudra is interpreted as the power that checks and dissolves bondage.

This is a tantric *nirvacana*, a meaningful doctrinal explanation.

It is not a secure historical etymology of the name Rudra.

That distinction is extremely important.

Abhinavagupta is explaining what Rudra signifies within his system.

He is not conducting modern comparative Indo-European linguistics.

### But what is a pāśa?

A bond.

A fetter.

Something that limits the apparent freedom of the subject.

In Trika, bondage is not simply a collection of external obstacles.

Its fundamental condition concerns contracted self-recognition.

The subject apprehends itself as a limited knower, separate from the full reality of its own conscious ground.

The *āṇava mala* is especially relevant: the sense of incompleteness or smallness associated with limited subjectivity.

The person thinks:

“I am only this body.”

“I am only this mind.”

“I am the one who is trapped inside experience.”

“I am a little observer confronting an independently external universe.”

This contracted identity becomes the organising principle of experience.

And because it is taken as ultimately real, the universal freedom of consciousness is not recognised.

Rudra, in this particular interpretation, is the power that dissolves that misrecognition.

The terrible god who destroys the universe has suddenly become the destroyer of the little story you keep telling yourself about what you are.

Not a bad use of divine violence.

### The decisive statement

Abhinavagupta says:

**स एव ना पुरुषः**

*sa eva nā puruṣaḥ.*

“He alone is the man, the person.”

And continues:

**एतत्स्फुटं लभते**

*etat sphuṭaṃ labhate.*

“He obtains this clearly.”

The claim concerns the qualified subject who actually attains the heart.

Not merely a person who can repeat its phonemes.

And Abhinavagupta explicitly excludes the unqualified state.

**न त्वारुद्रः**

*na tv arudraḥ.*

“But not one who is not Rudra.”

Nor, he adds, one who is not born of the Yoginī.

This is not a claim that every human practitioner must possess a particular biological ancestry.

The surrounding prose is interpreting the subject through the metaphysical power of emission and the emergence of divine subjectivity.

It concerns the relation between the human knower and the supreme Śakti.

The key is not being born into the right family.

It is the transformation of the status of the knower.

The ordinary subject apprehends a limited self.

The awakened subject recognises its identity with the supreme ground.

And Abhinavagupta calls that transformed subject Rudra.

The individual has not acquired an additional body part, magical organ, or new cosmic passport.

The underlying condition of identification has changed.

That is why the next phrase matters.


## VI. Sadyoyoga: The Most Offensive Idea in the Entire Spiritual Industry

### Kashmir Series p. 244 - Gnoli p. 269

Abhinavagupta now gives us the breathtaking conclusion:

**सद्योयोगो भैरवैकात्म्यम्**

*sadyoyogo bhairavaikātmyam.*

“Immediate union is identity with Bhairava.”

Followed by:

**स एव मोक्षो निर्णीतः**

*sa eva mokṣo nirṇītaḥ.*

“That alone has been determined to be liberation.”

There.

After all the discussions of initiation, secret mantras, bodily powers, ritual substances, phonemes, cosmic emanation, and esoteric transmission, the great Kashmir master gives us a formula that could destroy approximately ninety percent of the spiritual self-improvement industry.

Liberation is identity with Bhairava.

Not acquiring a new experience and then spending fifteen years trying to reproduce it.

Not collecting increasingly obscure mantra qualifications.

Not becoming the world's most accomplished visualiser of blue light in the left nostril.

Not mechanically repeating a syllable until some cosmic administrator finally stamps your application.

**Recognition of the identity that was always fundamental.**

But before anybody triumphantly announces that they can therefore abandon the entire path, remember the distinction we've been developing.

Abhinavagupta is explaining the ultimate character of liberation.

He is not claiming that any person who intellectually assents to the proposition “I am Śiva” has thereby achieved stable recognition.

That distinction is precisely why his philosophy contains instruction, reflection, initiation, contemplation, and different *upāyas*.

### Sadyaḥ - सद्यः

The adverb means immediately, at once, forthwith.

In the compound:

**sadyoyoga**

*sadyaḥ + yoga* undergoes sandhi to produce *sadyo-*.

The same form appeared in the root text's expression **sadyoyogavimuktidam**.

Now Abhinavagupta interprets it.

*Yoga* is related to √*yuj*, to join, connect, yoke.

But the union here is not the production of an entirely new relationship between two previously unrelated substances.

If the supreme Self is truly universal, it cannot be somewhere else awaiting connection with a separate individual substance.

That would make it spatially limited.

And spatially limited consciousness would not be the ultimate reality of Trika.

Therefore the deepest yoga must concern recognition of an identity that had become obscured through contraction.

### Bhairavaikātmyam

**भैरवैकात्म्यम्**

*Bhairava + eka + ātmya.*

The expression denotes having a single nature or identity with Bhairava.

The *eka* element is important.

It is not a vague assertion that two things are spiritually similar.

It concerns identity at the fundamental level.

But the finite empirical personality is not thereby made an omnipotent individual ego.

To understand the claim correctly, distinguish the empirical person from universal consciousness.

The empirical person is a differentiated manifestation.

Bhairava is the supreme conscious ground.

Recognition concerns their ultimate non-separation, not the unrestricted omnipotence of the autobiographical ego.

Otherwise enlightenment would mean discovering that your personal opinions are the laws of the universe.

An absolutely horrifying prospect.

Some people can barely manage a group chat without becoming tyrannical.

Do not give them the thirty-six tattvas.

### Mokṣa - मोक्ष

Related to √*muc*, to release, liberate, let go.

*Mokṣa* is liberation or release.

From what?

Not ultimately from consciousness.

Not from being.

Not from the mere fact that a world appears.

Rather, from bondage constituted by misrecognition and restriction.

The limited subject takes its limitation as its ultimate nature.

The liberated subject recognises its actual grounding in universal consciousness.

This is why a person can remain alive, perceive objects, think thoughts, and participate in ordinary activity while liberated.

The world need not vanish.

The distinction between subject and object need not be mechanically abolished.

The transformation concerns the ultimate understanding of that distinction.

### Why the text says *nirṇītaḥ*

**निर्णीतः - nirṇītaḥ**

Determined, concluded, established.

A past passive participle related to *nir-* \+ √*nī*, to lead or bring to a conclusion.

Abhinavagupta isn't introducing this as a casual possibility.

He is presenting it as the conclusion already established by the preceding argument.

And he adds that the person who receives the heart receives precisely this immediate liberating union.

Notice the movement:

The guru reveals the mantra.

The qualified subject receives it.

The mantra's heart is understood as Bhairava-consciousness.

Recognition is identity.

Identity is liberation.

That is the logical sequence.

It is not:

The guru creates divinity inside someone who was previously metaphysically devoid of it.

That would contradict the universality of consciousness.

Rather, instruction participates in the manifestation of an already fundamental reality.

**The guru does not manufacture Śiva. The guru helps make Śiva recognisable to Śiva.**

And yes, that means the entire guru-disciple relationship ultimately occurs within one consciousness.

The ultimate teacher.

The individual teacher.

The student.

The teaching.

The act of knowing.

All differentiated within one self-manifesting reality.

Which is philosophically beautiful.

And also means the supreme consciousness has apparently arranged a pedagogical situation in which it explains itself to itself while charging itself tuition.

The cosmic economy is extraordinarily confusing.


## VII. Mantra, Mudrā, and Gaṇa: Three Powers, Not Three Separate Departments

Abhinavagupta continues immediately.

The discussion now returns to the root text's **mantramudrāgaṇa**.

And he analyses each component.

### First: mantra

His prose begins:

**मन्त्रा वर्णभट्टारका**

*mantrā varṇabhaṭṭārakāḥ.*

“The mantras are the venerable phoneme-lords.”

**varṇa** - phonemic unit.

**bhaṭṭāraka** - venerable lord, exalted master, an honorific term especially familiar in Kashmir Śaiva religious vocabulary.

The compound expresses the divine significance assigned to phonemes in this system.

The sounds are not merely arbitrary acoustic fragments.

They are understood as manifestations of powers.

This does not entail that every ordinary spoken word functions as an initiated mantra.

That distinction has already been established.

But it does mean that a mantra's phonemic character cannot be separated from its status as a form of consciousness.

Abhinavagupta continues by describing them as having both ordinary and specifically divine forms.

The key contrast is:

**laukika** - worldly, belonging to ordinary usage.

**pārameśvara** - belonging to the supreme Lord.

The same domain of linguistic expression can be considered under different orders of significance.

Ordinary words operate through conventional communication.

Mantras are treated through their relationship to the powers of the deity and the initiatory system.

But their deepest ground is the same consciousness.

### The functional explanation of mantra

He associates mantra with:

**मननत्राणरूपाः**

*mananatrāṇarūpāḥ.*

“Having the nature of reflection and protection.”

**manana** - reflection, thinking, mental consideration, from √*man*, to think.

**trāṇa** - protection, preservation, rescue, from √*trai*, to protect.

This is a traditional doctrinal explanation of *mantra* through the functions of contemplation and protection.

Do not mistake it for an exhaustive historical etymology.

Its significance is interpretive.

A mantra becomes meaningful not merely through repetition but through the way it participates in cognition and transformation.

The root passage has already asserted the potency of the supreme heart-mantra.

Abhinavagupta is now explaining how the powers of mantra and awareness relate.

### Another important textual difference

The GRETIL transcription continues with:

**अविकल्पसंविन्मयाः**

*avikalpasaṃvinmayāḥ.*

“Consisting of non-conceptual consciousness.”

The digital KSTS transcription instead presents:

**विकल्पसंविन्मयाः**

*vikalpasaṃvinmayāḥ.*

“Consisting of conceptual consciousness.”

This is not a negligible difference.

The negative prefix **a-** changes the doctrinal significance of the phrase.

The digital witnesses diverge, and the precise manuscript basis of this particular difference is not established by the accessible apparatus. It should therefore remain an unresolved textual difference, not be silently harmonised. ; \]

Why is this so interesting?

Because the preceding argument has been concerned precisely with the relationship between conceptual differentiation and the deeper unity of consciousness.

A mantra can be an articulated phonemic sequence.

That sequence can be held in thought.

But its supreme reality is not reducible to conceptual determination.

The GRETIL reading makes the nonconceptual grounding explicit.

The KSTS electronic reading foregrounds conceptual consciousness.

Within the larger Trika system, both dimensions of language matter, but that does not mean the transmitted words are interchangeable.

For a critical reading, the negative prefix must remain visible.

### Second: mudrā

Abhinavagupta says:

**मुद्राश्च सकलकरचरणादिकरणव्यापारमय्यः क्रियाशक्तिरूपाः**

*mudrāś ca sakalakaracaraṇādikaraṇavyāpāramayyaḥ kriyāśaktirūpāḥ.*

“And the mudrās consist of the activities of all the instruments, such as hands and feet; they are forms of the power of action.”

This is astonishing.

Because the ordinary picture of a mudrā is a particular hand gesture.

A special arrangement of fingers.

Perhaps a ritual gesture performed during worship.

But here Abhinavagupta expands the interpretation.

**sakala** - all, entire.

**kara** - hand.

**caraṇa** - foot, or an act of moving.

**ādi** - and so forth.

**karaṇa** - an instrument or means of action.

**vyāpāra** - operation, activity, functioning.

**-maya** - consisting of, made up of, pervaded by.

**kriyāśakti** - the power of action.

Mudrā is being interpreted through the whole field of embodied activity.

That does not abolish specific ritual mudrās.

It reveals a wider sense of the term.

If consciousness is ultimately the agent, then the body's activities can be understood as differentiated operations of its power.

Moving a hand.

Walking.

Turning the head.

Speaking.

Grasping an object.

These are not necessarily independent powers belonging to a metaphysically self-sufficient body.

They are modes of action appearing within the conscious manifestation.

Again, we must not collapse the distinction between metaphysical interpretation and biomechanics.

The muscles, nervous system, and bodily processes remain perfectly appropriate objects of physiological explanation.

Abhinavagupta is investigating their status within a theory of universal conscious agency.

### Third: gaṇa

**तत्कृतो गणः समूहात्मा परशक्त्येकरूपः**

*tatkṛto gaṇaḥ samūhātmā paraśaktyekarūpaḥ.*

“The group constituted by these has the nature of an aggregate and is of one nature with the supreme Śakti.”

**gaṇa** - group, company, multitude, assemblage.

**samūha** - aggregate or collection.

**paraśakti** - supreme power.

**ekarūpa** - possessing one form or nature.

The multitude of powers is not independent of the ultimate Śakti.

The individual components of ritual and consciousness are gathered within one encompassing reality.

This is the same metaphysical movement we saw in the analysis of the alphabet.

A sound can be understood as a specific manifestation of the expressive totality.

An action can be understood as a differentiated manifestation of the power of action.

A collection of divine powers can be understood as a group that remains grounded in one supreme Śakti.

And now the root text says that the mantramudrāgaṇa becomes oriented toward the practitioner.

But the really significant expression is:

**स्वदेहावेशलक्षणम् - svadehāveśalakṣaṇam**

Characterised by entry or possession of one's own body.

What the fuck does that mean?


## VIII. Āveśa: When the Limited Subject Stops Pretending to Be a Rock

### Kashmir Series pp. 244-245 - Gnoli pp. 269-270

Abhinavagupta explains *āveśa* through an intensely compressed sequence.

The central phrase is:

**झटिति परस्वरूपानुप्रवेशेन**

*jhaṭiti parasvarūpānupraveśena.*

“Through sudden entry into the supreme nature.”

Then:

**पारतन्त्र्यात्मकजडतातिरोधानेन**

*pāratantryātmakajaḍatātirodhānena.*

“Through the concealment of inertness characterised by dependence.”

And finally:

**स्वतन्त्रकर्तृतानुविद्धप्रमातृतोदयः**

*svatantrakartṛtānuviddhapramātṛtodayaḥ.*

“The arising of a knowing subject permeated by independent agency.”

This is one of the most philosophically important sentences in the present instalment. ; \]

Let's take it apart.

### Jhaṭiti - झटिति

Suddenly.

Immediately.

In a flash.

Not eventually, after the post office has processed your application.

The word is often associated with suddenness or immediacy.

It reinforces the meaning of *sadyaḥ*.

The transformation is presented as a direct opening into the supreme condition.

But the question is what transforms.

The body does not literally disappear.

The senses need not shut down.

The empirical memories need not be erased.

The change concerns the subject's participation in, and recognition of, its supreme nature.

### Para-svarūpa-anupraveśa

**para** - supreme, transcendent, highest.

**svarūpa** - own nature, intrinsic form.

**anupraveśa** - entry, penetration, entering into.

The instrumental ending **-ena** marks the means.

So:

“By entering into the supreme intrinsic nature.”

Again we encounter a paradox.

If the supreme nature is already the ultimate reality of the subject, why does the text speak of entering it?

Because the language of entry is pedagogical.

It describes a transformation in apprehension.

The subject does not travel through space to a remote consciousness.

Consciousness is not located fifteen kilometres beyond the Sahasrāra, awaiting the arrival of an especially enthusiastic meditator.

The movement is a change in the mode of self-recognition.

One may speak of entering the heart because the limited subject's habitual identification gives way to a deeper apprehension.

But the heart is not elsewhere.

### Pāratantrya - परतन्त्र्य

Dependence.

Subjection to another.

Being governed from outside one's own power.

The compound here includes:

**pāratantrya-ātmaka**

“Having the nature of dependence.”

Then:

**jaḍatā**

Inertness, insentience, the condition of being *jaḍa*.

And:

**tirodhāna**

Concealment, veiling, obscuration.

The resulting idea is the concealment of a mode of inertness or dependence.

Be careful with the logic.

Abhinavagupta is not saying that the physical body is empirically inert in every respect.

Nor is he claiming that enlightenment causes the person to become physically indestructible.

He is describing the condition in which the subject identifies with restricted, dependent, object-like existence.

The body is experienced.

The mind is experienced.

Sensory events are experienced.

But the limited subject identifies itself with these conditioned forms and fails to recognise the universal consciousness in which they appear.

This is the philosophical target.

### Svatantrakartṛtā - स्वतन्त्रकर्तृता

Now the opposite appears.

**svatantra** - independent, self-governing, free.

**kartṛtā** - the condition of being an agent or doer.

The noun is built from *kartṛ*, agent, connected with √*kṛ*, to do.

**anuviddha** - permeated, penetrated, interwoven.

**pramātṛ** - knower, cognising subject.

**udaya** - arising, emergence.

Combine them:

The arising of subjectivity permeated by free agency.

And this is astonishingly different from a model of liberation in which the person becomes absolutely passive.

Abhinavagupta is not celebrating an insentient void.

He is describing the manifestation of conscious freedom.

The subject does not disappear into the condition of a stone.

Indeed, his prose explicitly contrasts the awakened condition with a merely inert or stone-like state.

This is a crucial Trika distinction.

### The difference between stillness and freedom

Suppose you meditate until thoughts become very quiet.

Wonderful.

That may be a valuable contemplative condition.

But does the absence of thought by itself establish recognition?

No.

An inert object also does not appear to engage in conceptual thought.

The mere absence of conceptual activity is not equivalent to divine self-awareness.

Trika consistently refuses to identify liberation with unconsciousness.

The important issue is the self-revealing character of consciousness.

The subject is not liberated because it has successfully switched itself off.

It is liberated through recognition of what its conscious nature actually is.

This makes sense of the earlier assertion that conceptual thoughts may continue after recognition.

The liberated person can think.

Can perceive.

Can act.

Can remember.

The problem was never that thoughts occurred.

The problem was misrecognising the nature of the subject to whom they occurred.

And now Abhinavagupta connects this awakening with a rather unexpected word.

**Svadā.**

### The strange derivation of svadā

He explains:

**स्वं स्वभावं पदार्थस्य ददातीति स्वदा**

*svaṃ svabhāvaṃ padārthasya dadātīti svadā.*

“It is called *svadā* because it gives a thing its own nature.”

Notice the structure.

**svam** - one's own.

**svabhāvam** - intrinsic nature, accusative singular.

**padārthasya** - of a thing or meaningful object, genitive singular.

**dadāti** - gives, present third-person singular of √*dā*.

**iti** - marks the explanation.

This is another doctrinal *nirvacana*.

Abhinavagupta is unpacking the syllables and their meanings within the text's interpretive framework.

He is not asserting that all uses of the relevant Sanskrit sound-sequence must historically derive from this philosophical sentence.

The claim is that the divine power allows things to manifest in their own nature.

That includes their differentiated natures.

The point is not to eliminate differentiation.

It is to understand differentiation as arising within conscious freedom.

Then he associates **īhā** with the progression from desire through action.

**ईहा - īhā**

Effort, striving, desire, purposive activity.

From √*īh*, to seek, endeavour, or strive.

The sequence of powers from *icchā* toward *kriyā* is thereby brought into the interpretation.

And so the body, which appeared merely to be a limited object, becomes intelligible as a field in which the powers of consciousness operate.

The practitioner doesn't need to become an abstract entity detached from life.

The problem is the misidentification of the living subject with an ultimately dependent and inert condition.

Now comes the next absurdity.

Abhinavagupta begins explaining the word “immediately” through the Sanskrit word for “being.”

Because, apparently, the concept of instantaneous awakening requires a detour through the ontology of existence.


## IX. The Word *Sadyaḥ* Contains a Clue to *Sat*: Immediate Awakening and the Reality of Being

### Kashmir Series p. 245 - Gnoli p. 270

The next sentence begins:

**सद्य इत्यनेन सत् इत्यप्यनुप्रवेशः सूच्यते**

*sadya ity anena sat ity apy anupraveśaḥ sūcyate.*

“By the expression *sadya*, entry into *sat* is also indicated.”

This is an interpretive association.

It is not a claim that *sadyaḥ* is historically derived from *sat* according to ordinary linguistic rules.

Here **sat** means being, reality, what is.

We already studied this in the previous chapter.

It is connected with √*as*, to be.

Abhinavagupta draws out the deeper significance of immediate transformation as entry into the reality of being.

The transformation is not a progression toward a distant object.

It is an unveiling of the ground that makes every present experience possible.

### Tanmukhatām eti

He then explains the root text's phrase about the mantramudrāgaṇa becoming oriented toward the practitioner.

**तन्मुखतामेति**

*tanmukhatām eti.*

“It comes to face that,” or “becomes oriented toward that.”

The substantive **mukha** means face, mouth, opening, entrance, or direction.

The abstract **-tā** gives the condition of being oriented toward something.

And **eti**, from √*i*, means “goes,” “comes,” or “enters into a condition.”

The passage explains that the powers become oriented toward their supreme nature.

Contrast this with the unawakened subject, in whom that nature is concealed.

Again, the issue is not necessarily that objectively separate beings literally turn around in physical space.

The commentary is interpreting the orientation of the powers of consciousness.

### The ordinary person and the awakened person

The limited subject becomes absorbed in the object.

The awakened subject recognises the conscious ground of the object and the act of apprehension.

Both can perceive exactly the same external scene.

Both can speak.

Both can remember.

Both can act.

But the meaning of the experience differs.

Take the simple act of seeing a tree.

Ordinarily:

“I am here. The tree is there. I am a little consciousness inside this body looking at an external object.”

In the Pratyabhijñā analysis, the tree's appearance and the limited subject's apprehension both arise within consciousness.

Recognition does not require denying the tree's differentiated appearance.

It requires understanding the relation between the tree, its manifestness, and the ultimate subject.

The empirical distinction between observer and tree continues to function.

The metaphysical assumption of absolute separation is no longer taken as ultimate.

That is the kind of change Abhinavagupta is explaining.

But then he reaches the word **muhūrta**.

A short period of time.

And the discussion takes a distinctly unsettling turn.


## X. Muhūrta: How Long Does Recognition Take If Consciousness Isn't in Time?

### Kashmir Series pp. 245-246 - Gnoli p. 270

Abhinavagupta writes:

**अत एव मुहूर्तम्**

*ata eva muhūrtam.*

“Therefore, a moment.”

But what does *muhūrta* mean?

Ordinarily it refers to a division of time, conventionally one-thirtieth of a day and night, or forty-eight minutes in the standard reckoning.

And yet Abhinavagupta immediately qualifies it:

**अकालकलितत्वेऽपि परकलनापेक्षया उन्मेषमात्रम्**

*akālakalitatve 'pi parakalanāpekṣayā unmeṣamātram.*

“Although it is not delimited by time, relative to another mode of reckoning it is merely an opening or flash.”

Now the metaphysical problem becomes serious.

If consciousness is the ground of time, it cannot itself be an ordinary object contained within time.

Yet recognition occurs in the experience of a person who appears to live through temporal succession.

One moment the person fails to recognise their true nature.

Another moment recognition occurs.

How can something timeless be recognised at a particular time?

**Because the temporal occurrence of recognition is not identical with the temporal production of the reality recognised.**

This distinction is fundamental.

Suppose you fail to recognise someone you know.

You see them across the street.

You stare.

You wonder who they are.

Suddenly:

“Oh! It's my old friend!”

The recognition occurs at a particular time.

But your friend did not begin existing at the moment you recognised them.

The event of recognition is temporally located.

The recognised identity is not produced by that event.

This is an analogy, not a complete account of liberation.

But it helps clarify the logic.

The ultimate Self does not begin existing when the practitioner recognises it.

The recognition occurs within a particular experiential condition.

Its object-or more accurately, the reality disclosed by the recognition-is not manufactured at that moment.

### Akālakalita

Break the compound.

**a-** - negation.

**kāla** - time.

**kalita** - reckoned, measured, constructed, determined; related to √*kal*, to count or calculate.

**akālakalita** - not determined by time.

The locative **akālakalitatve** means “in the condition of not being temporally determined.”

**api** introduces the concession: even though.

Then:

**parakalanāpekṣayā**

*para-kalanā-apekṣayā.*

“With reference to another reckoning.”

**apekṣā** means reference to, dependence on, or consideration of something.

This is an instrumental singular.

The point is that temporal language is being used relative to a particular perspective.

From the level of ordinary activity, recognition may be described as occurring instantly.

From the standpoint of supreme consciousness, that reality is not divided into before and after.

### Unmeṣa - उन्मेष

An opening.

A blossoming.

The opening of an eye.

A sudden emergence.

Related to *ud-* \+ √*miṣ*, to open the eyes, in the relevant verbal family.

You remember the opening verse of the *Spandakārikā*:

**यस्योन्मेषनिमेषाभ्यां**

*yasyonmeṣanimeṣābhyām.*

“By whose opening and closing…”

The oscillation between *unmeṣa* and *nimeṣa* is used to describe the emergence and dissolution of manifestation.

But the supreme consciousness is not simply another event inside that oscillation.

It is the ground through which emergence and withdrawal become possible.

So a moment of recognition can be called *unmeṣa*.

An opening.

A flash.

Not because the infinite has travelled through time.

But because the limited subject's apprehension has opened into what was already fundamental.

### The difference between metaphysical timelessness and distorted time perception

This is worth stressing.

A profound meditative experience can involve an altered sense of time.

An hour may seem short.

A moment may seem extraordinarily expansive.

But those phenomenological changes are not by themselves proof of a timeless metaphysical absolute.

Abhinavagupta's argument is philosophical.

Consciousness, conceived as the ultimate condition of temporal manifestation, is not itself treated as merely one more temporally delimited object.

Experiences of altered time may be interesting.

They do not automatically establish that metaphysical claim.

Now he turns to **smaraṇa**, remembering.

And the next section of the commentary becomes an extraordinarily sophisticated investigation of memory, identity, and the relation between the past and present.


## XI. The Cosmic Magnet: How Memory Reassembles the Entire Universe Without Moving Anything

Abhinavagupta explains the phrase about the practitioner who remembers for a moment.

**यः स्मरत्यनुसंधत्ते**

*yaḥ smaraty anusaṃdhatte.*

“The one who remembers, who connects or integrates.”

The two verbs are worth distinguishing.

**smarati** - remembers, from √*smṛ*, to remember.

**anusaṃdhatte** - connects, joins, brings into relation, traces or integrates, from *anu-* \+ *sam-* \+ √*dhā*, to place or put.

The second verb is especially important.

This is not just retrieval of a memory-image.

It concerns the integration of experience.

The subject recognises continuity through changing mental states.

An experience appears.

It disappears.

A memory arises.

The person identifies that memory as referring to a previous experience.

How is such recognition possible?

In Pratyabhijñā, memory plays a major role in the argument that consciousness is not simply a succession of isolated, mutually inaccessible events.

The recognition “I saw that before” presupposes a unity of subjectivity through which the earlier and later cognitive moments can be related.

Abhinavagupta now puts that continuity to work.

### The mantramudrāgaṇa is bound together

He writes that the practitioner:

**स्वात्मन्येकीकरोत्यद्वयतः**

*svātmany ekīkaroty advayataḥ.*

“Makes them one within their own Self, nondually.”

Break it.

**sva-ātmani** - within one's own Self, locative singular.

**ekīkaroti** - makes one, unifies.

**advayataḥ** - nondually, without duality.

The practitioner does not merely assemble a committee of separate supernatural entities.

The many mantra and mudrā powers are recognised as belonging within the unity of consciousness.

The differentiated powers do not cease to exist as differentiations.

But their ultimate non-separation is understood.

### And then comes the magnet

Abhinavagupta asks:

**कथम् - katham?**

“How?”

His answer involves:

**चुम्बकेन विश्वस्पर्शकेन शाक्तेन रूपेण**

*cumbakena viśvasparśakena śāktena rūpeṇa.*

“Through the magnetic, universally touching, Śākta form.”

*Where on earth did a magnet come from?*

We've spent hundreds of pages in the metaphysics of Sanskrit phonemes.

Suddenly the universe is being discussed as if somebody has dropped a giant cosmic magnet into the middle of consciousness.

But the image is brilliant.

**cumbaka** - magnet, or something that attracts.

**viśva** - the universe, everything.

**sparśa** - touch, contact.

**viśvasparśaka** - touching everything, universally contacting.

**śākta** - belonging to Śakti, constituted by or related to power.

The meaning is not that a physical magnetic field literally pulls the thirty-six tattvas into one location.

The image concerns the power through which differentiated manifestations can be drawn into the recognition of their common ground.

### The philosophical problem of unity

Consider ordinary perception.

You see a cup.

You feel its warmth.

You smell the tea.

You remember where you bought it.

You anticipate its taste.

These are different modes of cognition.

Vision.

Touch.

Smell.

Memory.

Expectation.

Yet they can all be apprehended as belonging to one experience.

The fact that they can be integrated is philosophically significant.

Abhinavagupta's universal claim is more ambitious.

He is not merely explaining multisensory integration.

He is arguing that all manifest powers are grounded in one consciousness.

The magnet image describes the recognition of that underlying unity.

Nothing needs to be physically transported into the Self.

The manifestations were never outside consciousness in the first place.

The transformation is one of recognition and integration.

### Abhimudrita - अभिमुद्रित

The root text speaks of being marked or sealed by the *cumbaka*.

Abhinavagupta explains this through the action of *mudraṇa*, sealing or impressing.

The compound:

**abhi-mudrita**

Means thoroughly marked, sealed, impressed.

Connected with the verbal root √*mudr*, to seal or stamp, in the relevant lexical usage.

The bodily ritual gesture is now inseparable from a more comprehensive metaphysical act of sealing all differentiated powers within awareness.

The person is not simply performing a gesture.

They are interpreting the relation between activity and the supreme Śakti.

### The famous little particle tu

Abhinavagupta even pauses to explain **tu**.

He gives its force as:

**अवधारणे - avadhāraṇe**

“For restriction, determination, or emphasis.”

The particle serves to clarify exactly whom the passage describes.

The one who is truly oriented toward the Śākta vibration and the supreme nature acts in this way.

Not simply any person or inert object.

This matters because the text's promises of powers must be interpreted in relation to the status of the subject.

A mantra is not a universal machine that produces identical results regardless of understanding or context.

The practitioner is described as one in whom a transformation of subjectivity has occurred.

Now, with the entire mantramudrāgaṇa unified, the root text makes a somewhat alarming claim.

The practitioner can answer questions about past and future matters.

And Abhinavagupta is about to bring time itself into the discussion.


## XII. Knowing the Past and Future: What Is Actually Being Claimed?

### Kashmir Series p. 246 - Gnoli p. 270

The root text says:

**अतीतानागतानर्थान् पृष्टोऽसौ कथयत्यपि**

*atītānāgatān arthān pṛṣṭo 'sau kathayaty api.*

“When questioned, he also relates matters past and future.”

This is a traditional claim about extraordinary knowledge.

It should be reported as such.

It is not independent evidence that tantric practitioners can accurately predict future events.

But Abhinavagupta's explanation is philosophically interesting.

He asks how the relation between questioning, knowing, and manifestation is to be understood.

The text includes the expression:

**यदेव किल ज्ञीप्सति तत्तदेवान्तर्गतं बहिष्कुरुते**

*yad eva kila jñīpsati tat tad evāntargataṃ bahiṣkurute.*

“Whatever he seeks to know, that very thing, already within, he brings outward.”

This follows a short, difficult passage about the person being questioned and the desire to know. Its quotation boundaries are not accompanied by a securely identified independent author in the GRETIL transcription, so I will not assign it to a named scripture. ; \]

### Jñīpsati

**ज्ञीप्सति - jñīpsati**

A desiderative form associated with √*jñā*, to know.

It expresses the wish or desire to know.

This is not merely the ordinary present *jānāti*, “he knows.”

It signifies an orientation toward knowing.

The distinction matters because the passage is investigating the relation between intention and manifestation.

Something is sought.

An answer emerges.

And the extraordinary claim is that the knowledge was in some sense already contained within consciousness.

### Antargataṃ bahiṣkurute

**antar-gata** - gone within, situated inside.

**bahiṣ** - outwardly, outside.

**kurute** - makes, does, performs, middle-voice present of √*kṛ*.

The phrase means something like “makes outward what is contained within.”

Now the metaphysical interpretation.

If the universal consciousness contains the differentiated universe as its own manifestation, then the distinction between an internally available reality and its external disclosure becomes significant.

Abhinavagupta is not saying that a limited individual's ordinary autobiographical memory literally contains every historical fact.

The deeper claim concerns the relation between the individual and universal consciousness.

But the text does make an extraordinary assertion about the possible result of such recognition.

We must distinguish the philosophical framework from the empirical claim of paranormal knowledge.

The philosophical framework can be analysed.

The paranormal capacity would require evidence beyond the authority of the text to establish as fact.

### Why the root text says “when asked”

The participle **pṛṣṭaḥ** means “asked,” “questioned,” from √*prach*, to ask.

The distinction between knowledge appearing unprompted and knowledge appearing in response to a question matters in the ritual framework.

The practitioner is not simply described as permanently and consciously reciting the entire contents of the universe.

The question provides the occasion for manifestation.

This corresponds to the wider theme of **icchā**, intention or will.

The conscious power is oriented toward a particular disclosure.

And Abhinavagupta invokes the *Spandakārikā*.


## XIII. The Spandakārikā Enters: Sun, Moon, Dream, and the Manifestation of Desire

### Authentic citation: Spandakārikā 3.1

The verse reads:

**यथेच्छाभ्यर्थितो धाता जाग्रतोऽर्थान् हृदि स्थितान् ।**

*yathecchābhyarthito dhātā jāgrato 'rthān hṛdi sthitān.*

**सोमसूर्योदयं कृत्वा सम्पादयति देहिनः ॥**

*somasūryodayaṃ kṛtvā saṃpādayati dehinaḥ.*

My literal translation:

“Just as the sustaining power, invoked according to desire, brings into manifestation the objects residing in the heart of a waking embodied being, having brought about the rising of moon and sun…”

The sentence continues into the following *Spandakārikā* verse, which makes the comparison with dreaming explicit.

The KSTS digital transcription appears to have a malformed *soma-sūrya* form; both GRETIL and the independent *Spandakārikā* witnesses support **somasūryodayaṃ**. ; ; \]

Now, let's not pretend that this is a straightforward statement about the astronomical sun and moon physically rearranging themselves in response to someone's wish.

The Spanda tradition interprets solar and lunar imagery through powers such as knowledge and action.

The imagery participates in a technical contemplative framework.

### Yathecchābhyarthito

Break the compound.

**yathā** - according to, just as.

**icchā** - will, desire, intention.

**abhyarthitaḥ** - requested, solicited, invoked.

The participle derives from *abhi-* \+ √*arth*, to request or seek.

The sandhi creates a beautifully compact expression:

*yathā + icchā + abhyarthitaḥ → yathecchābhyarthitaḥ.*

The *ā + i* combination gives *e*.

The meeting of *icchā* and *abhyarthitaḥ* produces the extended *ābhy-* sequence in the transmitted form.

The meaning concerns a power invoked according to desire.

**Dhātā - धाता**

The sustainer, arranger, or creator, from √*dhā*, to place, establish, or support.

**jāgrataḥ** - of the one awake, genitive singular of the present participle of √*jāgṛ*, to be awake.

**arthān** - objects or meaningful contents, accusative plural.

**hṛdi** - in the heart, locative singular.

**sthitān** - situated, established, from √*sthā*, to stand.

**sampādayati** - brings about, accomplishes, produces; causative formation of *sam-* \+ √*pad*, to attain or arrive.

The grammar is a study in the manifestation of an already inwardly present possibility.

The *dhātā* brings forth the contents residing in the heart.

### The role of knowledge and action

The Spanda tradition does not treat manifestation as a mere act of passive observation.

The power of cognition and the power of action are intimately involved.

An object is manifest.

It is apprehended.

It can become the object of intention.

And activity can arise in relation to it.

Abhinavagupta's Trika system develops this through the powers of *icchā*, *jñāna*, and *kriyā*.

We can formulate the problem as follows.

A person intends something.

There is a content of intention.

That content is not yet an accomplished external result.

Action may then bring about some state of affairs.

At the level of everyday life, the process depends on causes, bodily capacities, circumstances, and environmental constraints.

At the metaphysical level, Abhinavagupta understands cognition, intention, and action as differentiated expressions of consciousness.

The extraordinary tantric claim concerns a practitioner whose recognition of that power is so complete that unusual results become possible.

That claim is part of the tradition's account of siddhi.

It is not an established causal theory of supernatural manifestation.

### Now the metre

The verse is anuṣṭubh.

| Pāda | Syllables | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *yathecchābhyarthito dhātā* | ya-thec-chā-bhyar-thi-to-dhā-tā | 8 |
| *jāgrato 'rthān hṛdi sthitān* | jā-gra-to-'rthān-hṛ-di-sthi-tān | 8 |
| *somasūryodayaṃ kṛtvā* | so-ma-sūr-yo-da-yaṃ-kṛt-vā | 8 |
| *sampādayati dehinaḥ* | sam-pā-da-ya-ti-de-hi-naḥ | 8 |

The syllable containing *hṛ* is a genuine Sanskrit syllable.

The vowel ṛ is not an ordinary consonantal *r* followed by a vowel.

And in **कृत्वा**, the group त्व represents त् + व; the vowel ऋ belongs to कृ.

In **सूर्योदयम्**, the sequence र्यो represents र् + य with the following vowel *o*.

The metre works through the phonological units.

### What does this mean philosophically?

The commentary's concern is the relationship between the internally held form of experience and its manifestation.

Dreaming provides a particularly useful example.

In a dream, a vast world can appear.

Places.

People.

Events.

Time.

Distances.

A sense of history.

A sense of what comes next.

Yet the entire dream-world is presented within the condition of dreaming consciousness.

The dreamer may not know that they are dreaming.

The dream-world appears externally situated relative to the dream-person.

Only later may the subject recognise that the apparently external dream-world was manifest through the dreaming process.

This makes dreaming a powerful analogy for examining the status of appearance.

But the analogy must be used carefully.

Trika does not merely assert that the waking universe is literally identical to an individual's ordinary dream.

Its claim concerns the universal conscious ground, not merely the subjective dream-production of an empirical person.

Still, dreaming reveals that the *appearance of externality* is not as straightforward as naive realism might suggest.

And Abhinavagupta uses the example to push us toward the question of temporal manifestation.

Now he says something that is almost offensively radical.

The past and future do not exist for the supreme subject in the way they exist for the limited knower.


## XIV. There Is Only the Present, but Not in the Motivational-Poster Sense

### Kashmir Series pp. 246-247 - Gnoli pp. 270-271

Abhinavagupta writes:

**एको ह्यसौ स्मरणोत्प्रेक्षणादावपि तावानेव वर्तमानः**

*eko hy asau smaraṇotprekṣaṇādāv api tāvān eva vartamānaḥ.*

“He is one, remaining the same even in remembering and imagining.”

This is the crucial assertion.

The subject is not divided into separate subjects by the emergence of memories and anticipations.

The cognitive contents change.

The modes of temporal presentation change.

But the fundamental consciousness is not thereby divided into numerically independent realities.

Let's examine the terms.

**smaraṇa** - remembering.

**utprekṣaṇa** - imagining, conjecturing, anticipating, mentally projecting.

**vartamāna** - present, existing, occurring.

The participial formation is related to √*vṛt*, to occur, proceed, exist, or turn.

Abhinavagupta then makes the temporal point explicit.

The supreme subject is not itself constituted as an independent past subject and future subject.

The expressions of past and future arise within the power of temporal differentiation.

### The problem of memory

Consider an experience you had five years ago.

You remember it now.

Where is the original experience?

It is not occurring again as the same historical event.

But a present memory can refer to it.

Now consider an anticipated experience.

You imagine something that might occur next year.

That future event is not presently realised as an actual event.

Yet it is presently imagined.

Both memory and anticipation are occurring now as acts of consciousness.

This does not prove that the actual past and future are metaphysically unreal in every possible sense.

But it reveals that their experienced presentation is always mediated through present conscious activity.

Abhinavagupta's more radical thesis is that time itself is an expression of the supreme subject's freedom.

The subject is not ultimately imprisoned within the order it manifests.

### The quotation about time

He briefly invokes:

**कालोभयापरिच्छिन्नम्**

*kālobhayāparicchinnam.*

“Unbounded by the two aspects of time.”

The quotation is truncated in the *Parātriṃśikāvivaraṇa*.

GRETIL notes possible relationships to other tantric sources, including a citation in the *Mālinīvijayavārttika*, but the source identification is not secure enough to supply the missing passage as if it were quoted here.

We should leave it as a fragment.

Its immediate relevance is clear: the supreme reality is not limited by the two temporal directions.

### Sakṛdvibhātatvam

The commentary develops the idea of:

**सकृद्विभातत्वम् - sakṛdvibhātatvam**

The condition of shining forth all at once.

**sakṛt** - once, at one time, simultaneously in this contextual philosophical usage.

**vibhāta** - manifested, shone forth.

**-tva** - abstract condition.

This belongs to the characteristic Trika account of consciousness as fundamentally undivided despite the successive manifestation of differentiated appearances.

An analogy may help.

Suppose you read a sentence.

The words appear in sequence.

You do not encounter all the phonetic events at exactly the same instant.

Yet the completed meaning can be apprehended as a coherent unity.

The temporal succession of phonemes does not necessarily entail that the meaningful whole is merely an externally assembled pile of independent units.

Similarly, Abhinavagupta argues that the successive appearance of temporal events does not force the conclusion that consciousness itself is exhausted by that succession.

The comparison to linguistic meaning is especially appropriate given the preceding discussion of Bhartṛhari.

### The dream quotation

Abhinavagupta then introduces a short verse describing how, within a short interval of waking time, dreamers may experience astonishingly varied spans of time.

The GRETIL reading begins:

**किं च जाग्रति कस्मिंश्चिद्घटिकाभिमतापि या**

*kiṃ ca jāgrati kasmiṃścid ghaṭikābhimatāpi yā.*

“And further, what is reckoned as a *ghaṭikā* in some waking condition…”

It continues by describing dreaming subjects who experience varieties of days, watches, years, and other spans.

The KSTS digital transcription has a different expression for part of the sequence, and its apparatus reports an alternative reading concerning the verbal form in the concluding portion. The larger point is stable: dream duration and experienced duration can diverge. ; \]

Now the philosophical significance.

The relation between objective clock time and experienced time is not simple.

Dreams can contain apparent narratives, remembered histories, and experienced durations that are not straightforwardly identical with the elapsed time of sleep.

But we should not make the exaggerated claim that neuroscience has demonstrated a dreamer can literally experience a thousand years during one second.

The passage is a philosophical use of the phenomenon of temporal appearance.

It is not a modern empirical measurement of dream duration.

The key distinction is between:

**The order in which events are represented in consciousness and the clock time during which those representations occur.**

And Abhinavagupta uses that distinction to argue that time cannot be treated as an entirely independent container within which consciousness is forced to exist.

The metaphysical claim is that the power of time arises within consciousness.

This is why the divine subject is described as manifesting *kālaśakti*.

The power of temporal differentiation.

And that prepares the next astonishing move.

Abhinavagupta returns to the root verse's phrase about perceiving a deity-form.

But instead of simply telling us that the practitioner sees a god, he begins dissecting the Sanskrit word **prahara**.

The man is entirely incapable of letting a word remain a word.


## XV. Prahara: Abhinavagupta Disassembles a Word and Finds the Entire Process of Perception Inside

### Kashmir Series p. 247 - Gnoli p. 271

The root text contains:

**प्रहराद्यदभिप्रेतं देवतारूपमुच्चरन्**

*praharād yad abhipretaṃ devatārūpam uccaran.*

This concerns the manifestation or perception of an intended divine form, in the context of a period expressed through *prahara*.

A **prahara** ordinarily denotes a watch or division of the day and night, commonly about three hours in a system of eight watches.

But Abhinavagupta now gives a doctrinal reinterpretation.

He begins:

**प्रकृष्टो हरः संहारोऽकुलाख्यः**

*prakṛṣṭo haraḥ saṃhāro 'kulākhyaḥ.*

“The supreme withdrawal, called Akula.”

This is not an ordinary dictionary derivation of *prahara*.

It is a tantric semantic decomposition.

**pra-** can convey prominence, intensity, forward movement, or excellence.

**hara** can be related to taking away, removing, or destroying, connected with √*hṛ*.

**saṃhāra** - withdrawal, contraction, dissolution, reabsorption.

**Akula** - the supreme principle beyond differentiated *kula*, in the relevant nondual Kaula vocabulary.

Thus the word *prahara* becomes an occasion for describing the withdrawal of differentiated experience into its undivided ground.

The ordinary word denotes a division of time.

The tantric interpretation turns it into a structure of cosmic and cognitive manifestation.

This is precisely the kind of *nirvacana* that Part XXIX taught us not to apply arbitrarily to every word in ordinary language.

Abhinavagupta is interpreting a particular scriptural term within a specific metaphysical framework.

### What comes after withdrawal?

The text proceeds through another series of associations.

The intended form, *abhipreta*, is related to the emergence of the deity's powers.

**abhipreta** - desired, intended, aimed at.

**rūpa** - form, appearance, configuration.

**devatā** - deity, divine power.

The commentary links this with *icchāśakti*, the power of will, and *jñānaśakti*, the power of cognition.

The crucial movement is:

The supreme condition is undivided.

Within it arises intention.

The intended form begins to become cognitively differentiated.

Then it becomes a manifest object of perception.

This is not a claim that any arbitrary fantasy instantly materialises.

It is an interpretation of the stages by which experience becomes differentiated.

### The supreme condition

The *Akula* ground is not itself one more particular object.

It is the undivided conscious reality.

The commentary then describes the manifestation of desire.

Something is intended.

The intended object is not yet fully differentiated.

It begins to emerge as a possible form of experience.

That initial emergence is associated with knowledge-power.

The form then acquires increasing definiteness.

The senses participate.

The object is apprehended.

In Trika, the whole process is the movement of Śakti.

### The extraordinary phrase about perception

Abhinavagupta explains that what we ordinarily call seeing arises through the differentiation and manifestation of conscious powers.

He connects perception with a process of outward orientation, attraction, and disclosure.

Terms such as **rodhana** and **drāvaṇa** reappear.

Earlier, these were associated with Rudra's power to dissolve bondage.

Here they operate in a different interpretive context, concerning the movement of perceptual manifestation.

The same root can be employed across distinct levels of philosophical explanation.

That does not mean every occurrence has an identical technical meaning.

A precise reading must attend to the local argument.

### The relationship between internal and external

Imagine intending to look at something.

There is a direction of attention.

You turn your head.

The senses become oriented toward an object.

An object becomes manifest in a particular perceptual configuration.

From the ordinary perspective, this involves attention, bodily movement, sensory processing, and cognition.

Abhinavagupta's philosophical account asks how intention, cognition, and manifestation are related within the supreme power of consciousness.

The *icchāśakti* does not merely sit beside perception.

It participates in the broader account of how determinate perception becomes possible.

The sensory faculties are understood as powers of consciousness.

But again, that does not eliminate their specific empirical functioning.

The eye and ear are different.

Vision and hearing are different.

The metaphysical ground is one.

The distinctions arise within it.

### What is *sākṣāt*?

The root text says:

**साक्षात्पश्यत्यसंदिग्धम्**

*sākṣāt paśyaty asaṃdigdham.*

“He sees directly, without doubt.”

**sākṣāt** - directly, immediately, before one's eyes.

**paśyati** - sees, third-person singular present of √*paś*, to see.

**asaṃdigdha** - undoubted, unambiguous, free from uncertainty.

The point is direct manifestation rather than vague inference or mere supposition.

In the surrounding tantric context, the claim concerns the perception of deity-forms.

That is a traditional claim of yogic experience.

It cannot be established as objectively verified apparition merely by quoting the verse.

But the philosophical explanation is meaningful.

The experience is presented as a movement from implicit intention toward direct apprehension.

### The problem of error

Abhinavagupta even considers uncertain perception.

A person might mistake a post for a human figure.

This is a classical Indian philosophical example.

The perceiver sees something at a distance.

The shape is ambiguous.

The mind cannot confidently settle whether it is a post or a person.

There is uncertainty concerning the object.

But there is no uncertainty that a cognitive appearance is occurring.

This distinction is essential.

**A perception can be uncertain in content while remaining manifest as an occurrence of consciousness.**

The knowing of uncertainty is itself a form of awareness.

In fact, the judgement:

“I don't know whether that is a post or a person”

already presupposes an awareness in which the uncertainty is presented.

This is exactly the kind of philosophical observation that makes Pratyabhijñā so powerful.

It does not require pretending that mistaken cognition is correct.

It requires recognising that both mistaken and correct cognition are manifest within consciousness.

The reality of manifestness is distinct from the accuracy of a particular judgement.

And now Abhinavagupta links perception to memory.

Which brings us to the goddesses.


## XVI. Three Goddesses, Three Cognitive Functions, and the Extraordinary Metaphysics of Remembering Your Shopping List

### Kashmir Series pp. 247-248 - Gnoli pp. 271-272

The commentary moves from direct perception to remembering the perceived form and then to conceptual determination.

Abhinavagupta describes the powers of the three goddesses through these cognitive modes.

He explains that the practitioner who remembers the previously perceived form is functioning through **Parāparā**, the intermediate goddess associated here with *smṛtiśakti*, the power of memory.

Then he turns to conceptual activity as belonging to **Aparā**.

Let's examine the sequence carefully.

### First: Parā

**परा - parā**

The supreme goddess.

In this immediate context, she is connected with direct manifestation or seeing.

But do not reduce Parā's entire theological identity to the ordinary sensory faculty of sight.

The passage is giving a specific explanatory correspondence.

Parā is supreme consciousness in its undivided or highest expression.

Here the immediacy of manifestness becomes the relevant cognitive analogy.

The object appears.

Before elaborate conceptual determination, its manifestness is already present.

The primary fact is not yet the fully articulated judgement:

“That is a blue ceramic cup, probably manufactured in 2023, belonging to me, and standing on the table beside a half-finished Sanskrit commentary.”

The primary concern is that something is manifest.

That manifestness is being related to the supreme power.

### Second: Parāparā

**परापरा - parāparā**

Literally incorporating both *parā* and *aparā*, supreme and non-supreme, but functioning as the name of the intermediate goddess.

The passage associates her with **memory**.

Why?

Because memory mediates between a previously manifest event and its present recollection.

The remembered object is not simply the immediately present external object.

Nor is memory entirely detached from what appeared.

It connects a past experience with a present cognitive act.

This makes it an especially useful illustration of an intermediate power.

Think about remembering the taste of a mango.

You are not necessarily eating a mango now.

Yet a representation of that taste appears.

The present cognition refers to an earlier sensory event.

And you recognise the continuity:

“I tasted that mango.”

The cognitive form is not identical to the earlier sensation.

But it is not wholly unrelated.

The passage uses memory to illuminate this power of relation.

### Third: Aparā

**अपरा - aparā**

The non-supreme or differentiated goddess.

Here Abhinavagupta associates her with **vikalpa**.

Conceptual determination.

Articulation.

The activity through which an experience is cognitively distinguished and described.

This does not mean Aparā is evil.

Nor does it mean ordinary thinking is inherently an obstacle to liberation.

We have already encountered Utpaladeva's statement that conceptual differentiation may continue in the liberated person.

Aparā is a power.

Her manifestation can become the basis of bondage when its differentiated appearances are misrecognised as ultimately separate.

But the same power can function within recognition.

The world doesn't have to stop appearing.

The mind doesn't have to stop thinking.

The powers are to be recognised as what they are.

### The authentic doctrinal summary

Near the conclusion of this section, Abhinavagupta quotes a pair of verses.

The first says:

**दर्शनं तु परा देवी स्मरणं च परापरा ।**

*darśanaṃ tu parā devī smaraṇaṃ ca parāparā.*

**विकल्पस्त्वपरा देवी त्रिकशक्तिमयः प्रभुः ॥**

*vikalpas tv aparā devī trikaśaktimayaḥ prabhuḥ.*

“Seeing is the supreme goddess Parā, remembering is Parāparā, and conceptual determination is Aparā. The Lord consists of the powers of the Trika.”

The second reads:

**मायाविद्ये उभे तस्य माया तु चतुरण्डिका ।**

*māyāvidye ubhe tasya māyā tu caturaṇḍikā.*

**विद्या स्वरूपसंवित्तिरनुग्रहमयी शिवा ॥**

*vidyā svarūpasaṃvittir anugrahamayī śivā.*

“Māyā and Vidyā are both his. Māyā has the character of the four cosmic spheres, while Vidyā is awareness of one's own nature, auspicious and consisting of grace.”

These verses appear in the commentary as an embedded quotation. The source is not securely identified in the accessible GRETIL text, so they should not be represented as a newly composed verse by Abhinavagupta merely because he quotes them. ; \]

And the philosophical implications are extraordinary.

### The three powers are not three isolated minds

The first verse maps perception, memory, and conceptual determination onto the three goddesses.

But the final expression is:

**त्रिकशक्तिमयः प्रभुः**

*trikaśaktimayaḥ prabhuḥ.*

“The Lord consists of the powers of the Trika.”

The powers remain ultimately grounded in one Lord.

This is why differentiation does not constitute an absolute division.

Perception is one mode.

Memory is another.

Conceptual articulation is another.

But their manifestness belongs to one conscious reality.

The ordinary person experiences these as the operations of a limited psychological subject.

The awakened person recognises their deeper nature as powers of the supreme consciousness.

### Māyā and Vidyā

Now the second verse complicates everything.

Both Māyā and Vidyā belong to the supreme Lord.

This is very important.

**Māyā is not an independent cosmic villain who has somehow defeated Śiva.**

Māyā is itself a power manifested within the supreme reality.

Its function concerns differentiation and limitation.

It allows the universe to appear through structured distinctions.

And those distinctions can become the basis for misrecognition.

**Vidyā**, in this context, is awareness of one's own nature.

It reveals what the limited subject fails to apprehend.

The distinction between bondage and liberation does not require positing two independent ultimate realities.

The same supreme consciousness can manifest limitation and its overcoming.

This is the philosophical significance of having both powers belong to the Lord.

### Caturaṇḍikā

The compound:

**catur** - four.

**aṇḍa** - egg, sphere, cosmic enclosure.

**-ikā** - feminine formation.

The reference is to the four *aṇḍas*, the great cosmic spheres or enclosures in the tantric cosmological classification.

We encountered them in the mantra analysis:

The earthly sphere.

The sphere associated with Prakṛti.

The Māyīya sphere.

The Śākta sphere.

The exact mapping requires attention to the particular tantric system, but the present commentary explicitly associates the four cosmic spheres with the differentiated domain of Māyā.

Notice how the argument circles back.

The mantra's phonemes pervade the cosmic spheres.

The powers of consciousness are manifested through the differentiated universe.

The goddess associated with Māyā encompasses the structured field of manifestation.

And Vidyā is self-recognition.

Thus the mantra is a way of expressing the relationship between cosmic differentiation and recognition.

### What does *anugrahamayī* mean?

**अनुग्रहमयी - anugrahamayī**

*anugraha* - grace, favour, support, elevation, from *anu-* \+ √*grah*.

*-mayī* - consisting of, full of, constituted by.

The feminine form agrees with Vidyā.

So Vidyā is characterised by grace.

Now we can answer the guru problem more clearly.

The ultimate reality is already present.

But its recognition can be revealed through grace.

Grace is not a foreign substance imported into consciousness.

It is the power through which the subject's own nature becomes manifest to itself.

The teacher, the teaching, and the event of understanding can be treated as differentiated expressions of this power.

This is how a nondual system can retain a meaningful role for the guru.

The teacher does not create the student's ultimate nature.

But the teacher can participate in the event through which that nature is recognised.


## XVII. The Mothers, the Heroes, the Lords of Heroes, and the Divine Powers That Apparently Run Your Cognitive Apparatus

### Kashmir Series p. 248 - Gnoli p. 272

The root text now invokes a remarkable collection of beings.

**Mātaraḥ.**

**Yogīśvaryaḥ.**

**Vīrāḥ.**

**Vīreśvarāḥ.**

**Śākinīgaṇaḥ.**

The mothers.

The powerful Yoginīs.

The heroes.

The lords of heroes.

The company of Śākinīs.

In a superficial reading, this becomes a list of supernatural personnel who arrive to grant powers to an initiated practitioner.

But Abhinavagupta's commentary supplies a more sophisticated interpretation.

He links these groups with aspects of cognition, subjectivity, mental functioning, the senses, phonemes, and the powers of awareness.

This is one of the most valuable passages for understanding Trika's psychocosmology.

### First: the Mātṛs

**मातरः - mātaraḥ**

The mothers.

A nominative plural form of *mātṛ*, mother.

The commentary relates them to powers of subjectivity.

The crucial idea is that these powers are grounded in the subject and possess their own status as conscious manifestations.

They do not require an independently external object to become manifest as powers.

Here Abhinavagupta uses the term **pramātṛtva**.

The condition of being a knowing subject.

This matters because an ordinary cognitive faculty is often treated as an instrument possessed by a separate ego.

The ego sees.

The ego remembers.

The ego acts.

But in the Trika analysis, the powers of cognition are manifestations of the universal subject.

The individual ego is itself a contracted expression within that system.

Therefore the divine mothers are not merely decorative images placed around an otherwise ordinary mind.

They represent a way of understanding the powers through which experience is constituted.

### Yogīśvaryaḥ

**योगीश्वर्यः - yogīśvaryaḥ**

Powerful female yogic divinities or mistresses of yogic power.

The word connects *yoginī* and *īśvarī* in the relevant grammatical and theological environment.

The context associates the divine powers with extraordinary capacity.

But Abhinavagupta's principal explanatory move is to relate them to differentiated powers of consciousness.

In other words, the goddesses are not simply external things that appear after someone has successfully repeated a mantra.

Their significance is also internal to the structure of experience.

### The inner faculties

The commentary refers to the **antaḥkaraṇa**, the internal instrument.

In Indian philosophical vocabulary this includes cognitive and mental functions such as *buddhi*, *ahaṃkāra*, and *manas*, although the precise enumeration differs by system.

These are not merely modern psychological categories translated into Sanskrit.

They belong to distinct philosophical analyses of cognition.

But their relevance here is understandable.

Mental processes operate as a coordinated field.

Attention.

Judgement.

Memory.

Determination.

Identification.

Intention.

Each has a differentiated role.

Abhinavagupta interprets these operations through the powers of the divine.

And in the liberated condition, their activity is no longer apprehended as fundamentally separate from supreme consciousness.

### Vīra - वीर

Hero.

In tantric contexts, it can denote a practitioner possessing the requisite qualities for heroic ritual or spiritual activity.

Here Abhinavagupta associates the *vīras* with cognitive and active faculties.

The hero is not simply a man holding a sword.

The heroic principle concerns power and agency within the differentiated manifestation.

The **buddhīndriyas** and **karmendriyas**-the faculties of cognition and action-are drawn into the explanation.

The senses are not merely passive windows.

They are expressions of powers.

Seeing and hearing are modes of cognition.

Speaking and grasping are modes of action.

The divine symbolism concerns their participation in the universal Śakti.

### Vīreśvara - वीरेश्वर

*Vīra + īśvara.*

Lord of heroes.

The commentarial explanation connects these with the phonemic powers beginning with **ka** and the related divine ordering.

We previously examined how the Sanskrit phonemes can be understood as deities or manifestations of powers.

This is where that theory returns.

The phonemic structure is not separate from the structure of consciousness.

The senses, the powers of action, the phonemes, and the goddesses are integrated into a single system.

But their functions remain differentiated.

### Śākinīgaṇa - शाकिनीगण

The company of Śākinīs.

The commentary relates the group to the *śaktis* manifested through the phonemic ordering, including divine figures such as Brāhmī and related goddesses.

It also links the powers with mental dispositions, including *rāga* and *dveṣa*, attraction and aversion.

Now that's interesting.

Because attraction and aversion are normally experienced as personal psychological events.

“I like this.”

“I dislike that.”

“I want this.”

“I fear that.”

But Abhinavagupta is asking whether such differentiated mental activity can also be recognised as manifestations of consciousness.

This doesn't mean all desires are spiritually wise or ethically harmless.

It means that the powers operating through experience are not ultimately independent of consciousness.

They can bind through misrecognition.

They can also become occasions for recognition.

### The distinction between a power and its misapplication

A knife is an instrument.

It can prepare food.

It can cause injury.

The fact that it has a capacity does not establish that every exercise of that capacity is good.

Similarly, recognising an emotion as a manifestation of consciousness does not justify every action motivated by it.

Anger appears within consciousness.

That does not mean anger is always accurate or that harming others becomes permissible.

The metaphysical reality of an experience is not identical with the ethical correctness of every act arising from it.

This is particularly important in a tradition containing antinomian ritual language.

Nonduality cannot simply be used to abolish responsibility.

The empirical world has differentiated consequences.

And Abhinavagupta has not forgotten this.

Indeed, shortly he will explicitly return to the role of **niyati**, determinacy, in ordinary causal activity.

But first we have to confront the spectacular promises of the root text.


## XVIII. The Siddhis: Can Recognition Grant Every Accomplishment?

The root text speaks of the divine powers granting supreme accomplishment or an intended result.

**यच्छन्ति परमां सिद्धिं फलं यद्वा समीहितम्**

*yacchanti paramāṃ siddhiṃ phalaṃ yad vā samīhitam.*

“They grant supreme accomplishment, or the fruit that has been desired.”

The grammar is straightforward.

**yacchanti** - they give, present third-person plural of √*dā*, with its irregular present stem.

**paramām** - supreme, highest, accusative feminine singular.

**siddhim** - accomplishment or attainment, accusative feminine singular.

**phalam** - fruit or result, accusative neuter singular.

**samīhitam** - desired, intended, sought, from *sam-* \+ √*īh*.

And the distinction between **paramā siddhi** and **samīhita phala** is crucial.

The highest accomplishment is not necessarily identical with any particular desired result.

You might seek wealth.

A ritual accomplishment.

A visionary experience.

Knowledge of an unknown fact.

Or the liberation from limitation itself.

These do not all have the same status.

Abhinavagupta's commentary distinguishes the kinds of accomplishment available within differentiated experience from the ultimate significance of the supreme heart.

### Vyāvahārika siddhi

An accomplishment within ordinary or transactional reality.

A particular result.

Something obtained within the differentiated field of causes, actions, intentions, and circumstances.

### Pāramārthika siddhi

Ultimate accomplishment.

The recognition of the supreme reality.

And Abhinavagupta insists that the highest accomplishment depends upon entry into the heart.

This is the decisive distinction.

The practitioner could theoretically obtain unusual capacities within a restricted worldview and still fail to recognise the supreme Self.

Such an attainment would not, by itself, constitute liberation.

That is why supernatural powers are not the final criterion of spiritual realization.

The distinction is philosophically consistent with Trika's hierarchy.

**A power operating within the universe is not identical with recognition of the ultimate nature of the universe.**

The same is true of ordinary abilities.

You might have an extraordinary memory.

A brilliant intellect.

Exceptional sensory sensitivity.

The ability to concentrate for long periods.

None of those accomplishments automatically entails liberation.

The question is how the subject understands its own nature.

### The astonishing expansion of the claim

The commentary refers to practitioners of other mantra systems and even those who possess particular attainments.

Abhinavagupta insists that their ultimate completion lies in the recognition of this heart.

This is the tradition's universalising claim.

The heart of Bhairava is the ultimate ground of all powers.

Therefore any particular accomplishment remains ontologically dependent on that ground.

Now, that is a metaphysical conclusion within the Trika system.

It does not establish that every historical practitioner of every other tradition would agree with it.

Nor does it demonstrate the factual existence of every supernatural siddhi described in the tantras.

But its internal logic is coherent.

If consciousness is truly universal, then no power can ultimately exist outside it.

Every manifestation is grounded in its freedom.

And supreme accomplishment is recognition of that grounding.


## XIX. The Four Cosmic Eggs Get Dissolved: Māyā, Vidyā, and the Destruction of Separation

### Kashmir Series pp. 248-249 - Gnoli p. 272

Abhinavagupta now returns to the heart-mantra's relationship with the cosmic spheres.

This is the final major synthesis before the next verse.

He describes the supreme Lord as the very nature of the heart, enriched by the three powers and possessing the unity of the innumerable manifestations of consciousness that continuously arise and withdraw.

The passage includes the remarkable idea of dissolving the differentiated *aṇḍas*.

The cosmic spheres.

Here the root **√lup** becomes significant.

To cut off.

Erase.

Destroy.

Cause to disappear.

Abhinavagupta connects the dissolution of the spheres with the removal of the limiting distinctions through which the universe is misrecognised.

But a distinction is necessary.

This is not the claim that the practitioner literally destroys the physical universe.

It concerns the removal of the imposed limitation that makes the manifest world appear ultimately separate from consciousness.

The spheres are not merely an external astronomical model.

They form part of the tantric account of differentiated manifestation.

### The four aṇḍas

The ordinary interpretation of the word **aṇḍa** is an egg.

In tantric cosmology, the term can denote a cosmic sphere or enclosure.

The four spheres are arranged according to increasingly subtle levels of manifestation.

Their precise enumeration is connected with the division of the tattvas.

The *sa* of the mantra pervades the lower triad.

The *au* relates to the fourth sphere.

Visarga is associated with the supreme principle beyond these.

Thus the heart-mantra symbolically encompasses the differentiated cosmos and what transcends differentiation.

In this context, dissolution of the spheres means something more philosophically specific than smashing four objects.

It means the recognition that their differentiated existence does not establish ultimate separation from consciousness.

### The problem of absolute difference

Suppose you think:

“I am fundamentally a limited subject.”

“Everything else is outside me.”

“The world is composed of absolutely independent objects.”

“Consciousness is merely one such object, somewhere inside my head.”

That worldview makes the supreme subject appear constrained by the contents of experience.

Recognition involves re-examining the status of the knowing subject and its objects.

In Trika, the ultimate subject is not a little awareness trapped among external things.

It is the universal luminous reality through which subject-object differentiation arises.

The four cosmic spheres can therefore be understood as differentiated expressions rather than independent limits on the ultimate.

This is the metaphysical meaning of the mantra's pervasion.

### Vidyā and Māyā are not two competing ultimate substances

Abhinavagupta summarises:

**एवमेष विद्यामायोभयात्मा परमेश्वर एक एव चिद्धनः**

*evam eṣa vidyāmāyobhayātmā parameśvara eka eva cidghanaḥ.*

“Thus this supreme Lord, whose nature encompasses both Vidyā and Māyā, is one alone, a compact fullness of consciousness.”

**cidghana** is particularly beautiful.

*Cit* - consciousness.

*Ghana* - dense, compact, solid, homogeneous fullness.

The compound evokes consciousness as an undivided mass or plenitude, not a collection of independent fragments.

Again, it is metaphorical.

Consciousness is not literally a solid object with mass and density.

The expression emphasises its undivided nature.

And this is perhaps the culmination of the entire philosophical passage.

The same Lord manifests Māyā and Vidyā.

The same Lord manifests limited cognition and its recognition.

The same Lord manifests the powers of seeing, remembering, and conceptualising.

The same Lord is the heart of the mantra.

The same Lord is the reality of the practitioner.

The differentiation is real as manifestation.

But not ultimately separate.

### A crucial point about the ordinary world

This doctrine is not saying that the world is absolutely nonexistent.

Nor is it merely saying that the world is a deceptive dream to be ignored.

Abhinavagupta's nonduality includes the reality of manifestation as the freedom of consciousness.

The appearance is not an independent second substance.

But it is not nothing.

This is why it is possible for action to continue.

Perception to continue.

Language to continue.

Ethical and practical distinctions to continue.

Recognition does not require annihilating experience.

It requires understanding the ultimate nature of the experiencing field.

And now we reach the very last argument before the next verse.

Abhinavagupta says, in effect:

Yes, ultimate recognition is fundamental.

But if you're pursuing a particular yogic accomplishment within the differentiated world, you still have to follow the relevant discipline.

The cosmic freedom of consciousness does not mean you can simply ignore causality.


## XX. Niyati Returns: Enlightenment Does Not Mean You Can Disregard How Things Work

### Kashmir Series p. 249 - Gnoli pp. 272-273

The final passage of the present commentary introduces an alternative interpretation.

**यदि तु योगप्राधान्यम्**

*yadi tu yogaprādhānyam.*

“But if the emphasis is on yoga…”

Abhinavagupta explains that, in this case, the yogic practices and preliminary observances described in the *Śrīpūrvaśāstra* and related texts must be taken into account.

He invokes practices such as **vrata**, disciplined observance.

And returns to the root text's expression concerning the correct utterance of the mantra.

Now the important point:

**दृष्टकार्येषु नियतिपरतन्त्री क्रियाकलापं नियतमेवापेक्षते**

*dṛṣṭakāryeṣu niyatiparatantrī kriyākalāpaṃ niyatam evāpekṣate.*

“In the case of observable results, activity, being subject to determinacy, necessarily requires the appropriate course of action.”

This is a powerful qualification. ; \]

Let's consider what it means.

The ultimate reality is free.

But the differentiated manifestation operates through determinate relations.

A seed requires suitable conditions to grow.

A body requires food and water.

Language requires established conventions for ordinary communication.

A particular contemplative discipline may require training and repetition.

The fact that all these processes arise within consciousness does not eliminate their operative structures.

### Niyati - नियति

Determinacy.

Restriction.

Ordering.

Limitation.

The concept belongs to the Trika account of the limited powers through which the universal freedom of consciousness becomes manifested in differentiated existence.

It is not simply identical with modern physical determinism.

But it includes the idea of structured constraint.

The finite subject acts within conditions.

A particular activity has particular requirements.

A desired result depends upon the relevant causes and circumstances.

Abhinavagupta's claim is therefore quite different from the crude assertion:

“Because I am Śiva, I can make anything happen by imagining it.”

That would confuse the metaphysical freedom of universal consciousness with the capacities of a contracted individual.

### The nāḍī and cakra disciplines

He concludes by mentioning the yogic use of:

**nāḍī** - subtle channel.

**cakra** - wheel, centre, structured configuration.

**karaṇa** - method, instrument, operation.

**bhāvanā** - contemplation, cultivation, imaginative construction or meditative development.

**saṃvedana** - experiencing, awareness, cognition.

These belong to the particular yogic techniques of the tradition.

The precise operations vary among texts and systems.

The point here is not that every adept must perform one identical sequence.

It is that a particular yogic result can depend upon the rules and conditions of the particular discipline.

And that is entirely compatible with the higher claim that recognition is the essence of liberation.

### Two distinct explanatory levels

At the ultimate level:

Consciousness is free.

Its nature is not produced by an external act.

Liberation is recognition.

At the level of specific practice:

Particular results can depend on particular means.

The body has limitations.

The mind has conditioning.

Rituals have procedures.

Languages have rules.

Teachers transmit techniques.

Practices can require sustained effort.

Confusing the two levels creates the most spectacular philosophical nonsense.

It is like saying:

“Because all numbers belong to mathematics, I can ignore arithmetic while doing my accounts.”

No.

The fact that arithmetic belongs to a larger system does not abolish its rules.

Likewise, metaphysical nonduality does not entail that differentiated action has no structure.

### The genuinely radical conclusion

This is what makes Abhinavagupta's position more sophisticated than a simplistic spirituality of either effort or effortlessness.

He is not simply telling everyone to strive forever.

Nor is he asserting that all discipline is unnecessary in every case.

He distinguishes the direct recognition of ultimate reality from the requirements of particular practices and attainments.

The first concerns what the subject truly is.

The second concerns the processes through which particular states and effects may arise.

And he insists that the distinction matters.

**The supreme freedom of consciousness includes the power to manifest an ordered world.**

It is not contradicted by order.

Order is one of its possible modes of expression.

And this connects us back to the earlier discussion of *niyati* and language.

The supreme power of speech can manifest through particular phonemes.

But ordinary words are not therefore free to mean absolutely anything in every context.

The supreme power of action can manifest through particular processes.

But those processes are not therefore exempt from their conditions.

The supreme consciousness can manifest through the subject.

But the limited ego does not thereby become an omnipotent independent agent.

The metaphysics is consistent.

And the commentary ends the section.


## XXI. The Next Authentic Transition: The Man Who Has Never Seen the Maṇḍala

### Kashmir Series p. 249 onward - Gnoli p. 273

Abhinavagupta introduces the next section with:

**अस्येदानीं त्रिकार्थस्य**

*asyedānīṃ trikārthasya.*

“Now, concerning this meaning of the Trika…”

He recalls the claim that Trika is superior to *Kula* and prepares to explain its unsurpassable character.

The crucial phrase is:

**सर्वोत्तरमनुत्तरत्वम्**

*sarvottaram anuttaratvam.*

“The unsurpassable condition that stands above all.”

And then he introduces the next root verse.

**अदृष्टमण्डलोऽप्येवं यः कश्चिद्वेत्ति तत्त्वतः ।**

*adṛṣṭamaṇḍalo 'py evaṃ yaḥ kaścid vetti tattvataḥ.*

**स सिद्धिभाग्भवेन्नित्यं स योगी स च दीक्षितः ॥**

*sa siddhibhāg bhaven nityaṃ sa yogī sa ca dīkṣitaḥ.*

My translation:

“Even one who has not seen the maṇḍala, whoever truly knows in this manner, becomes a possessor of accomplishment forever. He is a yogin, and he is initiated.”

This is the next great turn.

Because the text has just spent considerable energy explaining the importance of the guru, transmission, mantric understanding, and ritual disciplines.

Now it declares that someone who has not even seen the ritual maṇḍala may nevertheless be an accomplished yogin and an initiate through true knowledge.

**The man who hasn't attended the ceremony may nevertheless possess its ultimate result.**

And that is exactly the paradox Part XXXI must investigate.

### A textual warning about the numbering

The 1918 KSTS presentation numbers this section as verse **18**, and GRETIL's commented text agrees in placing *adṛṣṭamaṇḍala* at the next transition.

However, the independently transmitted *Parātriṃśikā* edited in Marino Faliero's transcription counts the corresponding material differently: it contains an additional line concerning the power of entering the mantra and numbers *adṛṣṭamaṇḍala* as **verse 19**.

That difference must not be concealed. It reflects a difference in how the root text is transmitted and segmented, not a licence to invent or omit words. ; ; \]

Part XXXI should therefore begin from **KSTS verse 18, *adṛṣṭamaṇḍalo 'py evam***, explicitly preserving the independent verse-numbering difference.

And we stop here.

Because Abhinavagupta has just raised the next and perhaps most dangerous question in the whole text:

**If real initiation is recognition, can someone be truly initiated without performing the prescribed initiation?**

And if so, what happens to ritual authority?


## Closing: The Guru Is Śiva. The Student Is Śiva. So Who the Fuck Is Teaching Whom?

Let's return to the beginning.

We started with a guru whose satisfaction apparently determines whether a disciple can receive the supreme mantra.

The mantra must be transmitted correctly.

The tradition places restrictions on recording it.

The teacher must possess the relevant understanding.

The student must be prepared.

A particular ritual order has to be respected.

And yet, at the very heart of this theological structure, Abhinavagupta declares that liberation is nothing other than identity with Bhairava.

How can both be true?

Because there are two distinct questions.

**What is the ultimate nature of liberation?**

And:

**How does a limited subject come to recognise that nature?**

The first concerns identity with universal consciousness.

The second concerns the differentiated process through which recognition may occur.

The guru is not a second ultimate reality standing outside Śiva.

The student is not an independently existing substance that Śiva must somehow transform into itself.

The mantra is not a foreign power travelling from one isolated being to another.

The entire situation takes place within the manifestation of consciousness.

The teacher speaks.

The student hears.

The meaning becomes intelligible.

Recognition may arise.

And the supposed distance between the two beings is understood through the deeper unity of their conscious ground.

But the differentiated situation remains meaningful.

The teacher's words can be helpful.

A student's understanding can be incomplete.

The tradition's procedures can serve particular purposes.

The fact that the whole process ultimately belongs to Śiva does not imply that its individual components are meaningless.

**Nonduality is not the abolition of differentiated reality. It is the recognition of its ultimate ground.**

And this explains why Abhinavagupta devotes so much effort to language.

An utterance arises.

It is made of particular sounds.

Those sounds are governed by phonological rules.

The words participate in semantic structures.

The sentence becomes intelligible.

Understanding occurs.

But the possibility of understanding is not itself merely another sound in the sentence.

The ground of intelligibility is more fundamental than any particular expression.

Similarly, a teacher can communicate the doctrine of consciousness.

But the consciousness through which the student understands the teaching is not manufactured by the teacher's utterance.

It was already present as the condition of hearing anything at all.

That is the extraordinary insight toward which the passage moves.

The guru does not install consciousness.

The mantra does not import consciousness.

The ritual does not create consciousness.

The teaching makes possible the recognition of what was never absent.

And that is why the next verse is explosive.

The student who has never seen the maṇḍala may nevertheless be initiated.

Not because rites are automatically worthless.

But because the supreme purpose of initiation is not finally exhausted by the external rite.

The question becomes whether the inner transformation has occurred.

The ritual points toward that transformation.

The guru teaches that transformation.

The mantra expresses that transformation.

But the reality disclosed is not an object belonging to the guru, the ritual, or the manuscript.

It is the very nature of consciousness.

The teacher looks at the student.

The student looks at the teacher.

Both are manifestations of the same supreme subject.

And somewhere in eleventh-century Kashmir, Abhinavagupta is probably laughing at the fact that the entire universe has organised an elaborate ceremony so that consciousness can remind itself that it is consciousness.

The alphabet has completed its lesson.

The guru has finished speaking.

The student has heard the words.

But the real question is no longer what the teacher said.

It is:

**Who, precisely, was present to hear it?**
