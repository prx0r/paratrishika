# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART IX - Mālinī's Secret Alphabet

*Abhinavagupta takes fifty phonemic powers, rearranges the alphabet, turns hearing into tasting, makes the Earth principle appear in several different places, and reveals why the Goddess refuses to keep her metaphysical departments separate.*

Commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series, pp. 151-156


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

Right.

We've reached a particularly dangerous point in the life of a Sanskrit student.

You thought the alphabet was a reasonably stable institution.

अ आ इ ई उ ऊ…

क ख ग घ ङ…

Nice orderly arrangement. Everyone knows where they stand.

Then Abhinavagupta introduced Mālinī.

The vowels started appearing among the consonants. The tattvas began occupying several phonemes simultaneously. One sequence represented the inner life of Śiva; another represented differentiated manifestation; and apparently the Goddess had never agreed to follow an ordinary alphabetical filing system.

We discovered in Part VIII that the explanation rests on three things.

First, every manifested principle implicates the whole: **sarvasarvātmakatva**.

Second, Śiva and Śakti are symbolized as **bīja** and **yoni**, seed and generative matrix.

Third, the transformation of undivided consciousness into differentiated expression does not make that expression independent of its source.

Now Abhinavagupta is going to demonstrate what this means.

Not with another vague declaration that everything is consciousness.

With actual letters.

Actual correspondences.

And some of the most fantastically compressed Sanskrit prose we've yet encountered.

One textual distinction is essential. The 1918 Kashmir Series transcription contains explanatory material in square brackets. Some of it gives extremely useful phoneme charts and *nyāsa* instructions, but **it must not be attributed automatically to Abhinavagupta**. His own prose resumes on p. 151 and gives the astonishingly dense mapping across pp. 152-154.

We're going to keep those layers separate.

And we're not going to pretend the letters have one simple correspondence each. That's precisely the assumption he's challenging.

## 1. The final point from Part VIII - Mālinī is Śakti in her expressive form

We resume with the concluding claim about Mālinī's nature.

Abhinavagupta describes her as:

**मुख्यपारमार्थिकमध्यमाधामशक्तिसतत्त्वम्**

*Mukhyapāramārthikamadhyamādhāmaśaktisatattvam.*

A substantial compound referring to the essential reality of the power belonging to the principal, ultimately real domain of Madhyamā speech.

It's a glorious Sanskrit centipede.

Let's dissect its constituents.

| Element | IAST | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **मुख्य** | *mukhya* | Principal, primary |
| **पारमार्थिक** | *pāramārthika* | Pertaining to ultimate reality |
| **मध्यमा** | *madhyamā* | The intermediate level of speech |
| **धाम** | *dhāma* | Abode, domain, manifestation |
| **शक्ति** | *śakti* | Power |
| **सतत्त्व** | *satattva* | True nature, essential reality |

Now notice *pāramārthika*.

It belongs to the family of:

**परमार्थ - paramārtha**

The supreme or ultimate meaning, the highest reality.

From:

*parama + artha → paramārtha*.

The long ā arises through vowel sandhi.

Then the derivative *pāramārthika* means pertaining to ultimate reality.

This is an important distinction.

Abhinavagupta is not describing Madhyamā merely as the private inner monologue of an ordinary person.

He is identifying a divine level of expressive consciousness from which further differentiation becomes possible.

### Why Mālinī belongs to Madhyamā

Remember the speech hierarchy.

**Parā:** undivided supreme speech.

**Paśyantī:** differentiation begins to emerge in a unified apprehension.

**Madhyamā:** the relation between signifying expression and what it signifies becomes more explicit.

**Vaikharī:** articulated external speech.

Mālinī's rearrangement of phonemes belongs to the manifestation of Śakti as differentiated expressive power.

And that's why her unusual arrangement matters.

The letters are not merely symbols sitting on a shelf.

Their relationships express different ways consciousness manifests and apprehends its own powers.

But Abhinavagupta now brings in the scripture itself.


## 2. The Mālinīvijaya's instruction - Construct the Śākta body

SCRIPTURAL QUOTATION REPRODUCED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA · P. 151

## यथेष्टफलसंसिद्ध्यै मन्त्रतन्त्रानुवर्तिनाम्।

## न्यसेच्छाक्तशरीरार्थं भिन्नयोनिं तु मालिनीम्॥

*Yatheṣṭaphalasaṃsiddhyai mantratantrānuvartinām.*

*Nyasec chāktaśarīrārthaṃ bhinnayoniṃ tu mālinīm.*

A close translation:

For the attainment of the desired results of those following mantra and Tantra, one should install Mālinī, the differentiated generative matrix, for the establishment of the Śākta body.

This is a quotation from the *Śrīpūrva* or Mālinīvijaya tradition, **not a newly numbered verse of the Parātrīśikā**. ; \]

Now that's quite a statement.

Mālinī isn't simply something you recite.

She constitutes a divine body through the placement of phonemic powers.

### Yatheṣṭaphalasaṃsiddhyai

**यथेष्टफलसंसिद्ध्यै - yatheṣṭaphalasaṃsiddhyai**

For the complete accomplishment of the desired result.

Break it into:

**यथा - yathā:** according to, as.

**इष्ट - iṣṭa:** desired, wished for.

**फल - phala:** result, fruit.

**संसिद्धि - saṃsiddhi:** complete accomplishment.

The initial combination:

*yathā + iṣṭa → yatheṣṭa*

Because **ā + i → e**.

And the final **-yai** is the feminine dative singular of *saṃsiddhi*, giving the purpose: *for accomplishment*.

That is a lovely grammatical detail.

Sanskrit is not merely saying the word "perfection" and hoping you understand its function.

The dative explicitly expresses the aim.

### Nyaset

**न्यसेत् - nyaset**

One should place or install.

From the verb *ni-√as*, to place or deposit, in an optative formation.

Now look at the text's continuous spelling:

**न्यसेच्छाक्तशरीरार्थम्**

*Nyasec chāktaśarīrārtham.*

The underlying word boundary is:

**न्यसेत् + शाक्तशरीरार्थम्**

*nyaset + śāktaśarīrārtham*.

At the boundary, the final dental *t* is assimilated before *ś*, producing the familiar *cch*-type sandhi in this continuous form.

So you pronounce the beginning:

*nyasec chākta...*

But when understanding the grammar, you recover:

*nyaset śākta...*

This is exactly the kind of distinction that makes Sanskrit reading possible.

The written sound sequence and the underlying grammatical words need not look identical.

### Śāktaśarīra

**शाक्तशरीर - śāktaśarīra**

The body belonging to Śakti.

**शाक्त - śākta**

Pertaining to Śakti.

**शरीर - śarīra**

Body.

And:

**अर्थम् - artham**

For the sake of.

Thus:

**śāktaśarīrārtham**

For the sake of the Śākta body.

The term is part of a ritual context of *nyāsa*, installation of sacred phonemes.

It doesn't mean that one literally manufactures a second physical body out of painted consonants.

The body is the divine and ritual configuration of the phonemic powers.

And now the most important compound:

**भिन्नयोनिम् - bhinnayonim**

Mālinī as the differentiated matrix.

We learned:

*bhinna* - differentiated, divided.

*yoni* - womb, origin, generative source.

But here the compound also distinguishes Mālinī from the more familiar Mātṛkā ordering.

Her phonemes are arranged through a distinctive Śākta structure.

Not merely the conventional alphabetical sequence.

And the point is ritual as well as philosophical.

The phonemes acquire a particular relationship when configured as the body of Mālinī.

The arrangement matters.

Now you can understand why Abhinavagupta cannot simply say that all alphabetic orders are arbitrary.

The scripture itself attributes an operative role to a particular arrangement.

But now a second question arises.

**Why install the mantra in Śakti rather than directly in Śiva or the individual subject?**

And the text answers with a wonderfully blunt contrast.

## 3. Why mantras are placed in Śakti, not Puruṣa or the transcendent principle

Immediately after the Mālinī verse, Abhinavagupta quotes another teaching.

The transmitted text begins with the contrast:

**न पुंसि न परे तत्त्वे शक्तौ मन्त्रं निवेशयेत्।**

*Na puṃsi na pare tattve śaktau mantraṃ niveśayet.*

The sense is:

One should establish the mantra in Śakti, rather than in the limited Puruṣa or the transcendent principle considered apart from operative manifestation.

The same citation relates this distinction to *jaḍatva*, inertness, and *niṣkriyatva*, inactivity, and to the traditional goals of enjoyment and liberation.

Now, excellent.

We've just spent an entire reading establishing that Śiva and Śakti are inseparable.

And suddenly the text says:

Don't place your mantra in Puruṣa.

Don't place it in the supreme principle.

Place it in Śakti.

What the hell?

Has the Absolute developed a departmental hierarchy?

Is Śiva the chairman, Śakti the chief operating officer, and Puruṣa the unfortunate intern who isn't allowed to touch the sacred machinery?

Not quite.

### First, the actual grammar

**पुंसि - puṃsi**

In the person or Puruṣa.

This is a locative singular form belonging to the noun *puṃs* or *pumān*, a man or person.

Now compare it with:

**पुरुष - puruṣa**

Person, conscious subject, the limited experiencer in the tattva system.

The meanings overlap in this technical context, but the words have different declensions.

Then:

**परे तत्त्वे - pare tattve**

In the higher or supreme principle.

Both are locative singular.

*Para* is the adjective.

*Tattva* is the noun.

The endings agree.

Now:

**शक्तौ - śaktau**

In Śakti.

This is especially worth learning.

The feminine noun:

**शक्तिः - śaktiḥ**

Power, nominative singular.

But its locative singular is:

**शक्तौ - śaktau**

So the ending *-au* here is **not** the masculine dual ending we encountered in *somasūryau*.

It is a feminine *-i* stem's locative singular.

Same visible ending.

Different grammatical function.

You have to know the stem and the sentence.

And finally:

**निवेशयेत् - niveśayet**

One should install, place, cause to enter.

Third-person singular optative of a causative-type verb associated with *ni-√viś*, to enter.

Notice the ending:

**-येत् - -yet**

A common optative formation.

The text isn't merely saying that somebody happens to place a mantra somewhere.

It's expressing an instruction.

### The metaphysical problem

Why Śakti?

Because *mantra*, in this context, is understood through **operative divine power**.

The limited Puruṣa doesn't possess independent universal agency.

Its knowledge and action are contracted.

The transcendent principle, considered in abstraction from the expression of activity, is not the differentiated operative power through which a particular ritual result manifests.

Śakti is that operative freedom.

But remember the important qualification.

**This is a distinction of function within the theological system, not a claim that Śiva is ultimately powerless.**

The whole doctrine of Trika would collapse if Śiva and Śakti became two unrelated substances.

So when the text describes the supreme principle as inactive in this specific contrast, it concerns the principle as distinguished from its operative power.

Not an absolute denial of divine freedom.

### Two more words worth retaining

**जडत्व - jaḍatva**

Inertness, insentience.

From:

*jaḍa + tva*.

And:

**निष्क्रियत्व - niṣkriyatva**

The condition of being without activity.

From:

*niṣkriya + tva*.

Now compare:

**भोग - bhoga**

Enjoyment, experience.

**अपवर्ग - apavarga**

Liberation, release.

The scripture associates the relevant ritual accomplishments with Śakti's operation.

Again, those are claims within a historical Tantric ritual system; this philosophical discussion isn't evidence that particular supernatural ritual results have been empirically demonstrated.

The essential point is this:

**The mantra's operative power cannot be understood by separating it from the divine Śakti through which manifestation occurs.**

And that prepares us for the alphabet itself.

### An important editorial distinction

Inside the square-bracketed material between the first and final lines of the Sanskrit citation, the edition supplies additional verses about *mantravīrya*, the potency of mantra, and the relation between manifest and unmanifest power.

Those are useful for understanding the passage.

But they are editorially embedded explanatory material, not an uninterrupted continuation of Abhinavagupta's own argument.

The same applies to the detailed bodily *nyāsa* scheme immediately before p. 151.

It assigns letters to the head, face, eyes, limbs and other parts of a divine body.

That scheme belongs to the editor's explanatory apparatus. We're not going to sneak it into Abhinavagupta's prose and pretend he suddenly began issuing anatomical instructions.

Now, with that distinction established, he finally gives the opening sequence.


## 4. Mālinī's first sixteen phonemes - The internal life of Śiva

We now arrive at the transition from p. 151 to p. 152.

Abhinavagupta introduces the principle of *sarvasarvātmakatva* and then enumerates the first sixteen phonemes:

PHONEMES ENUMERATED IN ABHINAVAGUPTA'S MAIN PROSE

## न ऋ ॠ ऌ ॡ थ च ध ई ण उ ऊ ब क ख ग

*na ṛ ṝ ḷ ḹ tha ca dha ī ṇa u ū ba ka kha ga*

Count them.

Sixteen.

But look at the composition.

The ordinary Mātṛkā sequence would begin with the sixteen vowels.

Mālinī begins with **न - na**.

Then four vocalic vowels.

Then a dental aspirate.

Then a palatal consonant.

Then another dental aspirate.

Then a long vowel.

And so forth.

This is not simply the normal alphabetic order written backward.

It's a distinct arrangement.

And here's an important correction to make when comparing transcriptions: the long editorial bracket on p. 121 presents a sequence with an apparent duplicated **ण** where the p. 151 main prose and a normalized comparison give **ब - ba**. I'm using the p. 151 main reading for the first sixteen, rather than silently reproducing the bracket's inconsistency. ; \]

### Why sixteen?

In the regular Mātṛkā arrangement, sixteen vowel-powers occupy the corresponding supreme field.

Here, the initial sixteen Mālinī sounds include consonants as well as vowels, but they are interpreted in relation to the divine internal life of Śiva.

The important issue isn't simply numerical symmetry.

It's how the same underlying divine power can be expressed through different phonemic arrangements.

A modern explanatory chart distinguishes the correspondences within Parā, and their further reflection through Parāparā. That chart is useful, but it is an editorial reconstruction of Abhinavagupta's compressed argument, not a table printed by Abhinavagupta himself.

Now let's follow the actual movement of the prose.

## 5. न - Na: The whole universe begins with hearing

The first phoneme is:

# न

na

In the ordinary Mātṛkā correspondence:

**न - na** is associated with **śrotra**, the faculty of hearing.

But Abhinavagupta now describes that hearing-power through:

**नाद - nāda**

Sound, resonance, or a subtle manifestation of sound-consciousness.

The phrase at the opening of p. 152 characterizes *śrotra* through a *nādātmaka* condition.

### Nāda - नाद

**नाद - nāda**

Sound, resonance.

Within Tantra, *nāda* can signify subtle sound or an undivided vibratory power preceding fully differentiated phonetic expression.

But it is not simply any measurable acoustic frequency.

The ordinary meanings and the technical contemplative meanings must be distinguished.

Now look at the form:

**नादात्मक - nādātmaka**

Having the nature of Nāda.

Break it down:

*nāda + ātmaka → nādātmaka*.

Because:

**a + ā → ā**.

This is becoming wonderfully intuitive now.

And the philosophy?

The first phoneme is connected with hearing.

But hearing is interpreted through a deeper capacity of conscious manifestation.

The sound isn't an independently existing substance that subsequently acquires awareness.

Its manifestation as apprehended sound depends upon the power of consciousness.

### The cosmic soundcheck

Imagine the universe has not yet produced ordinary articulated speech.

No words.

No names.

No conversations.

No man sitting at a teashop explaining that he's personally attained the highest possible nondual realization.

Wonderful.

And yet the power from which differentiated sound can manifest isn't absent.

That's the symbolic significance of Nāda here.

The beginning is not simply an external acoustic event.

It's the expressive potential of awareness.

And this is precisely why Mālinī can begin with a consonant associated elsewhere with hearing.

The sound's deeper reality isn't exhausted by its lower-level correspondence.


## 6. ऋ ॠ ऌ ॡ - The four nectar-producing vowels

Now Śiva's conscious power reaches the four unusual vocalic vowels.

And now Abhinavagupta introduces the remarkable notion of a group of four vowel-seeds associated with nourishment by *amṛta*.

### Bījacatuṣka - बीजचतुष्क

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

**चतुष्क - catuṣka**

Group of four.

So:

**बीजचतुष्क - bījacatuṣka**

A fourfold group of seeds.

Now:

**अमृत - amṛta**

Deathless, immortal, or the nectar of immortality.

And:

**आप्यायन - āpyāyana**

Nourishing, replenishing, filling.

The passage describes the divine awareness as becoming strengthened or expanded through this fourfold nectar-bearing stage.

### Bṛṃhitatva - बृंहितत्व

**बृंहित - bṛṃhita**

Strengthened, expanded, nourished.

Related to √*bṛh*, to grow or expand.

Then:

**-त्व - -tva**

The abstract condition.

Thus:

**बृंहितत्व - bṛṃhitatva**

The condition of being strengthened or expanded.

But here's the important philosophical qualification.

**Śiva is not literally deficient before the four vowels arrive.**

The supreme consciousness doesn't need a nutritional supplement from ṛ, ṝ, ḷ and ḹ.

We're studying the differentiation of divine self-expression.

The language of strengthening belongs to that manifestation.

Within the symbolic sequence, the original awareness unfolds through richer expressions of its own power.

The absolute reality is not being repaired.

Its self-manifestation is being articulated.

### Why these vowels are worth learning

Now your Devanāgarī lesson.

**ऋ - ṛ**

Short vocalic r.

**ॠ - ṝ**

Long vocalic r.

**ऌ - ḷ**

Short vocalic l.

**ॡ - ḹ**

Long vocalic l.

These aren't ordinary consonant-plus-vowel combinations such as *ri* or *li*.

They're independent vowel phonemes in Sanskrit's traditional phonological inventory.

And their dependent signs are different from ordinary र and ल conjuncts.

For example:

**कृ - kṛ**

Uses the dependent sign **ृ**.

But:

**क्रि - kri**

Uses the conjunct **क्र** and the vowel sign **ि**.

You mustn't confuse them.

This is exactly the kind of difference that makes the phonemic metaphysics worth studying alongside real Sanskrit grammar.

The sounds are distinguished for linguistic reasons.

Abhinavagupta gives them an additional sacred interpretation.

One does not prove the other.


## 7. थ - Tha: Śiva tastes himself

The next sound is:

**थ - tha**

The aspirated dental consonant.

Don't confuse it with:

**ठ - ṭha**

The aspirated retroflex.

Your tongue does different things.

And in the regular Mātṛkā correspondence, *tha* is associated with **rasanā**, the faculty of taste.

Abhinavagupta takes that relation into his exposition of divine self-apprehension.

### Rasanā - रसना

**रसना - rasanā**

The tongue or faculty of tasting.

Related to:

**रस - rasa**

Juice, taste, flavour, essence, aesthetic relish.

Now, *rasa* is one of those Sanskrit words whose philosophical significance is enormous.

In aesthetics, it concerns the savouring of aesthetic experience.

In other contexts, taste, sap, essence or pleasure.

Here Abhinavagupta uses the taste-related power as a symbolic expression of conscious self-apprehension.

Śiva, as it were, tastes his own nature.

Now be careful.

The supreme consciousness isn't sitting around literally licking itself.

We're dealing with a metaphor for reflexive awareness and blissful self-revelation.

But it is a remarkably appropriate metaphor.

To taste something is not merely to possess a description of it.

The experience is immediate.

And the tradition uses such experiential immediacy to illuminate the self-apprehension of consciousness.

### Grahaṇa - ग्रहण

**ग्रहण - grahaṇa**

Taking, grasping, apprehension, cognition.

From √**grah**, to grasp or seize.

This is another critical word for Indian epistemology.

The mind apprehends an object.

But Trika asks what makes apprehension itself possible.

And in the supreme interpretation, the object of that apprehension is not fundamentally external to conscious reality.

Thus the taste-symbolism participates in the reflexive delight of Śiva.

The supreme reality is not merely a cold luminous surface.

It is *vimarśa*, living self-awareness.

Which means Śiva has effectively turned himself into the most metaphysically elaborate tasting menu imaginable.


## 8. च - Ca: Consciousness recognizes its own scent

Now:

**च - ca**

A palatal unaspirated consonant.

In the ordinary Mātṛkā arrangement, *ca* corresponds to:

**गन्ध - gandha**

Smell or odour.

And in the tattva scheme, *gandha* is especially associated with Earth.

Abhinavagupta develops the relation through the differentiated recognition of a particular quality.

The Sanskrit expression involves the appearance of a distinct *gandha* characterized through the Earth principle.

### Viśeṣībhūya

**विशेषीभूय - viśeṣībhūya**

Having become particularized or distinguished.

Break it down:

**विशेष - viśeṣa**

Distinction, particular characteristic.

**भूय - bhūya**

Having become, in an absolutive-related formation from √*bhū*.

Together, the expression concerns becoming differentiated as a particular form.

Now the philosophical transition becomes clearer.

The preceding stage concerned tasting or immediate apprehension.

Here, the manifestation becomes distinguishable as a definite characteristic.

Consciousness recognizes its own particularity.

Not because the Absolute has become a second independent object.

But because its power can manifest a determinate form.

### The metaphysical perfume counter

Imagine somebody saying:

"Śiva has discovered his own scent."

Obviously, that sounds ridiculous if taken literally.

But the symbolism concerns recognition of a particular manifestation as belonging to one's own nature.

A smell is an apprehended quality.

Its presentation depends upon consciousness.

And within Trika's doctrine, even the particularity of the experience is an expression of divine freedom.

So the point isn't that Śiva smells like sandalwood.

Or jasmine.

Or whatever increasingly expensive incense the monastery has purchased.

The point is that the divine consciousness can manifest and recognize a differentiated quality without surrendering its own unity.

And *ca* becomes the phonemic position expressing that aspect.


## 9. ध - Dha: The divine touches its own power

Now we reach:

**ध - dha**

A voiced aspirated dental consonant.

Not **द - da**.

And not **ढ - ḍha**.

Three different consonants.

The regular correspondence of *dha* involves **tvak**, the faculty of touch.

And Abhinavagupta develops this through:

**स्पर्श - sparśa**

Touch, contact.

From √**spṛś**, to touch.

### Sparśa-karaṇatā

**स्पर्शकरणता - sparśakaraṇatā**

The condition of functioning as an instrument or power of touch.

Break it down:

- sparśa - touch.
- karaṇa - instrument, means.
- -tā - abstract condition.

Now the symbolism.

Through the power of contact, consciousness apprehends its own Śakti.

Again, not through a literal physical hand reaching across a metaphysical room to grasp a second goddess.

The contact is an expression of the relation between awareness and its manifesting power.

And here's the key.

A touch ordinarily seems to require two things.

A toucher.

And something touched.

But the Tantric symbolism asks how such a distinction can appear within nondual consciousness.

The distinction is manifest.

Yet its ultimate reality isn't separate.

This is the same philosophical problem we've been examining since Part I.

And the phonemes are now providing a concrete symbolic account of the stages of that self-apprehension.


## 10. ई - Ī: The divine womb and Īśāna

Next comes the long vowel:

**ई - ī**

Abhinavagupta invokes the seed associated with **Īśāna**.

The Sanskrit:

**ईशान - īśāna**

Lord, ruler, governing power; a name of Śiva.

Related to √*īś*, to rule or possess power.

And:

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

The relevant phrase concerns the divine awareness abiding through the seed associated with Īśāna in the Śākta generative domain.

Now here's something beautifully symmetrical.

We have already encountered the consonants as *yoni*, generative matrix.

And the vowels as *bīja*, seed.

Now a vowel appears within Mālinī's differentiated sequence and participates in the unfolding of divine self-awareness.

This is exactly why Mālinī is *bhinnayoni*.

The vowel and consonant powers are no longer kept apart as two clean groups.

They are interwoven.

### Ordinary script versus sacred symbolism

Look at:

**ई**

The independent long-*ī* vowel.

And:

**की - kī**

The same vowel expressed through the dependent sign **ी** attached to क.

In ordinary phonology, both represent the same vowel quality in different syllabic contexts.

But Abhinavagupta interprets the phoneme's place within the Mālinī arrangement as an expression of a particular divine power.

The writing doesn't itself prove that identification.

The symbolism depends on the tradition's metaphysical account.

And in the present sequence, the vowel expresses a further stage in the relation between consciousness and Śakti.


## 11. ण - Ṇa: The first-person awareness reflects into speech

Next:

**ण - ṇa**

The retroflex nasal.

This is not:

**न - na**

The dental nasal.

Remember:

**न - na:** tongue near the teeth.

**ण - ṇa:** tongue curled back toward the retroflex articulation region.

They're different Sanskrit phonemes.

And this distinction becomes important because *na* and *ṇa* are involved in contrasting positions within the Mātṛkā and Mālinī mappings.

In the ordinary Mātṛkā correspondence, **ṇa** represents the faculty of speech, **vāk**.

Abhinavagupta describes the divine awareness becoming reflected in the operative power whose essence is speech.

### Karaṇaśakti

**करणशक्ति - karaṇaśakti**

The power of operating, producing or functioning.

**करण - karaṇa**

Instrument, act of doing, operative means.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

Together:

*karaṇa + śakti → karaṇaśakti*.

And the philosophical claim concerns the divine "I" manifesting within its own expressive activity.

The speech-power isn't merely an inert tool operated by a fundamentally separate subject.

Its manifestation belongs to consciousness.

But neither is it the complete, undifferentiated nature of consciousness in its external articulated form.

The differentiated function appears.

And Abhinavagupta's point is that the self-apprehension of Śiva remains present within it.

### The extraordinary relation between hearing and speech

Notice the pattern.

The first phoneme *na* was linked to hearing.

Now *ṇa* is linked to speaking.

The two can be contrasted through the mirrored arrangement.

One involves receiving sound.

The other producing sound.

But within the supreme conscious power, both are modes of expression.

The listener and the speaker are not independent of universal consciousness.

Remember Bhairava's own command:

**शृणु - śṛṇu**

Listen.

And:

**कथयामि - kathayāmi**

I explain.

The same philosophical relation has now reappeared inside the sacred alphabet.

The structure of the original dialogue is reflected in the phonemic cosmology.

That's the sort of recursive detail that makes this text extraordinary.


## 12. उ ऊ - U and Ū: Opening, expansion, and the twofold movement

Now the next pair:

**उ - u**

**ऊ - ū**

Abhinavagupta associates them with special powers expressed through:

**उन्मेष - unmeṣa**

Opening, emergence, the opening of the eyes.

And:

**ऊर्ध्वाश्रयण - ūrdhvāśrayaṇa**

An upward movement or resort to a higher support.

We must be careful not to impose a single modern psychological interpretation on these terms.

In the phonemic system, they describe complementary movements of conscious power.

And they're associated with the divine freedom to manifest.

### Unmeṣa - उन्मेष

We encountered this in the Spanda teaching.

The opening or blossoming of awareness.

From a verbal formation related to opening the eyes.

And now:

**ऊर्ध्व - ūrdhva**

Upper, upward.

**आश्रयण - āśrayaṇa**

Resorting to, resting upon, taking support.

Thus:

**ūrdhvāśrayaṇa**

An upward orientation or taking of a higher support.

The two vowel powers are treated as complementary expressions within the unfolding of Śiva's conscious activity.

But here's the really important point.

They are still vowels.

They don't become separate material objects just because their positions in Mālinī's sequence are differentiated.

Their function is symbolic.

And the commentary's whole argument concerns how such differentiation occurs within a universal conscious ground.


## 13. ब - Ba: The intellect becomes a divine womb

Now comes:

**ब - ba**

The voiced unaspirated labial.

In the Mātṛkā correspondence, *ba* is associated with:

**बुद्धि - buddhi**

The determinative intellect.

But in the Mālinī exposition, the intellect functions as another generative matrix through which divine self-awareness is expressed.

This is remarkable.

Because ordinarily, we think of the intellect as something belonging to the limited individual.

It makes decisions.

Distinguishes objects.

Determines what is true or false.

And sometimes spends three hours convincing you that reading more Sanskrit is more urgent than eating lunch.

But here Abhinavagupta interprets *buddhi* through the manifestation of Śiva's own awareness.

The intellect isn't being declared identical in every respect with the supreme consciousness.

Rather, its capacity for determination is understood as a dependent manifestation of that consciousness.

### Buddhi versus Bodha

These two are worth distinguishing.

**बुद्धि - buddhi**

Intellect, determinative cognition.

**बोध - bodha**

Awareness, understanding, knowledge.

Both belong to the root-family of √**budh**, to awaken or understand.

But their technical roles differ.

*Buddhi* often names a particular cognitive faculty.

*Bodha* can refer more broadly to knowing or awareness.

Abhinavagupta's doctrine insists that the limited faculty doesn't independently create universal consciousness.

And yet its operation isn't alien to consciousness.

The faculty is a manifestation of Śakti.

The distinction between limited cognition and supreme awareness remains.

This is exactly the kind of relationship the *sarvasarvātmakatva* doctrine was designed to explain.


## 14. क ख ग - Ka, Kha, Ga: Śiva reaches the gross elements without leaving himself

And finally, the first sixteen Mālinī phonemes end with:

# क

*ka*

Earth

# ख

*kha*

Water

# ग

*ga*

Fire

These are the familiar Mātṛkā correspondences within the earlier tattva mapping.

But here they occur within the **first sixteen Mālinī powers**, the sequence being interpreted as the internal manifestation of Śiva.

And that is astonishing.

Earth.

Water.

Fire.

The apparently gross elements.

But they're being used to articulate the supreme consciousness's own inner activity.

Now here's the philosophical problem.

How can a gross element be understood within a supreme level of awareness without turning Śiva into a physical heap of matter?

The answer is that we're dealing with **the conscious powers corresponding to those manifested forms**, not the claim that the physical Earth element has already become externally articulated within the supreme state.

The differentiations remain implicit in the conscious power.

They can be made manifest.

But their ultimate ground is not a physical object.

### The Sanskrit: Samāviṣṭa

**समाविष्ट - samāviṣṭa**

Entered into, pervaded, completely included.

From *sam-ā-√viś*, to enter.

The commentary describes the Śiva principle as entering or being present in these generative matrices.

Now the important point.

Śiva doesn't literally travel from a celestial location into a lump of soil.

The entry expresses the relation between universal consciousness and its differentiated manifestation.

The supreme reality is not absent from the gross elements.

But neither is it reduced to them.

And the first sixteen phonemes conclude by expressing that very relationship.

### Why the first sixteen are so surprising

Think about the ordinary Mātṛkā scheme.

The sixteen vowels represent the supreme field.

The consonants articulate the differentiated tattvas.

But in Mālinī, the first sixteen contain both vowels and consonants.

And consonants associated elsewhere with hearing, taste, touch, intellect, Earth, Water and Fire become expressions of Śiva's inner life.

This is not merely a reshuffling of positions.

**It's a transformation of the relationship between phoneme and ontological level.**

The same sound can participate in different manifestations depending upon the level at which it is interpreted.

And Abhinavagupta is about to develop precisely that through reflection.


## 15. The first reflection - Parā becomes manifest through Paśyantī

Now the prose changes direction.

Abhinavagupta introduces:

**पश्यन्तीरूपानुमूर्त्या - paśyantīrūpānumūrtyā**

Through a manifestation taking the form of Paśyantī.

The details of the compound are dense, but the main components are:

**पश्यन्ती - paśyantī**

The visionary level of speech.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form, nature.

**अनुमूर्ति - anumūrti**

A corresponding embodiment or manifestation.

The final **-yā** marks instrumental singular.

Now we've moved from the interpretation of the initial sixteen powers within Parā toward their manifestation in Paśyantī.

And the mirror theory from Part VIII returns.

Remember the point.

The reflection is not an independent substance.

But it can reveal the same underlying reality through a different arrangement.

Thus a sound associated with one faculty in Mātṛkā may appear through another faculty or principle in the reflected configuration.

The differences aren't being erased.

They're being interpreted through a wider account of manifestation.

### Na and Ṇa exchange roles

Let's start with the clearest example.

In one correspondence:

**न - na**

Hearing, *śrotra*.

And:

**ण - ṇa**

Speech, *vāk*.

But through the reflective account, the relationship reverses.

The hearing-power becomes associated with the expressive power.

The power of speaking becomes related back to hearing.

Why?

Because the relation between hearing and speaking is not ultimate separation.

Both are manifestations of the same conscious freedom.

Now be precise.

This doesn't mean hearing and speaking are empirically identical cognitive functions.

They aren't.

But their ultimate ground is the same.

The reversal is symbolically intelligible through the mirror-like manifestation of Śakti.

### Tha and Da

A second example:

**थ - tha**

The tongue or tasting faculty in one arrangement.

And:

**द - da**

The visual faculty in another position.

Through the reflection, the taste-associated power is related to the power of sight.

Again, these are not ordinary linguistic derivations.

There is no Sanskrit rule saying *tha* literally means "eye" because its consonant is reflected.

The correspondence belongs to the Tantric system.

And the philosophical meaning is that the differentiated sensory powers may be represented through alternative relations without escaping the one consciousness.

### Ca and Va

**च - ca**

Smell as a subtle quality in the Mātṛkā ordering.

**व - va**

A phoneme associated with Māyā in the earlier mapping.

The reflected interpretation brings the smell-associated power into relation with the operative power of differentiation.

Here the point is not merely sensory.

It's the way a particular manifestation becomes determinable through a wider field of cognitive power.

### Dha and Ta

**ध - dha**

Touch-faculty.

**त - ta**

Smell-faculty in the Mātṛkā mapping.

Another reflection of the relation between powers that are functionally distinct but not metaphysically independent.

Now I know what you're thinking.

This is starting to resemble somebody taking the tattva chart, feeding it into a cosmic blender and announcing that the result is still nonduality.

Fair criticism.

But the crucial point is that Abhinavagupta isn't claiming the conventional senses have randomly exchanged their empirical jobs.

He is interpreting the **reflected manifestation of the phonemic powers**.

The relationships are theological.

Their basis is the nondual consciousness in which all those powers arise.

### The remaining reflections

The sequence continues through the seed-powers, the divine speech-faculty and the intellect.

And it concludes by bringing the final three gross-element positions into relation with the high principles of Śakti, Sadāśiva, Īśvara and Śuddhavidyā.

Now that is the really extraordinary inversion.

What appears as gross-element manifestation in one arrangement corresponds through the reflected structure to high divine principles in another.

The universe is not being turned into an ordinary physical mirror image.

The *tattva* order is being interpreted through the relationship between supreme consciousness and its differentiated expression.

And Abhinavagupta states the conclusion in terms of the infinite Śakti of the Śiva principle, encompassing beginning, middle and end.

## 16. Sarvāgra, Sarvamadhya, Sarvāntya - Beginning, middle and end

Now we need to understand three important terms appearing in Abhinavagupta's concluding formulation.

**सर्वाग्र - sarvāgra**

At the beginning of all.

**सर्वमध्य - sarvamadhya**

In the middle of all.

**सर्वान्त्य - sarvāntya**

At the end of all.

And the compound:

**सर्वाग्रमध्यान्तगामित्व - sarvāgramadhyāntagāmitva**

The condition of going or extending through beginning, middle and end.

Let's actually dismantle that magnificent bastard.

*Sarva* - all.

*Agra* - beginning, front, foremost part.

*Madhya* - middle.

*Anta* - end.

*Gāmin* - going, extending, reaching.

*-tva* - abstract condition.

Thus the divine reality is described as extending through the entire order.

Now, modern explanatory tables distinguish three stages, sometimes called *sarvāgrarūpatā*, *sarvamadhyarūpatā* and *sarvāntyarūpatā*, to make the layered correspondence easier to follow.

Those tabular labels are useful editorial aids. The compact Sanskrit prose itself expresses the all-encompassing reach of Śiva through beginning, middle and end rather than supplying a neat classroom chart.

And the philosophical point is essential.

**Śiva isn't merely present at the first stage and absent from the rest.**

Nor does Śiva become fundamentally transformed into something unrelated to consciousness once differentiated manifestation occurs.

The supreme reality extends through every stage of the manifestation.

The beginning.

The middle.

The end.

And the reflective relations between them.

That's why the same sound can be interpreted differently at different levels.

And why the next thirty-four sounds require another account.

Now we've reached the second great movement of pp. 153-154.

The remaining alphabet.

And we're going to map the entire bloody thing.


## 17. The remaining thirty-four phonemes - Mālinī's complete second movement

Abhinavagupta now announces that the remaining sequence is to be understood through the differentiated conscious power operating in Mālinī, alongside the corresponding Paśyantī manifestation.

This is the dense material running through pp. 153-154.

First, here's the remaining sequence in its normalized order.

## 18. घ ङ इ अ - From Sadāśiva to Māyā

The sequence begins:

**घ - gha**

**ङ - ṅa**

**इ - i**

**अ - a**

And their Mālinī correspondences are:

Sadāśiva.

Īśvara.

Śuddhavidyā.

Māyā.

Notice the first three.

These are high divine principles.

And then Māyā appears.

The movement is from the more explicitly divine levels toward the power of differentiation.

But the sounds themselves have other roles within Mātṛkā.

### Gha - घ

A voiced aspirated velar consonant.

Compare:

**ग - ga**

Voiced, unaspirated.

**घ - gha**

Voiced, aspirated.

In the regular phonetic alphabet, they're neighbouring members of the velar class.

In the Mātṛkā tattva mapping, *gha* relates to Air.

But in Mālinī, *gha* expresses Sadāśiva.

Now, in the higher Śaiva epistemological account, Sadāśiva is associated with the predominance of universal I-consciousness and a comparatively less explicit manifestation of the object-pole.

The world is not yet apprehended as something fully separate.

The corresponding sound therefore participates in an entirely different philosophical relation from its lower-level elemental assignment.

### Ṅa - ङ

The velar nasal.

You've encountered this sound inside:

**अङ्ग - aṅga**

Limb, component.

And:

**सङ्घ - saṅgha**

Assembly, community.

Its written form is:

**ङ**

Don't confuse it with:

**ञ - ña**

The palatal nasal.

Or:

**ण - ṇa**

The retroflex nasal.

Now in the Mālinī scheme, *ṅa* is associated with Īśvara.

In the standard high tattva interpretation, Īśvara emphasizes the manifestation of the objective pole within universal consciousness.

The "this" becomes more explicit.

And that prepares the transition to Śuddhavidyā.

### I - इ

The short vowel *i*.

In this arrangement, it corresponds to Śuddhavidyā.

This is particularly interesting because the vowel belongs to the divine vowel-series in the Mātṛkā arrangement.

But here the same phoneme is associated with a specific high tattva within differentiated manifestation.

The symbolic level has changed.

And now the next sound is even more dramatic.

### A - अ

The first vowel.

Anuttara.

Our old friend.

In the original symbolic sequence, the very first expression of the unsurpassable.

But in Mālinī, it is associated with Māyā.

Now imagine the Absolute looking at its own first vowel and finding that the Goddess has assigned it to the department of differentiation.

"Excuse me. That was supposed to be my primordial phoneme."

"Yes, Lord. And now it expresses Māyā."

Magnificent.

But the point isn't that Anuttara has become trapped in ignorance.

The phoneme is being considered at a different level of manifestation.

Māyā doesn't exist outside universal consciousness.

Therefore the sound expressing Māyā can still participate in the supreme divine reality.

**The change of correspondence reflects a mode of manifestation, not the destruction of Anuttara.**

This is exactly what Abhinavagupta's mirror theory was preparing us to understand.


## 19. व भ य - The limitations begin to unfold

Now:

**व - va**

**भ - bha**

**य - ya**

These correspond in Mālinī to:

Niyati.

Kāla.

Rāga.

And now we've entered the limiting powers.

### Niyati - नियति

Constraint, necessity, determinate ordering.

From a formation involving *ni-* and √*yam*, to restrain or regulate.

In the tattva system, Niyati limits the universal freedom by manifesting a restricted order of relationships and circumstances.

Now don't reduce it to ordinary fatalism.

It isn't simply the claim that everything you do is predetermined and free choice is an illusion.

Its role is more technical.

It is one of the ways universal freedom manifests conditioned experience.

### Kāla - काल

Time.

The limitation by which experience is situated within temporal succession.

Past.

Present.

Future.

This is not the same as the absolute consciousness being itself a product of time.

The limitation belongs to the manifested structure.

### Rāga - राग

Attachment, desire, attraction.

From √*rañj*, to colour or become affected.

The word has an enormous range of meanings, including colour, passion and musical modes.

But in the tattva system it concerns the restricted subject's orientation toward something as desirable.

Now remember the three malas.

The limited subject experiences incompleteness.

Objects become distinguished as separate sources of possible satisfaction.

And action proceeds through attachment and aversion.

The limiting power of Rāga is deeply connected with that structure.

So the phonemic sequence has moved from the divine levels into the powers through which the finite experiencer is conditioned.

But the limitation is still a manifestation of the same Śakti.

And Abhinavagupta's point is that the correspondence can change without creating a second ultimate reality.

The universal consciousness is not under attack by its own phonemes.


## 20. ड ढ ठ - Knowledge, action and the limited Puruṣa

Now we reach three retroflex aspirated and unaspirated consonants.

**ड - ḍa**

**ढ - ḍha**

**ठ - ṭha**

And their Mālinī assignments are:

Vidyā.

Kalā.

Puruṣa.

### Vidyā - विद्या

Knowledge.

But in the limiting-tattva context, this is **restricted knowledge**.

Not the unlimited knowing of Śiva.

The limited individual knows some things rather than all things.

Now distinguish:

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowing, knowledge.

**विद्या - vidyā**

Knowledge, learning, and in particular technical contexts a specific power or tattva.

The two aren't automatically interchangeable.

### Kalā - कला

A portion, aspect, operative capacity.

In this context, limited agency.

The universal freedom of Śiva manifests as an individual who can perform some actions but not all possible actions.

Again, don't confuse:

**कला - kalā**

Limited capacity.

With:

**काल - kāla**

Time.

They differ in vowel length and their consonantal ending.

One is *ka-lā*.

The other *kā-la*.

And in the tattva system, they explain different limitations.

### Puruṣa - पुरुष

The finite conscious subject.

Now here we finally reach the individual experiencer.

The one who knows.

Acts.

Desires.

Experiences temporal succession.

And regards himself as bounded by a particular body and mind.

But remember what Abhinavagupta's doctrine insists.

Puruṣa isn't an independent substance outside the reality of Śiva.

It's consciousness manifest under limitation.

And in the Mālinī arrangement, this position belongs to *ṭha*, rather than the familiar *ma* correspondence in the regular tattva mapping.

That is precisely the sort of difference the commentary is explaining.

The limited individual appears in a new phonemic position.

But the nature of universal consciousness hasn't changed.


## 21. झ ञ ज र - The cognitive machinery emerges

Now the sequence continues.

**झ - jha**

**ञ - ña**

**ज - ja**

**र - ra**

And these correspond to:

Prakṛti.

Buddhi.

Ahaṃkāra.

Manas.

Beautiful.

We've arrived at the psychological and material structures familiar from the Sāṃkhya-influenced portion of the tattva system.

### Prakṛti - प्रकृति

The primordial productive nature of the differentiated object-field.

Not a single physical object.

Not merely nature in the ordinary ecological sense.

A technical principle explaining the emergence of the lower cognitive and material structures.

### Buddhi - बुद्धि

The determinative intellect.

The faculty through which objects and relations are decisively ascertained.

You see an unclear shape.

You determine it's a tree.

That determination involves the kind of function associated with Buddhi.

### Ahaṃkāra - अहङ्कार

The ego-making function.

Now here's a lovely compound.

**अहम् - aham**

I.

**कार - kāra**

Making, producing, forming, related to √*kṛ*.

Thus:

**अहङ्कार - ahaṃkāra**

I-making.

The function through which experience is appropriated as belonging to a particular individual.

Notice the nasal in the Devanāgarī.

**ङ् - ṅ**

The form involves assimilation of the final nasal before the velar *k*.

And it isn't simply written as the independent word *aham* followed unchanged by *kāra* in ordinary continuous Sanskrit.

The phonological form reflects the combination.

Now here's the philosophical point.

Ahaṃkāra is not the supreme *aham*.

The limited ego-making function is not identical in its role to universal I-consciousness.

This distinction is absolutely essential to Trika.

Otherwise people read "I am Śiva" and immediately conclude that their personal biography and preferences are omnipotent.

No.

The universal subjectivity is the ground of the limited ego-function.

The ego is not a separate ultimate reality.

But it is also not simply identical with Śiva's unlimited agency as a psychological individual.

### Manas - मनस्

Mind.

The coordinating and processing mental faculty.

Now the Mālinī sequence has unfolded the structures through which the limited subject apprehends the world.

Prakṛti.

Buddhi.

Ahaṃkāra.

Manas.

And their manifestation through different phonemes illustrates how consciousness can express its cognitive functions in a particular order.

But the order is not the supreme reality.

It's a manifestation.

Which is why Abhinavagupta can compare it with another ordering.

Next:

**प - pa**

**छ - cha**

**ल - la**

**आ - ā**

And their Mālinī correspondences are:

Hearing.

Touch.

Sight.

Taste.

Smell.

Now that's a lovely fivefold group, even though it does not coincide with a single ordinary phonetic *varga*.

### Śrotra - श्रोत्र

The auditory faculty.

Related to √*śru*, to hear.

### Tvak - त्वक्

Skin, the faculty of touch.

Notice the final virāma:

**क् - k**

### Cakṣus - चक्षुस्

### Jihvā - जिह्वा

Tongue, tasting faculty.

### Ghrāṇa - घ्राण

Smelling faculty.

From the same broad root-family as √*ghrā*, to smell.

Notice the conjunct:

**घ्र = घ् + र**

Then the long ā.

So:

*ghrā-ṇa*.

Now here's the interesting thing.

In the ordinary Mātṛkā mapping, these five faculties occupy the dental and related phonetic positions we've already studied.

But here Mālinī arranges them as:

ṭa, pa, cha, la, ā.

One long vowel among four consonants.

The Goddess is not interested in preserving your conventional phonetic categories.

But the five senses remain five differentiated functions.

So this is not a denial of the sensory system.

It's another symbolic organization of it.

And this is why **bhinnayoni** matters.

Mālinī's vowels and consonants are interwoven in a different structure.

The divine expressive power isn't confined to the arrangement used by ordinary Sanskrit phonetics.


## 23. स अः ह ष क्ष - The five faculties of action

Now:

**स - sa**

**अः - aḥ**

**ह - ha**

**ष - ṣa**

**क्ष - kṣa**

Corresponding in Mālinī to:

Speech.

Hands.

Feet.

Excretion.

Generation.

The five *karmendriyas*, faculties of action.

And here's something particularly surprising.

The phoneme **अः - aḥ**, associated in the ordinary vowel-series with *visarga*, is here assigned to the faculty of the hands.

Now that's quite a transition.

From cosmic emission to the hand.

But remember the theological point.

The same phonemic power can be interpreted in a distinct mode of manifestation.

### Vāk - वाक्

Speech.

And:

**वाच् - vāc**

The related stem appearing in other formations.

From √*vac*, to speak.

### Pāṇi - पाणि

Hand.

### Pāda - पाद

Foot.

### Pāyu - पायु

The faculty of excretion.

### Upastha - उपस्थ

The generative faculty.

Now, in the ordinary tattva scheme, these are the five powers of action available to the limited subject.

But within Mālinī, they occupy positions including *visarga* and the terminal *kṣa*.

This is exactly why the simple identification:

"kṣa always means Śakti"

cannot be maintained at every level.

The meaning of the correspondence depends upon the arrangement.

And now the distinction between *visarga* as a written sign and *visarga* as metaphysical emission becomes important again.

**अः** is the written representation.

The Tantric significance of emission is an interpretive layer.

And the assignment to a faculty of action belongs to another layer of the sacred mapping.

They're related within the system.

But not identical kinds of explanation.

The five subtle qualities remain recognizable.

But their phonemic assignments change.

### The five subtle qualities are not the five physical senses

This is worth emphasizing.

*Śrotra* is the faculty of hearing.

*Śabda* is sound as a subtle quality.

*Cakṣus* is the faculty of seeing.

The tattva system distinguishes the instrument of cognition from the corresponding quality.

That's why there are two separate fivefold groups.

And finally.

**द - da**

### Why *pha*?

In this system, *pha* occupies the position of Pṛthivī.

But it would be a mistake to invent a historical linguistic derivation claiming that the sound *pha* naturally means Earth in every Sanskrit context.

It does not.

Its association here is part of Mālinī's sacred phonemic arrangement.

And that is exactly the point.

The same Earth tattva can be represented differently within different divine and ritual configurations.

The universal reality isn't confined to one compulsory sound sequence.

### The final cosmic punchline

Imagine learning the regular Mātṛkā alphabet.

**क - ka = Earth.**

Excellent.

Then you study the reverse arrangement.

Now:

**क्ष - kṣa = Earth.**

Fine.

Then Mālinī enters.

**फ - pha = Earth.**

And you throw your notebook into the Ganges.

But Abhinavagupta's argument is precisely designed to prevent that.

The different arrangements represent distinct modes of manifesting and interpreting the tattva-order.

**The fact that the phonemic position changes does not imply the ontological principle has become an entirely different independent substance.**

And because universal consciousness is free, its powers can manifest through multiple coherent configurations.

That is how he interprets the difference.

Not through arbitrary historical etymology.

Not by pretending the charts are identical.

But through the metaphysical relation between Śiva, Śakti and differentiated expression.

## 27. Sarvasarvātmakatva has been established

At the end of the phonemic mapping on p. 154, Abhinavagupta returns to the doctrine from Part VIII:

**सर्वसर्वात्मकत्वम्**

*Sarvasarvātmakatvam.*

The condition of the whole being implicated in everything.

And he says that the explanation has now established this relation.

Let's look at why.

The same sound has a position in Mātṛkā.

A different position in Mālinī.

And additional manifestations through the reflective levels.

The sound isn't treated as an entirely independent substance whose meaning is necessarily fixed by one position.

Rather, its role depends upon the broader conscious manifestation.

And the corresponding tattvas aren't independently self-sufficient realities.

Their differences are real within the system.

But the underlying ground of their manifestation is one.

### Nirvyūḍha

**निर्व्यूढ - nirvyūḍha**

Carried through, worked out, established or demonstrated.

Related to a verbal formation involving *nir-* and √*vah*, to carry or conduct.

Abhinavagupta's point is that the principle has been made intelligible through the detailed correspondences.

Not merely asserted.

Now this matters because the entire problem began with apparently contradictory maps.

The maps were different.

How could they all participate in an authentic account of Śiva's powers?

The answer lies in the nondual reality of those powers, and the different modes through which they can become manifest.

But here's the crucial subtlety.

**Nonduality does not mean every mapping has the same function in every ritual context.**

The differences remain meaningful.

And that's why Abhinavagupta's next argument is so important.

He's going to explain why a particular arrangement can produce one traditional result while another arrangement is associated with a different result.

But before that, he gives us a beautiful summary of Mālinī's manifestation.


## 28. Parā becomes Mālinī - The entire universe reflects into its own expressive power

Now we're at the end of p. 154.

And this is one of the most beautiful transitions in the whole commentary.

Abhinavagupta identifies the supreme Goddess, Parā, as the reality whose own manifestation becomes reflected in Paśyantī, and whose differentiated expression takes the form of Mālinī within the domain of Madhyamā.

Let's understand the movement.

**Parā** is supreme consciousness, undivided in its own nature.

**Paśyantī** is the manifestation in which differentiation begins to become apprehensible.

**Madhyamā** is the domain in which the relation between expression and expressed meaning becomes articulated more distinctly.

And Mālinī is the differentiated generative arrangement of the phonemic powers.

But this is not three independent goddesses performing an elaborate relay race.

It's the same supreme conscious power manifesting through distinguishable modes.

### The Sanskrit: Pratibimba

**प्रतिबिम्ब - pratibimba**

Reflection.

We've already learned:

*bimba* - original image.

*pratibimba* - reflected image.

Now the important thing.

In ordinary physical reflection, the object and mirror are distinct physical entities.

But Abhinavagupta's metaphysical claim is stronger.

The reflected appearance of the universe isn't ultimately a second substance independent of consciousness.

It is consciousness appearing through a differentiated form.

Thus Parā can be said to manifest her own reflection in Paśyantī without ceasing to be Parā.

### Tādātmya

**तादात्म्य - tādātmya**

Identity of nature.

Remember:

*tat* - that.

*ātman* - self.

The derivative expresses identity with the underlying referent.

And the commentary says that the Madhyamā domain remains established in identity with her own nature.

This is essential.

Mālinī isn't an independent machine manufacturing a universe from foreign materials.

She is Parā manifesting through differentiated phonemic expression.

### The incredible compound: Yonibīja-paraspara-saṃbheda

Now here's one of the most important constructions in the passage.

**योनिबीजपरस्परसम्भेद - yonibījaparasparasaṃbheda**

The mutual intermingling of womb and seed powers.

Let's unpack it.

**योनि - yoni**

Womb, generative matrix.

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

**परस्पर - paraspara**

Mutual, reciprocal, one another.

**सम्भेद - saṃbheda**

Combination, intermingling, interpenetration; in other contexts, differentiation.

And now:

**वैचित्र्य - vaicitrya**

Variety, diversity, manifoldness.

The commentary attributes an inexhaustible variety to the possible mutual combinations of seed and matrix.

And what does that mean?

The phonemic powers can be combined in different ways.

Those combinations express different modes of divine manifestation.

The combinations aren't simply arbitrary external modifications.

They're grounded in the same universal freedom.

### Why Mālinī is inexhaustible

Imagine an alphabet.

You have a finite inventory of letters.

But those letters can be combined into an enormous range of words, sentences and meanings.

The inventory is finite.

The expressive possibilities are extraordinarily extensive.

Now imagine Abhinavagupta interpreting that expressive possibility as a manifestation of Śakti.

That is the analogy.

The divine power isn't limited to one predetermined arrangement of phonemes.

Different combinations can disclose different aspects of manifestation.

Yet their ground remains the same consciousness.

The particular Mālinī sequence is therefore one expression of a much more comprehensive creative freedom.

**The arrangement is definite, but the underlying expressive power is inexhaustible.**

And now comes a scriptural description that captures this beautifully.


## 29. Mālinī is surrounded by an infinity of divine families

Abhinavagupta quotes a verse describing the Goddess surrounded by innumerable *kula*-bodies and *kula*-powers.

The important expression is:

**अनन्तैः कुलदेहैः ... कुलशक्तिभिः**

*Anantaiḥ kuladehaiḥ ... kulaśaktibhiḥ.*

By endless bodies of Kula and powers of Kula.

The verse presents Mālinī as a goddess whose manifestation is surrounded by this vast divine family.

Now let's study the words.

### Anantaiḥ

**अनन्त - ananta**

Endless, infinite.

From:

*an-* \+ *anta*.

*Anta* means end or limit.

Thus *ananta* means without end.

Now the instrumental plural:

**अनन्तैः - anantaiḥ**

By or with endless ones.

The ending **-aiḥ** is important.

It's not merely a decorative final sound.

The case tells us how the noun or adjective relates to the rest of the sentence.

### Kuladeha

**कुलदेह - kuladeha**

A Kula-body, a body belonging to or constituted within the divine family.

**कुल - kula**

Family, collection, totality.

**देह - deha**

Body.

Now, in the explanatory tradition associated with this passage, *kuladeha* may refer to the vowel or seed-side of the divine phonemic arrangement.

And:

### Kulaśakti

**कुलशक्ति - kulaśakti**

The power of Kula.

In the associated phonemic interpretation, this can be correlated with the consonantal or matrix-side.

But don't turn this into a simple universal dictionary rule.

*Kulaśakti* doesn't always mean "consonant" in every Śaiva text.

Here the context is the sacred phonemic arrangement.

And the philosophical picture is extraordinary.

Mālinī is not merely one isolated Goddess surrounded by a collection of independent servants.

She is the one power manifesting innumerable configurations of her own generative capacities.

The "family" is the differentiated totality of those powers.

And their reality is inseparable from her.

### Why the garland metaphor is so appropriate

Think of a garland.

Different flowers.

Different colours.

Different shapes.

And yet they are strung together as one arrangement.

Change the order of the flowers.

The garland changes.

But the underlying materials have not suddenly become unrelated substances.

Now, once again, the analogy has limits.

The universe is not literally composed of flowers strung on a physical thread.

But the image expresses the relation between differentiated elements and their organized unity.

Mālinī is a garland of divine powers.

And the permutations of those powers are practically inexhaustible within the theological system.

Now the mantras become even more interesting.

Because if different arrangements express different powers, does every possible arrangement produce every possible result?

And that is where Abhinavagupta makes an absolutely crucial qualification.


## 30. No, every sound combination does not grant every siddhi

Now we're at p. 155.

Abhinavagupta discusses the traditional claim that a practitioner engaged in the appropriate forms of contemplation and ritual can obtain particular *siddhis*.

But this raises an obvious objection.

If all phonemic powers are manifestations of the same supreme freedom, why should some combinations be associated with particular results and not others?

Why couldn't you take any random syllables, chant them, and obtain any conceivable power?

And now Abhinavagupta introduces the most wonderfully ordinary comparison.

**Medicinal plants.**

Yes.

After all that cosmic phonemic machinery, we're back in the garden.

### Auṣadhi - औषधि

**औषधि - auṣadhi**

A medicinal plant, herb, medicine.

The plural feminine form:

**औषध्यः - auṣadhyaḥ**

Medicinal plants.

The commentary says that particular medicinal plants arise and produce particular effects.

Likewise, particular ritual or contemplative arrangements are traditionally associated with particular results.

Now, this doesn't mean that all traditional medicinal or supernatural claims are established facts.

The comparison concerns **specificity of effect within a causal or operative system**.

Different configurations have different functions.

And the existence of a universal ground does not eliminate those functions.

### The very important distinction

Consider tea.

Different leaves contain different mixtures of compounds.

Processing, brewing time and temperature affect the resulting drink.

You cannot assume every leaf steeped in hot water produces exactly the same chemical composition.

Now, the comparison with Tantric mantra is symbolic, not proof that mantra operates through the same kind of empirically measurable mechanism.

But the philosophical point is straightforward.

**A shared ultimate ground does not imply identical function at the level of differentiation.**

All sounds may be interpreted as expressions of consciousness.

Yet particular phonemic arrangements can have distinct roles within a transmitted ritual system.

### The Sanskrit: Bhāvanā

**भावना - bhāvanā**

Cultivation, contemplative formation, imaginative realization.

Related to the causative formation of √*bhū*, to be or become.

And:

**मन्त्रन्यास - mantranyāsa**

Placement or installation of mantra.

And:

**होम - homa**

Ritual oblation or offering into fire.

These appear as traditional forms of practice in the passage.

Abhinavagupta is not claiming every combination of them works identically.

Their application depends upon the particular system.

This is where the doctrine of *niyati*, restriction or determinate order, becomes important again.

### Niyati returns

**नियति - niyati**

Determinate ordering, limitation, restriction.

Now think about the irony.

We have spent chapters establishing divine freedom.

And now Abhinavagupta says there is still a relevant order governing particular manifestations.

Does that contradict the earlier doctrine?

No.

The universal freedom includes the capacity to manifest determinate relations.

At the level of ultimate consciousness, there's no external necessity imposed upon Śiva.

At the level of particular manifestation, there can be definite relationships and constraints.

So:

**Freedom of manifestation does not entail randomness of manifestation.**

That's the distinction.

And it's how he avoids the ridiculous conclusion that every arbitrary sound sequence must possess every possible effect.

Imagine someone proclaiming:

"Because all letters are Śakti, I can chant the ingredients on a shampoo bottle and attain Khecarī-samatā."

Abhinavagupta's answer would require you to establish the actual relationship between the chosen phonemic configuration, its transmitted role and the intended realization.

You cannot simply invoke nonduality to dispense with every distinction.

Which is the same point he made earlier when criticizing people who appealed to divine will to avoid philosophical investigation.

The man is remarkably consistent.


## 31. Varṇa-prastāra - Why different scriptures begin with different letters

Now the commentary explains the specificity of the different sacred arrangements.

The important term is:

**वर्णप्रस्तार - varṇaprastāra**

The spreading out or systematic arrangement of phonemes.

Break it down:

**वर्ण - varṇa**

Letter, phoneme, colour, class.

**प्रस्तार - prastāra**

Expansion, spreading out, arrangement.

Related to a formation involving *pra-* and √*stṝ*, to spread or extend.

Together:

**varṇaprastāra**

An arrangement or unfolding of the phonemic series.

The commentary explains that different scriptures have distinct arrangements according to the divine phonemic power emphasized within their own systems.

The first letter matters.

The ordering matters.

The grouping matters.

And the corresponding mantra is understood through that ordered configuration.

### Varṇaprāthāmya

**वर्णप्राथम्य - varṇaprāthamya**

The condition of a letter being first or predominant.

From:

**प्रथम - prathama**

First.

And the abstract formation:

**प्राथम्य - prāthamya**

Priority, firstness.

So the expression concerns which phoneme occupies the primary position.

Now this is fascinating.

In Mātṛkā, the familiar beginning is:

**अ - a**

But in Mālinī:

**न - na**

And the specific organization of the system is related to that chosen ordering.

This doesn't mean all ordinary Sanskrit words beginning with *na* suddenly become Mālinī mantras.

It's a structured sacred sequence, not a dictionary coincidence.

### The Nityātantra example

Abhinavagupta introduces a specific arrangement associated with the *Nityātantras*.

Here the relevant divine power is connected with the vowel **e**, and with a particular mantraic ordering related to the supreme resonance, *paranāda*.

The text contains a variant reading in the accompanying explanatory note concerning the exact phrase referring to the causal and supreme Nāda structure.

I'm not going to force that variant into one supposedly certain original compound.

The secure philosophical point is that the ordering depends upon the predominance of a particular mantraic power.

The arrangement begins and ends according to that transmitted system.

And the *kula-puruṣas* and *kula-śaktis* are ordered accordingly.

Now you can see how this differs from arbitrary convention.

The letter sequence is not chosen merely because somebody prefers the sound of one consonant over another.

Its placement belongs to an established theological and ritual structure.

Yet that structure is one manifestation within the universal freedom of Śakti.

Thus the apparent contradiction between the different sacred alphabets is not simply ignored.

It is contextualized.


## 32. The purpose is not merely to hide a secret alphabet

Now we're at p. 156.

And Abhinavagupta makes a very important statement.

The purpose of these arrangements isn't merely to conceal letters or mantras from outsiders.

**There is a deeper reason for the configuration.**

This matters because Tantric texts frequently employ secrecy.

The mantras may be transmitted through initiation.

Their arrangements may be encoded.

Their significances may be restricted to particular lineages.

But if the entire purpose were simply to make the sounds difficult to identify, that wouldn't explain their philosophical and ritual significance.

Abhinavagupta cites another scripture, the *Vājasaneyatantra*, describing the properly known **Mātṛkācakra** as capable of cutting the bonds of the limited subject.

### Mātṛkācakra - मातृकाचक्र

**मातृका - mātṛkā**

The alphabetic Mother-power.

**चक्र - cakra**

Wheel, circle, organized system.

Together:

**मातृकाचक्र - mātṛkācakra**

The wheel or circle of the alphabetic Mother.

Now the quoted text connects knowledge of this arrangement with instruction received from the guru.

### Gurumukhāt

**गुरुमुखात् - gurumukhāt**

From the mouth of the guru.

Break it down.

**गुरु - guru**

Teacher.

**मुख - mukha**

Mouth, face.

Thus:

*gurumukhāt*

From the mouth of the teacher.

And the text emphasizes knowing the arrangement properly through such instruction.

Now the distinction is important.

This is a historical claim about the efficacy of sacred knowledge transmitted in a Tantric lineage.

It doesn't independently demonstrate that every supernatural effect attributed to the system occurs as described.

But it establishes what the tradition considers crucial.

**The arrangement isn't merely a visible string of symbols. Its meaning and function belong to a transmitted body of understanding.**

And that leads directly into the next quotation.


## 33. The great statement of the Trikahṛdaya - Letters without power are merely letters

Now Abhinavagupta cites the *Trikahṛdaya*.

And this is one of the most memorable teachings in the whole passage.

The scripture distinguishes the outward arrangement of letters from the operative power present in the mantra.

Its conclusion is beautifully forceful.

The power within the mantra is what matters; otherwise, the remaining sounds are merely letters.

Now, that is a profound distinction.

Not because ordinary letters are worthless.

But because the tradition distinguishes the visible or audible form from the deeper divine potency attributed to it.

### Mantravīrya - मन्त्रवीर्य

**मन्त्र - mantra**

Mantra.

**वीर्य - vīrya**

Potency, power, strength.

Thus:

**मन्त्रवीर्य - mantravīrya**

The potency of a mantra.

Remember that *vīrya* can mean several things in Sanskrit, including vigour, power and generative potency.

Its meaning here concerns the operative potency of sacred speech.

And the associated word:

**स्फुरत्ता - sphurattā**

Vibrant manifestation, shining or pulsating presence.

Related to √*sphur*, to flash, throb or manifest.

Now look at the philosophical point.

A sound can be articulated.

Its phonemes can be recognized.

It can be written down.

But the Tantric tradition does not reduce the full reality of mantra to the bare physical production of sound.

The mantra is understood through a relation to consciousness and divine Śakti.

### The danger of confusing the symbol with the realization

Imagine someone finding a sacred mantra in a book.

They memorize it perfectly.

They learn the pronunciation.

They can write it beautifully in Devanāgarī.

But they have no understanding of its meaning, its ritual context, or the realization toward which the tradition directs it.

Now have they learned the sound?

Yes.

Have they necessarily realized the consciousness represented by the mantra?

No.

That's the distinction.

And this is exactly why the commentary is so relevant to the way we're learning Sanskrit.

You can understand the morphology.

You can pronounce the phonemes.

You can learn the transmitted symbolic meanings.

But Abhinavagupta is ultimately concerned with recognition of the conscious reality expressed through the system.

Not merely with accumulating a collection of sacred sounds.

### The deeper point: Mantra belongs to Parā Vāk

Remember Part I.

Parā Vāk is the supreme expressive power.

The outward articulated form is Vaikharī.

The two are not ultimately separate.

But they're not identical in every functional respect.

The articulated sound is one manifestation of divine speech.

The supreme power is its ground.

The philosophical task is to recognize that relationship.

And that's why the scriptural teaching insists upon the inner potency of mantra rather than treating the arrangement of letters as independently sufficient.


## 34. The same syllable can have different roles in different traditions

Abhinavagupta now makes the argument even more explicit.

He mentions the same or closely related sacred phonemes appearing in different Śaiva and non-Śaiva scriptural settings.

Their roles vary.

He names examples involving:

**मायाबीज - māyābīja**

The seed-syllable associated with Māyā or divine power.

**प्रणव - praṇava**

The sacred syllable Oṃ and its theological interpretations.

**अमृतबीज - amṛtabīja**

The seed of immortality or nectar, depending on the system.

And a four-*kalā* divine form, treated differently across several traditions.

Now here's the crucial point.

The same phonetic material may appear in more than one theological and ritual arrangement.

And its interpretation can vary according to the *upāya*, deity, lineage or scripture.

This is not merely an inconvenience.

It's part of the diversity of manifestation.

### Āṇava, Śākta and Śāmbhava

We've studied these before.

**आणव - āṇava**

Pertaining to the limited individual.

**शाक्त - śākta**

Pertaining to Śakti.

**शाम्भव - śāmbhava**

Pertaining to Śambhu, Śiva.

Now the text explicitly invokes differences among these categories of practice.

And the implication is important.

The same sound can be employed at different levels of spiritual means.

The phoneme doesn't necessarily change its ordinary acoustic identity.

But its role within the spiritual system may differ.

### Does that make meaning arbitrary?

No.

Not according to Abhinavagupta.

The variation belongs to the manifestation of divine powers through definite arrangements.

Those arrangements are interpreted within their respective scriptural contexts.

And the reality of those powers ultimately depends upon one universal consciousness.

So the proper relationship isn't:

"All sounds mean exactly the same thing."

Nor:

"Every different sound means a completely independent metaphysical reality."

It's:

**The differentiated sounds and their relations are manifestations of one expressive power, whose specific configurations have distinct meanings and functions within the tradition.**

That is a much more sophisticated doctrine.

And it's why the entire discussion of Mālinī was necessary.


## 35. Now the philosophical punchline becomes obvious

Let's gather the whole movement of Part IX.

We started with the problem of Mālinī's differentiated alphabet.

The first sixteen phonemes were interpreted as expressions of Śiva's inner conscious activity.

The remaining thirty-four were assigned to distinct tattvas.

But those same phonemes also had positions in the Mātṛkā order.

And the reflection through Paśyantī allowed multiple relationships to be understood.

So the letters were not independent objects with one compulsory metaphysical definition.

Their correspondences were expressions of divine manifestation.

And once we reached the full Mālinī sequence, Abhinavagupta explained how the Goddess herself is the differentiated power through which those arrangements become possible.

Then came the crucial qualification.

Not every possible arrangement is necessarily associated with every possible ritual result.

Specific configurations have particular transmitted functions.

Those functions belong to the tradition's account of mantra and Śakti.

And the efficacy attributed to mantra is not reduced to its outward phonetic form.

The inner power is essential.

Now here's the extraordinary coherence.

**The same argument has been operating throughout.**

The tattvas are distinguishable, but not ultimately independent.

The phonemes are distinguishable, but not ultimately independent.

The levels of speech are distinguishable, but not ultimately independent.

The guru and disciple are distinguishable, but not ultimately independent.

The knower and known are distinguishable, but not ultimately independent.

Everything is being interpreted through the one self-luminous, freely manifesting consciousness of Bhairava.

### Why this matters for learning Sanskrit

Now consider what we've actually been doing.

We've learned:

- How vowels and consonants are written.
- How Sanskrit case endings distinguish grammatical roles.
- How sandhi transforms words in continuous speech.
- How compounds compress philosophical relationships.
- How the Tantric tradition interprets the sounds themselves as divine powers.

And here's the really important distinction.

**Understanding the conventional Sanskrit doesn't automatically establish the metaphysical interpretation.**

You can correctly explain *śaktau* as the locative singular of *śakti* without believing that mantra possesses supernatural power.

You can correctly distinguish *na* from *ṇa* without accepting Mālinī's phonemic cosmology as an empirical fact.

And you can understand Abhinavagupta's philosophical argument without pretending every correspondence is historically derived from Sanskrit phonology.

That's what makes the study intellectually serious.

We're learning what the author argues.

How he argues.

Which meanings are conventional.

Which are esoteric.

And what philosophical commitments make the interpretation coherent within Trika.

That's much better than vague mystical word association.


## 36. The decisive distinction - The sound isn't the Goddess, but neither is it outside her

This is where I want to end the philosophical reading.

Imagine an ordinary sound.

A consonant.

**क - ka**

You pronounce it.

Your vocal tract performs the appropriate articulation.

A sound wave travels through the air.

Someone hears it.

Their auditory system processes it.

They recognize the syllable.

They may associate it with a Sanskrit word.

Or with a tattva.

Or with a mantra.

Now the conventional description can be explored through phonetics, physiology, acoustics and linguistics.

Abhinavagupta adds a different kind of explanation.

He asks:

What is the consciousness within which the sound is manifested, apprehended and recognized?

And what is the ultimate nature of the expressive power through which meaningful differentiation becomes possible?

His answer is that the entire process depends upon the self-revealing consciousness of Śiva and the expressive power of Śakti.

Now that doesn't mean the physical sound wave is literally a goddess in the same ordinary sense that a person is a person.

Nor that the written character possesses independently demonstrable supernatural agency.

It means that the **ultimate reality of the manifestation**, according to his metaphysical system, is not separate from consciousness.

And the sacred alphabet provides a symbolic system for understanding that manifestation.

That's why Mālinī is so much more than a shuffled sequence of letters.

She is the differentiated expression of the one supreme power.

The garland of consciousness.

The generative arrangement of Śakti.

And the relation between signifier and signified.

The letters can be different.

Their arrangements can be different.

Their ritual applications can be different.

But their ultimate reality is not divided into fifty independently existing consciousnesses.

**The Goddess remains one in the very multiplicity of her expression.**

And that is the philosophical heart of the passage.

### Three Devanāgarī patterns worth writing

**1. The Mālinī-Mātṛkā contrast**

## न - ण - ङ - ञ

na - ṇa - ṅa - ña

Four different nasal consonants. Dental, retroflex, velar and palatal respectively. Their forms and places of articulation differ.


**2. An optative plus sandhi**

## न्यसेत् + शाक्त → न्यसेच्छाक्त

nyaset + śākta → nyasec chākta

This is why you must recover the original words rather than reading every visible conjunct as part of a single lexical item.


**3. The locative of Śakti**

## शक्तिः → शक्तौ

śaktiḥ → śaktau

Nominative singular to locative singular. Remember that **-au** isn't always a dual ending.
