# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa - Part XXXV

The Concluding Epilogue: Abhinavagupta, His Companions, and the People for Whom He Wrote

## “Śiva Has Finished Explaining the Universe. Now Abhinavagupta Would Like a Word About His Friends, His Family, and Why Some People Cannot Be Bothered to Think.”

We have reached the end of the philosophical commentary.

Bhairava's teaching has concluded. The Heart-mantra has been explained. The universe has unfolded from consciousness and returned to its ground. The final yogic instructions have distinguished immediate recognition from the pursuit of particular accomplishments.

And Abhinavagupta has closed his explanation with:

**सततोदितं ह्येतत्सर्वस्येति शिवम् ।**

*satatoditaṃ hy etat sarvasyeti śivam.*

“For this is ever manifest for everyone. Thus, auspiciousness.”

But the manuscript continues.

Now the great philosopher stops interpreting Bhairava's words and begins speaking in his own voice.

We are about to meet the people around him: a royal minister, the minister's son, a deeply devoted woman, her son, Abhinavagupta's own brother, and another scholar with a rather formidable command of Sanskrit intellectual disciplines.

And, for the first time in the ending, we shall hear Abhinavagupta explaining **why he wrote this work at all**.

### One important correction: there are twenty-one concluding verses, not ten

The 1918 Kashmir Series edition and Somadeva Vasudeva's GRETIL transcription both preserve **twenty-one numbered concluding authorial verses** after *iti śivam*. The first ten form a natural initial group, concluding with a wish that the work guide everyone toward Śiva. Verses 11-21 continue with Abhinavagupta's ancestry, his identity, his intended readers, sharp criticism of incompetent teachers, and a final invocation of the goddesses.

So the earlier description of this ending as containing only ten verses was incorrect. ; \]

We'll read **verses 1-10 in this instalment**, preserving their exact order, then use Part XXXVI for the eleven remaining verses and the definitive closing of our audiobook.

The first ten are not merely a decorative appendix. They establish the human circumstances of the text's composition.

And that changes how the whole work feels.

Because the man who has spent hundreds of pages explaining absolute consciousness is suddenly standing in eleventh-century Kashmir, surrounded by real people, writing something for their benefit.

The absolute has apparently acquired friends.


## I. Verse 1 - The Bee at Śiva's Feet Finally Signs His Name

KSTS p. 277 · Gnoli p. 283 · Vasantatilakā

**Devanāgarī**

> इत्थं प्रपन्नजनतोद्धरणप्रवृत्त-
> श्रीमन्महेश्वरपदाम्बुजचञ्चुरीकः ।
> वृत्तिं व्यधात् त्रिकरहस्यविमर्शगर्भां
> काश्मीरिकाच्चुखलकादधिगम्य जन्म ॥१॥

**IAST**

> *itthaṃ prapannajanatoddharaṇapravṛtta-*
> *śrīmanmaheśvarapadāmbujacañcurīkaḥ &#124;*
> *vṛttiṃ vyadhāt trikarahasyavimarśagarbhāṃ*
> *kāśmīrikāc cukhalakād adhigamya janma &#124;&#124;*

**My literal translation:**

“Thus, having obtained birth from Cukhalaka of Kashmir, the bee at the lotus-feet of the venerable Great Lord-who is engaged in uplifting those who have taken refuge-composed this commentary, containing within it reflection upon the secret of Trika.”

And there he is.

Abhinavagupta.

Not some mysterious disembodied source of divine doctrine.

Not the absolute speaking anonymously from beyond time.

An actual Kashmiri intellectual.

A son.

A devotee.

A writer.

And apparently a bee.

After the entire metaphysical argument in which the universe becomes the manifestation of Śiva-consciousness, the philosopher concludes by identifying himself as an insect drinking from the lotus at the Lord's feet.

This is a magnificent change of register.

We have spent thirty-four instalments establishing the supreme identity of consciousness with Bhairava.

And now Abhinavagupta says, in effect:

“Yes. Quite right. Universal consciousness. Absolute freedom. The unsurpassable Heart.

“Anyway, I'm a little bee buzzing around Śiva's feet.”

The devotional humility is not a rejection of his nonduality.

It is an expression of it.

Within the manifest relation between devotee and Lord, he speaks as a devotee. The distinction can be lovingly maintained without being treated as an ultimate metaphysical separation.

### The remarkable compound

**प्रपन्नजनतोद्धरणप्रवृत्त - prapannajanatoddharaṇapravṛtta**

This describes the Great Lord.

Let's unfold it.

**prapanna** - one who has approached for refuge, surrendered, or taken shelter. A past participial form of *pra-* \+ √*pad*, to go or resort to.

**janatā** - people, a multitude of persons.

**uddharaṇa** - raising up, rescuing, lifting out, from *ud-* \+ √*hṛ*, to take or carry.

**pravṛtta** - engaged, active, set in motion, from *pra-* \+ √*vṛt*.

The compound therefore characterises Maheśvara as engaged in lifting up those who seek refuge.

And notice the consistency.

At the end of Part XXXIV, the supreme Heart was described as *satatodita*, ever manifest.

But its divine freedom also includes *anugraha*, grace.

The Lord is not a remote entity indifferent to the limited subject.

He is understood as intrinsically capable of liberating manifestation.

Now Abhinavagupta praises him through exactly that function.

### Śrīmanmaheśvarapadāmbuja

The next compound means the lotus-feet of the glorious Great Lord.

**śrīmat** - glorious, venerable, endowed with splendour.

**Maheśvara** - Great Lord, from *mahā* \+ *īśvara*.

**pada** - foot.

**ambuja** - lotus, literally “water-born,” from *ambu*, water, and *ja*, born.

The combination *pada + ambuja* yields **padāmbuja** through vowel sandhi:

*a + a → ā.*

The lotus is a conventional poetic comparison to the Lord's feet, conveying purity, beauty, and the object of devotional attachment.

But the compound's structure is theologically revealing.

The supreme consciousness is being spoken of through a devotional image.

And Abhinavagupta adopts the position of the worshipper.

### Cañcurīkaḥ

**चञ्चुरीकः - cañcurīkaḥ**

A bee-like or honey-seeking creature in this devotional imagery.

The better-known Sanskrit poetic word is *cañcarīka*, bee. Here both the KSTS text and the GRETIL transcription preserve **cañcurīka**. We should retain their transmitted form rather than silently replacing it with the more familiar word.

The grammatical ending **-aḥ** is masculine nominative singular.

The subject of the sentence is Abhinavagupta, described metaphorically through this compound.

And the picture is wonderful.

He lives at the lotus-feet of Śiva.

He takes nourishment from their nectar.

His intellectual work is an expression of that devotion.

The vast scholar becomes a humble participant in the divine reality he has spent the entire work describing.

### Vṛttiṃ vyadhāt

**वृत्तिं व्यधात्**

*vṛttiṃ vyadhāt.*

“He composed the commentary.”

**vṛtti** can mean functioning, activity, mode of existence, or explanatory commentary.

Here it is the feminine accusative singular: *vṛttim*.

**vyadhāt** is a past-tense form of *vi-* \+ √*dhā*, to arrange, establish, or compose.

The verb supplies the finite action of the sentence.

And the object is the commentary itself.

Now look at the next compound.

### Trikarahasyavimarśagarbhām

**त्रिकरहस्यविमर्शगर्भाम्**

*trika-rahasya-vimarśa-garbhām.*

This is one of the most significant self-descriptions Abhinavagupta could have given his book.

**Trika** - the triadic Śaiva tradition.

**rahasya** - secret, mystery, hidden meaning.

**vimarśa** - examination, reflective apprehension, and, in Trika, the supreme consciousness's reflexive self-awareness.

**garbha** - womb, interior, something contained within.

The final **-ām** is feminine accusative singular, agreeing with **vṛttim**.

Thus the commentary contains within itself a reflection on the secret of Trika.

But *vimarśa* is a particularly loaded term.

For Abhinavagupta, the supreme is not merely luminous.

It is self-aware.

Its *prakāśa* is inseparable from *vimarśa*.

And the entire *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* has investigated that relationship through the structure of speech and manifestation.

So even in the ordinary sense of “reflection on the mystery of Trika,” the compound has a powerful doctrinal resonance.

The book reflects upon the very reflexive consciousness that grounds the possibility of reflection.

A rather beautiful recursive structure.

### Kāśmīrikāc Cukhalakād

Now, finally, the biographical declaration.

**काश्मीरिकाच्चुखलकाद्**

*kāśmīrikāc cukhalakād.*

“From Cukhalaka, a Kashmiri.”

The underlying ablative forms are *kāśmīrikāt* and *cukhalakāt*.

The sandhi produces *-āc c-* before the palatal consonant and *-ād* before the following vowel of *adhigamya*.

The ablative identifies the source of Abhinavagupta's birth.

This is not simply poetic scenery.

It identifies his father.

**Cukhalaka**, also appearing in forms such as *Cukhala* and *Cukhulaka*, is identified in the wider textual tradition with **Narasiṃhagupta**, Abhinavagupta's father. The identification is supported by Abhinavagupta's other autobiographical material and Jayaratha's commentary. ; ; \]

We'll examine his ancestry directly from epilogue verses 11-13 in Part XXXVI.

For now the incontrovertible claim is that the author identifies himself as born of Cukhalaka of Kashmir.

### The metre

Every pāda has fourteen syllables.

The pattern of **Vasantatilakā** is:

- - ⏑ - ⏑ ⏑ ⏑ - ⏑ ⏑ - ⏑ - -

The four pādas follow that pattern. For example:

*it-thaṃ pra-pan-na-ja-na-tod-dha-ra-ṇa-pra-vṛt-ta*

- - ⏑ - ⏑ ⏑ ⏑ - ⏑ ⏑ - ⏑ - -

The compound may look intimidating, but the syllabification is regular.

In **प्रपन्न**, the conjunct न्न represents न् + न.

In **उद्धरण**, द्ध represents द् + ध.

In **प्रवृत्त**, त्त represents त् + त.

And the vowels determine the syllabic nuclei.

The metre is not merely fourteen arbitrary syllables; the heavy and light pattern matches Vasantatilakā.

### What is the point of this first verse?

Abhinavagupta has completed his philosophical explanation.

He now locates the act of writing in three relationships.

To Śiva, through devotion.

To the Trika teaching, through intellectual examination.

To his father and Kashmir, through birth.

The cosmic philosopher has become an identifiable historical author.

And that is the beginning of the epilogue's distinctive achievement.


## II. Verse 2 - “Who the Fuck Can Put a Limit on Śiva's Teaching?”

KSTS p. 277 · Gnoli p. 283 · Vasantatilakā

**Devanāgarī**

> एतावदेतदिति कस्तुलयेत्प्रसह्य
> श्रीशांभवं मतमनर्गलिताश्च वाचः ।
> एतत्तु तावदखिलात्मनि भाति यन्मे
> भातं ततोऽत्र सुधियो न पराङ्मुखाः स्युः ॥२॥

**IAST**

> *etāvad etad iti kas tulayet prasahya*
> *śrīśāṃbhavaṃ matam anargalitāś ca vācaḥ &#124;*
> *etat tu tāvad akhilātmani bhāti yan me*
> *bhātaṃ tato 'tra sudhiyo na parāṅmukhāḥ syuḥ &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“Who could forcibly measure the venerable Śāmbhava doctrine and its unrestrained utterances, declaring, ‘This is precisely its extent’? Yet this much has appeared to me within the all-encompassing Self; therefore let the discerning not turn away from what has thus become manifest.”

Now this is an astonishingly intelligent statement about the limits of philosophical exposition.

Abhinavagupta has just written an enormous commentary.

He has examined phonemes, consciousness, manifestation, ritual, and liberation.

And he immediately refuses to claim that the entire truth is exhausted by the words he has written.

**Who can measure the teaching of Śambhu?**

Who can close it inside a finite intellectual construction and declare that nothing remains beyond?

He isn't saying that philosophical enquiry is pointless.

He has just demonstrated quite the opposite.

He is distinguishing a systematic exposition from the inexhaustibility of the reality it investigates.

### Etāvad etad

**एतावदेतद् - etāvad etad**

“This much, this particular extent.”

*Etāvat* expresses a measure or extent.

*Etad* means this.

The combination imitates the act of setting a boundary.

“Here is exactly how much there is.”

Abhinavagupta challenges the assumption that the supreme doctrine can be delimited in that manner.

### Kas tulayet?

**कस् तुलयेत् - kas tulayet?**

“Who could measure or weigh?”

**kaḥ** - who, masculine nominative singular.

Before *t*, its visarga becomes *s* in the continuous form.

**tulayet** - third-person singular optative causative of √*tul*, to weigh, balance, compare, measure.

The optative expresses possibility.

Who could accomplish such a measurement?

The implied answer is no finite investigator can conclusively enclose the supreme reality in a limited formulation.

### Prasahya

**प्रसह्य - prasahya**

By force, forcibly, presumptuously, overpoweringly.

An adverbial absolutive associated with *pra-* \+ √*sah*, to endure or overpower.

The word adds a critical edge.

Abhinavagupta is not merely asking who can study the doctrine.

He is asking who can forcibly impose a final measure upon it.

The difference matters.

One can investigate.

Explain.

Argue.

Clarify.

But the unlimited conscious ground is not thereby transformed into a finite object possessed by the investigator.

### Śrīśāṃbhavaṃ matam

**श्रीशांभवं मतम्**

*śrīśāṃbhavaṃ matam.*

The glorious doctrine belonging to Śambhu.

**Śāṃbhava** is an adjective derived from Śambhu.

**mata** means doctrine, view, opinion, or what is accepted; it is connected with √*man*, to think or regard.

The adjective and noun are neuter accusative singular, governed by *tulayet*.

The doctrine is the thing whose extent someone might attempt to measure.

### Anargalitāś ca vācaḥ

**अनर्गलिताश्च वाचः**

*anargalitāś ca vācaḥ.*

Unrestrained utterances, speech without an imposed barrier.

The adjective is associated with *argala*, a bar or bolt obstructing a doorway.

With the negative prefix *an-*, the expression evokes the removal of restriction.

**vāc** - speech.

**vācaḥ** - feminine accusative plural here, another object of the measuring verb.

The words themselves are not adequately contained by a presumptuous finite delimitation of their subject.

But the statement is not permission to produce arbitrary theological claims.

Abhinavagupta's own work is extraordinarily precise about the limits of ordinary language, scriptural interpretation, and doctrinal reasoning.

The point is that conceptual articulation does not exhaust the reality of consciousness.

### Akhilātmani

**अखिलात्मनि - akhilātmani**

Within the all-encompassing Self.

**akhila** - whole, entire, without remainder.

**ātman** - Self.

The locative singular **ātmani** gives the locus.

The author says that the meaning has become manifest in the all-encompassing Self.

He isn't simply describing an external revelation delivered to a passive person.

The language of **bhāti**, shining or appearing, belongs to the self-luminous vocabulary we've been examining throughout the work.

### Bhāti, bhātam

**भाति - bhāti**

Shines, appears, manifests.

Present third-person singular from √*bhā*, to shine.

**भातम् - bhātam**

What has shone or become manifest.

A participial formation.

The repetition is deliberate and philosophically resonant.

What appears to the author is expressed through the same language of manifestness that grounds the Trika theory of knowledge.

And now he makes a modest but important appeal.

### Sudhiyaḥ

**सुधियः - sudhiyaḥ**

Those of good understanding.

**su-** - good, excellent.

**dhī** - intelligence, understanding, thought.

The plural refers to discerning readers.

**na parāṅmukhāḥ syuḥ** -

“Let them not turn away.”

**parāṅmukha** means averted, turned away, uninterested, facing elsewhere.

**syuḥ** is the third-person plural optative of √*as*, to be.

His request is not:

“You must accept every word I say because I am a guru.”

It is:

“Let intelligent readers not turn away from what has been disclosed here.”

That is an altogether different intellectual attitude.

### The metre and a textual note

Vasantatilakā again: fourteen syllables per pāda, with the same heavy-light pattern as verse 1.

The KSTS electronic transcription has a malformed **पसह्य** for *prasahya*, while GRETIL gives the linguistically expected form.

That is best treated as a digital or typographical defect unless a manuscript collation establishes otherwise-not as an esoteric variant with a hidden meaning.

### What Abhinavagupta is actually admitting

His commentary is a finite intellectual work.

The reality it investigates is not finite in that manner.

There may be genuine insights beyond the particular formulation he has produced.

He is not claiming that the book has said absolutely everything that could ever be said.

This is extraordinarily important for the scholarly spirit of the work.

A tradition that treats every sentence of a master as the final limit of possible thought risks contradicting the very inexhaustibility of consciousness that master is describing.

Abhinavagupta refuses that crude conclusion.

And the next verse makes his purpose even clearer.


## III. Verse 3 - “For the Confused: Clarity. For the Convinced: Make the Recognition Actually Stick.”

KSTS p. 277 · Gnoli p. 283 · Vasantatilakā

**Devanāgarī**

> अज्ञस्य संशयविपर्ययभागिनो वा
> ज्ञानं प्रकम्परहितं प्रकरोति सम्यक् ।
> रूढस्य निश्चयवतो हृदयप्रतिष्ठां
> संवादिनीं प्रकुरुते कृतिरीदृशीयम् ॥३॥

**IAST**

> *ajñasya saṃśayaviparyayabhāgino vā*
> *jñānaṃ prakamparahitaṃ prakaroti samyak &#124;*
> *rūḍhasya niścayavato hṛdayapratiṣṭhāṃ*
> *saṃvādinīṃ prakurute kṛtir īdṛśīyam &#124;&#124;*

**My literal translation:**

“For one who does not know, or who is afflicted by doubt and erroneous understanding, a work such as this properly produces knowledge free from wavering. For one already established and possessed of certainty, it brings about a corresponding firm establishment in the Heart.”

That is the actual pedagogical programme.

And it is remarkably sophisticated.

Abhinavagupta knows that different readers are not in the same condition.

Some do not understand the doctrine.

Some are doubtful.

Some have developed mistaken interpretations.

Others already understand the basic principle but need its recognition to become more firmly established.

The same commentary can serve different purposes.

Now imagine the modern spiritual-teaching industry reading this.

“Step one: sell everybody the exact same technique.”

“Step two: tell them the technique will work regardless of their condition.”

“Step three: if it doesn't work, blame the student.”

Abhinavagupta, by contrast, distinguishes the intellectual and contemplative needs of different readers.

Not everyone requires the same intervention.

### Ajñasya - अज्ञस्य

Genitive singular of *ajña*, unknowing or ignorant.

The prefix *a-* negates *jña*, knowing.

It describes someone who does not yet possess the relevant knowledge.

This is not necessarily a moral condemnation.

It identifies an epistemic condition.

### Saṃśaya - संशय

Doubt, uncertainty, hesitation between alternatives.

The word belongs to classical Indian theories of knowledge and reasoning.

Doubt occurs when the evidence does not permit a determinate judgement.

For example:

“Is that object a post or a person?”

The mind has not settled the classification.

In the Trika discussion, the doubt concerns the nature of consciousness and reality.

Is consciousness truly universal?

Are objects ultimately independent?

How is manifestation related to the supreme Self?

Such questions require argument and analysis.

Not merely an instruction to stop thinking.

### Viparyaya - विपर्यय

Error, mistaken apprehension, inversion.

From *vi-* \+ *pari-* \+ √*i*, in the traditional lexical and derivational family.

The term commonly refers to an erroneous cognition.

This is different from mere doubt.

A person may be uncertain.

Another may be confidently wrong.

Abhinavagupta's verse encompasses both.

**saṃśayaviparyayabhāgin** means participating in, afflicted with, or subject to doubt and error.

The genitive singular **-bhāginaḥ** agrees with the person receiving instruction.

### Prakamparahitam

**प्रकम्परहितम् - prakamparahitam**

Free from wavering or disturbance.

**prakampa** - trembling, shaking, instability.

**rahita** - without, free from.

The neuter accusative singular qualifies **jñānam**, knowledge.

The work aims to produce an understanding that is not constantly destabilised by confusion.

But again, that does not mean suppressing legitimate questions.

A robust philosophical conviction should be able to withstand examination.

The entire *Vivaraṇa* has supplied arguments precisely for that purpose.

### Prakaroti samyak

**प्रकरोति सम्यक्**

*prakaroti samyak.*

“Produces properly,” “brings about thoroughly.”

**prakaroti** - present third-person singular of *pra-* \+ √*kṛ*, to do or produce.

**samyak** - correctly, properly, completely.

The object is knowledge free from wavering.

Now look at the second half.

### Rūḍhasya niścayavataḥ

**रूढस्य निश्चयवतः**

*rūḍhasya niścayavataḥ.*

“For the person already established, possessing certainty.”

**rūḍha** - grown, established, firmly rooted, from √*ruh*, to grow or ascend.

**niścaya** - determination, certainty, settled judgement.

**-vat** - possessing.

The genitive singular **niścayavataḥ** describes the person who already possesses a settled understanding.

That person doesn't need the same kind of basic explanation as someone entirely ignorant of the doctrine.

They need the recognition to become firmly integrated.

### Hṛdayapratiṣṭhā

**हृदयप्रतिष्ठा - hṛdayapratiṣṭhā**

Establishment in the Heart.

**hṛdaya** - Heart, the supreme conscious ground in the technical context.

**pratiṣṭhā** - establishment, foundation, standing firmly.

Connected with *prati-* \+ √*sthā*, to stand.

The feminine accusative singular **pratiṣṭhām** is the object of *prakurute*.

The point is no longer merely acquiring the correct proposition.

It is becoming established in the recognised reality.

### Saṃvādinīṃ

**संवादिनीम् - saṃvādinīm**

Corresponding, concordant, harmonious, agreeing.

The adjective relates to *saṃvāda*, agreement or correspondence.

It qualifies *hṛdayapratiṣṭhām*.

The established reader receives a corresponding or consonant resting in the Heart.

The understanding becomes more deeply integrated.

This is the difference between being able to explain a doctrine and possessing the settled recognition it describes.

### Kṛtir īdṛśīyam

**कृतिरीदृशीयम्**

*kṛtir īdṛśīyam.*

“A composition of this kind…”

The underlying structure is:

*kṛtiḥ + īdṛśī + iyam.*

**kṛti** - a composition, work, creation.

**īdṛśī** - of this kind, feminine nominative singular.

**iyam** - this, feminine nominative singular.

Sandhi creates the compact surface form.

The subject of the verb is the commentary itself.

It is the work that serves these two functions.

### The extraordinary continuity with Parts XXX-XXXIV

Remember the distinction between immediate recognition and sequential practice.

Abhinavagupta consistently refuses to assume that everyone possesses the same degree of understanding.

Here that principle is expressed pedagogically.

For the unknowing person: clarification.

For the doubtful: resolution.

For the mistaken: correction.

For the already convinced: stabilization in the Heart.

The work is not merely a collection of doctrines.

It is intended to transform how the reader understands their own experience.

And that brings us directly to the scholarly project of producing accurate translations and commentaries.

The text itself recognises different levels of access.

Someone may need grammar.

Another may need philosophical comparison.

Another may need to trace the actual Sanskrit argument.

Another may already understand the doctrine but benefit from renewed contemplation.

The same work can meet these needs differently without changing the underlying source.

### Metre

Vasantatilakā throughout.

For the opening pāda:

*aj-ñas-ya-saṃ-śa-ya-vi-par-ya-ya-bhā-gi-no-vā*

Fourteen syllables, matching the established pattern.

The conjunct **ज्ञ - jña** is one consonantal cluster.

And **विपर्यय - viparyaya** contains the sequence र्य, representing र् + य.

Do not split it into an extra vowel-bearing syllable.

The metre is secure.


## IV. Verse 4 - The Book Is a Staircase, Not a Fucking Prison

KSTS pp. 277-278 · Gnoli p. 284 · Vasantatilakā

**Devanāgarī**

> एतावदर्थरससंकलनाधिरूढो
> धाराधिरूढहृदयो विमृशेदतोऽपि ।
> यद्युत्तरं तदपि नैव सहेत नेदं
> सोपानमेतदमलं पदमारुरुक्षोः ॥४॥

**IAST**

> *etāvadartharasasaṃkalanādhirūḍho*
> *dhārādhirūḍhahṛdayo vimṛśed ato 'pi &#124;*
> *yady uttaraṃ tad api naiva saheta nedaṃ*
> *sopānam etad amalaṃ padam ārurukṣoḥ &#124;&#124;*

**My translation, retaining the rhetorical double negative:**

“One whose heart has become established in the continuous stream of grasping the essential savour of all this meaning may reflect even beyond what has been explained. This staircase would not fail to support even what lies higher, for someone wishing to ascend to the stainless state.”

The third pāda is syntactically compressed, particularly the interaction of *naiva saheta* and *na idam* in the double-negative construction. The interpretation is supported by its immediate context: Abhinavagupta presents his exposition as a means of ascent, not as a final boundary beyond which no further reflection is possible.

And now we have the perfect metaphor for his scholarly humility.

**A staircase.**

Not a throne.

Not an intellectual fortress.

Not a sacred object to be worshipped instead of understood.

A staircase.

Something you use to ascend.

The book is a means of making a higher understanding possible.

It is not a prison built around the highest reality.

### Etāvadartharasa

**एतावदर्थरस - etāvadartharasa**

The essence, savour, or relishable meaning of everything explained thus far.

**etāvat** - this much, such an extent.

**artha** - meaning, purpose, object, reality, significance.

**rasa** - taste, juice, essence, aesthetic relish.

Now this is a very Abhinavaguptan word.

Because *rasa* is not merely intellectual content.

Abhinavagupta is also a major theorist of aesthetic experience.

In his aesthetic writings, *rasa* concerns the relishable nature of aesthetic experience under particular conditions.

We should not automatically import the whole technical theory of dramatic *rasa* into this verse.

But the choice of word is evocative.

The meaning is not merely accumulated information.

It possesses an essence that can be savoured and assimilated.

The philosopher has supplied a vast exposition.

The reader is invited to draw out its essential significance.

### Saṃkalana - संकलन

Gathering together, collection, integration, summarisation.

From *sam-* \+ √*kal*, to count or arrange, in the relevant nominal formation.

The compound concerns the gathering of the essential meaning.

This is not simply memorisation.

It is the intellectual integration of the argument.

The parts of the work are understood in their relationship.

The phonemic account.

The theory of consciousness.

The structure of manifestation.

The relation between ritual and recognition.

All become intelligible as components of one philosophical system.

### Adhirūḍha - अधिरूढ

Mounted, ascended, established upon.

From *adhi-* \+ √*ruh*, to ascend or grow.

In the first half, the person has ascended through the gathering of the meaning's essence.

In the second, the heart is established upon a continuous stream.

The repeated participial structure is deliberate.

**adhirūḍha** appears twice.

The movement is upward.

Not toward a distant physical place.

But toward increasingly complete understanding.

### Dhārā - धारा

Stream, flow, current, continuity.

The image suggests an unbroken flow of understanding or contemplation.

A thought can be intermittent.

A proposition can be remembered occasionally.

But the stable recognition of the Heart is presented as something more continuous.

We encountered this in the previous instalment under **satatodita**, ever manifest.

The commentator is concerned with the transition from occasional recognition to settled apprehension.

### Hṛdaya - हृदय

Again the Heart.

But now the word carries two mutually reinforcing meanings.

The ordinary heart or inner disposition of the reader.

And the supreme Heart of Trika metaphysics.

The reader's heart becomes established in the stream of the teaching.

And the teaching concerns establishment in the supreme Heart itself.

The relationship is both pedagogical and theological.

### Vimṛśet - विमृशेत्

One should reflect, examine, investigate, consider deeply.

Third-person singular optative of *vi-* \+ √*mṛś*.

Now this is a splendid choice.

After hundreds of pages discussing **vimarśa** as the reflexive awareness of consciousness, Abhinavagupta tells the reader:

**Vimṛśet.**

Reflect.

Investigate.

Do not merely repeat.

Do not simply accept.

The act of enquiry is explicitly invited to continue beyond the exposition.

### Uttarām? No: uttaram

**उत्तरम् - uttaram**

Higher, further, subsequent, beyond.

In this context it refers to something beyond the present exposition.

Abhinavagupta is not simply telling readers to invent whatever they like.

The movement is toward the stainless state.

But he does not close the philosophical enquiry at the boundary of his own text.

### Sopāna - सोपान

A staircase, stairway, steps, means of ascent.

A beautiful concrete image.

The book provides a structure through which the reader may ascend.

It does not itself become the ultimate state.

The distinction between means and goal has now been restated in literary form.

Just as an external rite can become a means of recognition without manufacturing consciousness, a written commentary can become a means of understanding without exhausting the supreme truth.

### Ārurukṣuḥ

The verse ends with:

**आरुरुक्षोः - ārurukṣoḥ**

“Of one wishing to ascend.”

This is a desiderative formation associated with √*ruh*, to ascend.

The form contains the desire to climb.

The genitive singular ending links it with the person for whom the staircase is intended.

And that person is the reader.

The philosopher is not asking the reader merely to admire the elegance of his composition.

He wants the reader to ascend.

To understand.

To recognise.

To proceed.

This is a rather profound statement about what scholarship is for.

### A small philological warning

GRETIL's electronic text has **dhārādhirūdha** with dental *dh* where the expected participle is **adhirūḍha**, with retroflex *ḍh*, supported by the KSTS Devanāgarī.

That is a plausible electronic transliteration error. No evidence from the consulted apparatus requires elevating it to the status of a substantive manuscript variant.

The metre is Vasantatilakā in all four pādas.

### The lesson of verse 4

Abhinavagupta is not trying to produce intellectual dependency.

He is trying to make comprehension possible.

The reader should grasp the essential structure of the argument, establish it in the Heart, and continue reflecting.

The book is a staircase.

The philosophical ascent is not exhausted by the staircase.

And now the epilogue changes completely.

Because after four verses explaining why he wrote and how the work should be used, Abhinavagupta starts naming people.

Real people.

In Kashmir.

Connected through family, friendship, devotion, and scholarship.

And the resulting historical picture is much more interesting than a generic story about a solitary sage sitting in a cave.


## V. Verse 5 - The Royal Minister, His Son, and the First Glimpse of Abhinavagupta's Actual World

KSTS p. 278 · Gnoli p. 284 · Śārdūlavikrīḍita

**Devanāgarī**

> कश्मीरेषु यशस्करस्य नृपतेरासीदमात्याग्रणीः
> श्रीमान्वल्लभ इत्युदाहृततनुर्यः प्राग्र्यजन्मा द्विजः ।
> तस्य स्वाङ्गभवः प्रसिद्धिपदवीपात्रं समग्रैर्गुणैः
> श्रीशौरिः शिशुचन्द्रचूडचरणध्यानैकरत्नाकरः ॥५॥

**IAST**

> *kaśmīreṣu yaśaskarasya nṛpater āsīd amātyāgraṇīḥ*
> *śrīmān vallabha ity udāhṛtatanur yaḥ prāgryajanmā dvijaḥ &#124;*
> *tasya svāṅgabhavaḥ prasiddhipadavīpātraṃ samagrair guṇaiḥ*
> *śrīśauriḥ śiśucandracūḍacaraṇadhyānaikaratnākaraḥ &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“In Kashmir, there was a distinguished Brahmin of eminent birth, known as the venerable Vallabha, foremost among the ministers of King Yaśaskara. His own son was the venerable Śauri, worthy of renown for his many virtues, a treasure-house devoted exclusively to meditation upon the feet of Śiva, whose crest bears the young crescent Moon.”

There.

Suddenly we're in history.

Not merely metaphysics.

Not the thirty-six tattvas.

Not the sixteen vowel-powers.

A king.

A minister.

The minister's son.

And a religious and intellectual world situated within medieval Kashmir.

This is particularly valuable because Abhinavagupta is not supplying an entirely legendary genealogy of vanished sages.

He names people whose circumstances can be compared with independent historical records.

### King Yaśaskara

The king is **Yaśaskara**, generally placed on the Kashmiri throne around **939-948 CE**.

That chronology is supported by the historical tradition represented in Kalhaṇa's *Rājataraṅgiṇī* and subsequent scholarship. ; \]

Now notice what this establishes.

Vallabha belonged to the ministerial environment of Yaśaskara's reign.

His son Śauri belonged to the following generation.

And Śauri's son Karṇa, whom we shall meet in verse 7, belonged to Abhinavagupta's circle.

This provides a meaningful chronological connection between Abhinavagupta's own lifetime and a historically datable royal court.

It does not, by itself, give the exact year in which the *Vivaraṇa* was composed.

But it helps situate the people who encouraged its composition.

### Amātyāgraṇīḥ

**अमात्याग्रणीः - amātyāgraṇīḥ**

*Amātya* means minister, adviser, or royal official.

*Agraṇī* means one who leads or stands at the front.

Thus the compound denotes a leading minister.

The form is masculine nominative singular.

It describes Vallabha.

This is not necessarily proof that his precise official title was “prime minister” in the modern constitutional sense.

The Sanskrit establishes his prominent place among royal ministers.

### Nṛpateḥ

**नृपतेः - nṛpateḥ**

Genitive singular of *nṛpati*, king.

*nṛ* - man, person.

*pati* - lord, ruler.

The king is Yaśaskara.

The ending **-eḥ** indicates the genitive singular of the *-i* stem.

The passage therefore describes Vallabha's relationship to the royal administration.

### Vallabha

**वल्लभ - Vallabha**

This is the minister's name.

The ordinary adjective *vallabha* can mean beloved, dear, cherished, or favoured.

But here it functions as a proper name.

The verse says:

**śrīmān vallabha ity udāhṛtatanuḥ**

“Whose person was known by the name venerable Vallabha.”

The name is introduced explicitly.

We should not translate it as an unnamed “beloved minister.”

### Prāgryajanmā dvijaḥ

**प्राग्र्यजन्मा द्विजः - prāgryajanmā dvijaḥ**

Of eminent birth, a Brahmin or twice-born person in the social context.

**dvija** literally means “twice-born,” from *dvi*, two, and *ja*, born.

In this setting it identifies Vallabha's Brahmanical status.

This is important historically, even if it is not the central philosophical claim of the work.

The religious and scholarly world in which Abhinavagupta operated was socially differentiated.

His nondualism did not mean those distinctions were absent from the society around him.

Indeed, recall the earlier verse asserting that birth and ritual observance are not universal prerequisites for supreme recognition.

The tension between social setting and highest metaphysical doctrine is real.

It deserves examination rather than romantic simplification.

### Tasya svāṅgabhavaḥ

**तस्य स्वाङ्गभवः - tasya svāṅgabhavaḥ**

“His own son.”

**tasya** - of him, genitive singular.

**sva** - own.

**aṅga** - limb, body, member.

**bhava** - arisen or born.

The compound is a poetic expression for an offspring.

The referent of *tasya* is Vallabha.

Therefore Śauri is **Vallabha's son**.

This is explicit in the Sanskrit.

### Śauri

**शौरिः - Śauriḥ**

The next named figure.

Śauri is described as possessing all virtues and as deeply devoted to Śiva.

The word **samagrair guṇaiḥ** is instrumental plural:

“Through complete or abundant excellent qualities.”

And the extraordinary final compound describes him as a treasure-house of meditation upon the Lord's feet.

### Śiśucandracūḍacaraṇadhyānaikaratnākaraḥ

Now try saying this without breathing.

**शिशुचन्द्रचूडचरणध्यानैकरत्नाकरः**

*śiśu-candra-cūḍa-caraṇa-dhyāna-eka-ratnākaraḥ.*

**śiśu** - young, infant.

**candra** - Moon.

**cūḍa** - crest or topknot.

**caraṇa** - foot.

**dhyāna** - meditation.

**eka** - one, sole, exclusive.

**ratnākara** - treasure-house, mine of jewels, or figuratively a vast repository.

The “young Moon” is the crescent.

Śiva is identified through the crescent decorating his crest.

Śauri is likened to a treasury whose jewel is meditation upon the feet of that deity.

Notice the difference between this and a mere assertion that he was wealthy or powerful.

His worldly position is not the culminating qualification.

His spiritual devotion is.

And Abhinavagupta devotes a long metrical pāda to expressing it.

### The metre: Śārdūlavikrīḍita

We have now changed metre.

The first four epilogue verses used Vasantatilakā.

Here the pādas expand to nineteen syllables.

**Śārdūlavikrīḍita** is the celebrated nineteen-syllable metre whose name evokes the play of a tiger.

Its heavy-light pattern is:

- - - ⏑ ⏑ - ⏑ - ⏑ ⏑ ⏑ - - - ⏑ - - ⏑ -

This stanza follows that pattern when read with the word division supplied by the transmitted text.

An important detail arises in the second pāda:

*śrīmān vallabha ity udāhṛtatanur yaḥ prāgryajanmā dvijaḥ.*

The transmitted **vallabha ity** must be attended to as written; merging the final *a* of *vallabha* with the initial *i* of *ity* in a mechanical digital syllable counter produces an apparent eighteen-syllable line. The transmitted word-boundary reading preserves nineteen vowel nuclei. This is a case where automated scansion without sensitivity to poetic word division is misleading.

And in the first pāda, **कश्मीरेषु - kaśmīreṣu** is the expected spelling.

GRETIL's **kasmīreṣu** loses the palatal ś, whereas KSTS has **कश्मीरेषु**. The discrepancy should not be elevated into a historical manuscript variant without evidence.

### Why this verse matters

We have now met a historically situated household.

Vallabha, a minister.

Śauri, his son.

And in the next verse, Śauri's wife.

It is becoming clear that Abhinavagupta's intellectual world was not simply a succession of teachers in a disembodied lineage.

It included households, women, family relationships, religious devotion, and a social network capable of supporting major scholarly work.

And now we meet one of the most interesting women associated with Abhinavagupta.

**Vatsalikā.**


## VI. Verse 6 - Vatsalikā: The Woman Whose Compassion Apparently Exceeded the Capacity of Sanskrit Compounds

KSTS p. 278 · Gnoli p. 284 · Śārdūlavikrīḍita

**Devanāgarī**

> शीलस्यायतनं परस्य यशसो जृम्भास्पदं नर्मभू-
> र्वात्सल्यस्य समग्रलोककरुणाधर्मस्य जन्मस्थितिः ।
> श्रीमद्वत्सलिकाभिधा सहचरी तस्यैव भक्त्युल्लसत्-
> प्रोद्रिक्तान्तरवृत्ति शंकरनुतौ यस्या मनो जृम्भते ॥६॥

**IAST**

> *śīlasyāyatanaṃ parasya yaśaso jṛmbhāspadaṃ narmabhūr*
> *vātsalyasya samagralokakaruṇādharmasya janmasthitiḥ &#124;*
> *śrīmadvatsalikābhidhā sahacarī tasyaiva bhaktyullasat-*
> *prodriktāntaravṛtti śaṅkaranutau yasyā mano jṛmbhate &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“His companion, the venerable woman named Vatsalikā, is an abode of virtuous conduct, a place where others' renown flourishes, a source of good humour, and the birthplace and abiding ground of affection and compassion for the entire world. Her mind, its inner disposition overflowing and expanding through devotion, blossoms in praise of Śaṅkara.”

This is one of the most strikingly affectionate verses in the entire epilogue.

Abhinavagupta does not simply call Vatsalikā pious.

He describes her through a rich collection of virtues.

Good conduct.

Care for others' reputation.

Humour.

Affection.

Universal compassion.

Devotional enthusiasm.

And her own inner life.

The text makes her much more than an incidental female name in a list of male scholars.

### Who was Vatsalikā?

The verse calls her:

**तस्यैव सहचरी - tasyaiva sahacarī.**

“His companion.”

The referent is **Śauri**, introduced immediately before.

The feminine **sahacarī** means a female companion, partner, or one who goes together with someone.

In this context, she is understood as **Śauri's wife**.

That relationship is corroborated by the broader *Tantrāloka* autobiographical material and scholarship. A notable textual dispute in *Tantrāloka* 37 concerns whether an expression should read *bhrātāpi*, “brother,” or *bhartāpi*, “husband”; the latter fits the relationship independently established by the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*. ; ; \]

So we can say more than that Vatsalikā was simply a woman in the entourage.

She was Śauri's companion or wife.

And she was important enough for Abhinavagupta to devote an entire elaborate verse to her.

### Śīlasyāyatanam

**शीलस्यायतनम् - śīlasyāyatanam**

An abode of good conduct.

**śīla** - character, disposition, conduct, virtue.

**āyatana** - abode, support, locus, foundation.

The genitive **śīlasya** means “of conduct.”

The compound or syntactic expression designates her as a dwelling-place of virtue.

The word *āyatana* is especially interesting because we encountered it in the root tantra's description of the supreme Lord as *svāyatana*, established in his own ground.

Here the meaning is ordinary and ethical.

Vatsalikā is the abode of virtuous character.

We shouldn't automatically import the full metaphysical meaning of *āyatana* into every occurrence.

Context determines the interpretation.

### Parasya yaśaso jṛmbhāspadam

The next phrase concerns the flourishing of others' reputation or renown.

**para** - other.

**yaśas** - fame, renown, good reputation.

**jṛmbha** - expansion, flourishing, opening, from √*jṛmbh*.

**āspada** - seat, place, basis, occasion.

The compound **jṛmbhāspada** therefore means a place where something expands or flourishes.

The interpretation is ethically significant.

Vatsalikā is praised as someone through whom others prosper or receive recognition.

Her virtue is relational.

Not just private ascetic accomplishment.

### Narmabhūḥ

**नर्मभूः - narmabhūḥ**

A source or ground of humour, playfulness, agreeable wit.

**narman** - jest, humour, playful affection.

**bhū** - ground, source, or place of arising.

This is rather wonderful.

Because we've spent thirty-four instalments treating Abhinavagupta as a philosopher of almost terrifying intellectual sophistication.

And here he praises a woman for being a source of **humour**.

A detail often lost when ancient religious figures are turned into permanently solemn icons.

The people around Abhinavagupta evidently possessed ordinary human qualities.

They laughed.

They cultivated affection.

They lived in relationships.

And at least one of them was sufficiently associated with wit or playful warmth for the philosopher to make it part of her formal praise.

### Vātsalya

**वात्सल्य - vātsalya**

Tender affection, especially parental or caring affection.

The word is historically connected with *vatsa*, calf or child, and develops the sense of tenderness toward the young or dependent.

It is also strikingly close to Vatsalikā's own name.

The poet exploits that semantic resonance.

The woman named Vatsalikā is praised as a source of *vātsalya*.

It is an elegant instance of meaningful name-associated wordplay.

### Samagralokakaruṇā

**समग्रलोककरुणा - samagralokakaruṇā**

Compassion toward the entire world.

**samagra** - entire, whole, complete.

**loka** - world, people, realm.

**karuṇā** - compassion, pity, responsiveness to suffering.

The compound expresses an expansive ethical disposition.

And this is particularly interesting in a tradition whose metaphysics stresses that all beings are manifestations of one consciousness.

The verse praises compassion, not merely intellectual realisation.

But we should distinguish theological interpretation from historical claim.

The Sanskrit establishes that Abhinavagupta praised Vatsalikā as compassionate.

It does not independently document every charitable action she performed.

### Bhaktyullasatprodriktāntaravṛtti

Now the compound becomes gloriously excessive.

**भक्त्युल्लसत्प्रोद्रिक्तान्तरवृत्ति**

*bhakti-ullasat-prodrikta-antar-vṛtti.*

**bhakti** - devotion.

**ullasat** - flourishing, rising, blossoming.

**prodrikta** - overflowing, heightened, abundant.

**antar** - inner.

**vṛtti** - disposition, movement, functioning, activity.

The whole expression describes an inward life overflowing with devotional energy.

And now comes its object.

### Śaṅkaranutau

**शंकरनुतौ - śaṅkaranutau**

“In the praise of Śaṅkara.”

**Śaṅkara** - Śiva, the beneficent or auspicious one.

**nuti** - praise, laudation, from √*nu*, to praise.

The locative singular **-au** marks the sphere in which her mind flourishes.

**yasyā manaḥ jṛmbhate** -

“Whose mind blossoms.”

The genitive feminine **yasyāḥ**, through sandhi, identifies Vatsalikā.

The finite verb **jṛmbhate** is third-person singular middle.

The subject is **manaḥ**, mind.

### The metre

Nineteen syllables per pāda, in Śārdūlavikrīḍita.

The extended line accommodates the extraordinarily rich nominal compounds.

The metre itself has become appropriate to the subject.

A wide, elaborate form for a lavish description of character.

And the Sanskrit is not merely piling up adjectives without structure.

It moves from conduct and social generosity to affection, compassion, and finally the flowering of her devotional mind.

### Why Vatsalikā matters historically

In the *Tantrāloka* autobiographical material, Vatsalikā also appears in connection with the household or circumstances supporting Abhinavagupta's writing.

The picture that emerges is that significant Sanskrit intellectual and religious work was supported by social relationships, not simply by solitary contemplation.

One must be careful not to invent a fully documented daily biography from a handful of verses.

We do not know every detail of her life.

But the evidence is enough to resist treating her as insignificant.

Abhinavagupta names her.

Praises her character.

Identifies her connection with Śauri.

And places her among the people surrounding the composition of his work.

For a historical reconstruction of the Trika tradition, that matters enormously.

And the next verse identifies a child of the household.

**Karṇa.**


## VII. Verse 7 - Karṇa: The Young Man Who Apparently Decided Saṃsāra Was a Terrible Career Option

KSTS p. 278 · Gnoli p. 284 · Śārdūlavikrīḍita

**Devanāgarī**

> तस्यैवात्मभवो विभावितजगत्सर्गस्थितिः शंकर-
> ध्यानार्चापरिचिन्तनैकरसिकः कर्णाभिधानो द्विजः ।
> यो बाल्येऽप्यथ यौवनेऽपि विषयासक्तिं विहाय स्थिरा-
> मेनामाश्रयते विमर्शपदवीं संसारनिर्मूलिनीम् ॥७॥

**IAST**

> *tasyaivātmabhavo vibhāvitajagatsargasthitiḥ śaṅkara-*
> *dhyānārcāparicintanaikarasikaḥ karṇābhidhāno dvijaḥ &#124;*
> *yo bālye 'py atha yauvane 'pi viṣayāsaktiṃ vihāya sthirām*
> *enām āśrayate vimarśapadavīṃ saṃsāranirmūlinīm &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“His own son, the Brahmin named Karṇa, who contemplates the creation and maintenance of the world and delights exclusively in meditation upon, worship of, and reflection on Śaṅkara, has abandoned attachment to sense-objects even in childhood and youth, and follows this steadfast path of reflective awareness that uproots saṃsāra.”

Now we have a young man who apparently looked at the ordinary prospects of human life and decided that the entire enterprise was structurally unsatisfactory.

Friends?

Pleasure?

Social ambition?

A respectable career?

Not particularly compelling.

He wants to contemplate the nature of the universe and attain liberation.

The medieval Kashmiri equivalent of a young man announcing that he is going to abandon his career in accounting and spend several years studying Sanskrit philosophy.

A potentially alarming conversation at the family dinner table.

Except in this case the family seems extraordinarily supportive.

### Whose son was Karṇa?

The phrase is:

**तस्यैवात्मभवः - tasyaivātmabhavaḥ.**

“His very own son.”

The masculine **tasya** refers back to Śauri, the male antecedent identified in the preceding verses.

Vatsalikā is Śauri's companion or wife.

Thus **Karṇa is explicitly Śauri's son**, and is generally understood as belonging to the household of Śauri and Vatsalikā.

The verses do not require us to invent a different relationship.

This point is independently recognised in scholarship discussing Abhinavagupta's associates.

### Ātmabhavaḥ

**आत्मभवः - ātmabhavaḥ**

Born from oneself.

Own offspring.

From:

**ātman** - self, person.

**bhava** - becoming, arising, born.

The compound is masculine nominative singular, agreeing with Karṇa.

### Vibhāvitajagatsargasthitiḥ

**विभावितजगत्सर्गस्थितिः**

*vibhāvita-jagat-sarga-sthitiḥ.*

**vibhāvita** - contemplated, made present in understanding, considered.

**jagat** - universe.

**sarga** - emission, creation, from √*sṛj*.

**sthiti** - maintenance, standing, persistence, from √*sthā*.

The compound describes someone who has contemplated the creation and maintenance of the world.

This is not a proof that Karṇa independently produced a complete cosmological system.

It is a praise of his religious and philosophical orientation.

His mind is directed toward the structure of manifestation.

And the particular pairing of *sarga* and *sthiti* is consistent with the cosmic functions we've repeatedly encountered in the work.

### Śaṅkaradhyānārcāparicintanaikarasikaḥ

Another magnificent compound.

**Śaṅkara** - Śiva.

**dhyāna** - meditation.

**arcā** - worship, honouring.

**paricintana** - thorough reflection.

**eka** - sole, exclusive.

**rasika** - one who relishes, enjoys, delights in.

Thus the young man delights particularly in meditation, worship, and reflection concerning Śiva.

Now this is especially significant for the intellectual atmosphere of the household.

His interest is not restricted to ritual.

It includes **paricintana**.

Philosophical reflection.

The same activity that has dominated Abhinavagupta's *Vivaraṇa*.

### Bālye 'pi, yauvane 'pi

**बाल्येऽपि - bālye 'pi**

Even in childhood.

**यौवनेऽपि - yauvane 'pi**

Even in youth.

Both are locative singular forms.

The repetition of *api* emphasises the precocious character of Karṇa's religious orientation.

He is not portrayed as someone who became interested in liberation only after experiencing every ordinary pleasure of life.

He is praised for possessing this disposition early.

### Viṣayāsakti

**विषयासक्ति - viṣayāsakti**

Attachment to sense-objects.

**viṣaya** - object, especially an object of experience or cognition.

**āsakti** - attachment, clinging, passionate involvement.

The accusative singular **viṣayāsaktim** is governed by **vihāya**.

**vihāya** means “having abandoned,” an absolutive from *vi-* \+ √*hā*, to abandon or leave.

The verse says he has abandoned attachment.

It does not necessarily say that he has ceased perceiving objects.

That would contradict the philosophy we've been studying.

The issue is not the mere occurrence of perception.

It is the binding attachment through which the subject identifies its fulfilment with particular differentiated objects.

### Vimarśapadavī

**विमर्शपदवी - vimarśapadavī**

The path or course of reflective awareness.

**vimarśa** - reflection, self-apprehension.

**padavī** - path, course, track, way.

This is a central word in the verse.

Karṇa is not merely praised for memorising mantras.

He follows the way of *vimarśa*.

And in this philosophical context, that means the enquiry into the supreme Self's reflexive consciousness.

It is the very path Abhinavagupta has been explaining.

### Saṃsāranirmūlinī

**संसारनिर्मूलिनी - saṃsāranirmūlinī**

That which uproots saṃsāra.

**saṃsāra** - the wandering course of conditioned existence, rebirth, or bondage.

**nirmūlana** - uprooting, removal by the roots.

From *nir-* \+ *mūla*, root, through the relevant verbal formation.

The feminine adjective qualifies the **vimarśapadavī**.

The path uproots saṃsāra.

The imagery is botanical again.

A tree can be uprooted.

A binding condition can be removed at its root.

The metaphor is especially fitting after our banyan-seed discussion.

The universe's manifestation is not the problem.

The mistaken identification that constitutes bondage is the root to be removed.

### The metre

Śārdūlavikrīḍita: nineteen syllables per pāda.

The compounds are long, but the pattern remains regular.

The continuous sequence:

*yo bālye 'py atha yauvane 'pi*

must be read with its sandhi and avagraha correctly.

The avagraha marks the elision of an initial vowel.

It does not create an additional syllable.

### What this verse proves-and what it doesn't

We can establish that Karṇa was named, praised, and associated with Śauri's family.

We can establish that Abhinavagupta considered him a serious practitioner, devoted to contemplation and worship of Śiva.

We can establish that his youthful abandonment of attachment is part of the author's praise.

But we cannot establish every psychological detail of Karṇa's childhood from the verse.

Nor can we conclude that every young person studying Trika should imitate his social circumstances.

The statement is biographical praise within a religious poem.

Not a universally binding life prescription.

And now the next person enters.

A relationship not merely inferred from the wider tradition.

Explicitly stated in Abhinavagupta's own words.

**His brother.**


## VIII. Verse 8 - Manorathagupta: “Yes, My Actual Brother Also Wants to Understand the Entire Fucking System.”

KSTS p. 278 · Gnoli p. 284 · Vasantatilakā

**Devanāgarī**

> भ्राता ममैव शिवशासनरूढचित्तः
> प्रेप्सुः परात्मनि मनोरथगुप्तनामा ।
> यः शास्त्रतन्त्रमखिलं प्रविवेक्तुकामः
> प्राप्तुं परं शिवपदं भवभेदनाय ॥८॥

**IAST**

> *bhrātā mamaiva śivaśāsanarūḍhacittaḥ*
> *prepsuḥ parātmani manorathaguptanāmā &#124;*
> *yaḥ śāstratantram akhilaṃ pravivektukāmaḥ*
> *prāptuṃ paraṃ śivapadaṃ bhavabhedanāya &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“My own brother, named Manorathagupta, whose mind is firmly established in Śiva's teaching, wishes to attain the supreme Self. He desires to examine the entire body of scripture and tantra in order to reach the supreme state of Śiva and break through conditioned existence.”

The most important words are the first three:

**भ्राता ममैव - bhrātā mamaiva.**

“My very own brother.”

There is no ambiguity about the relationship.

Abhinavagupta identifies Manorathagupta explicitly as his brother.

Not his cousin.

Not a symbolic spiritual brother.

Not a later biographer's hypothesis.

**His brother.**

And the verse gives us an extraordinarily direct glimpse into their shared intellectual interests.

### Bhrātā

**भ्राता - bhrātā**

Nominative singular of *bhrātṛ*, brother.

The noun belongs to the *-ṛ* kinship-stem declension.

The final long ā is part of the nominative singular form.

Compare *pitā*, father, from *pitṛ*.

### Mamaiva

**ममैव - mamaiva**

*mama + eva.*

“My very own,” or “of me indeed.”

The first-person genitive **mama** means “my.”

The emphatic particle **eva** reinforces the relationship.

The sandhi is:

*a + e → ai.*

Thus:

*mama eva → mamaiva.*

In Devanāgarī:

मम + एव → ममैव.

The resulting vowel sign **ै** represents *ai*.

This is a useful beginner sandhi pattern.

And it's attached to an unusually valuable historical statement.

### Śivaśāsanarūḍhacittaḥ

**शिवशासनरूढचित्तः**

*śiva-śāsana-rūḍha-cittaḥ.*

**śiva** - Śiva.

**śāsana** - teaching, doctrine, religious discipline.

**rūḍha** - established, firmly rooted.

**citta** - mind, thought, inner awareness in the relevant psychological sense.

The compound describes someone whose mind has become firmly established in Śiva's teaching.

Now notice the progression.

Verse 3 spoke of the **rūḍha** practitioner, already possessing conviction.

Verse 8 uses the same participial family to describe Manorathagupta's mind.

The vocabulary connects the pedagogical purpose of the work with the actual condition of one of the people for whom it was written.

### Prepsuḥ

**प्रेप्सुः - prepsuḥ**

Desirous of attaining or obtaining.

A desiderative formation.

The masculine nominative singular ending agrees with Manorathagupta.

Its object or goal is expressed through the following phrase concerning the supreme Self.

The word indicates intentional aspiration.

Not accomplished possession.

The verse praises him for seeking the supreme state.

It does not automatically declare that he has already attained the complete liberation described in the commentary.

That distinction matters.

### Parātmani

**परात्मनि - parātmani**

In or toward the supreme Self.

**para** - supreme, highest.

**ātman** - Self.

The locative singular is **ātmani**.

The compound therefore concerns the supreme Self as the focus of aspiration.

### Pravivektukāmaḥ

Now the wonderful word:

**प्रविवेक्तुकामः - pravivektukāmaḥ**

“Desirous of examining or discriminating thoroughly.”

**pra-** - intensifying or forward-directed prefix.

**vi-** - distinguishing or differentiating prefix.

**√vic** - to distinguish, discern, examine.

**viviktu / vivektu** - infinitival or desiderative-related form in the relevant construction.

**kāma** - wishing, desiring.

The compound expresses a wish to understand by analysis.

And the object is:

**śāstratantram akhilam** -

“The entire body of scripture and tantra.”

Now that is an ambitious project.

Not a brief introductory course.

Not twenty convenient inspirational quotations.

The entire textual system.

Philosophical scriptures.

Tantric teachings.

Their interrelationships.

Their significance for liberation.

Manorathagupta apparently wanted what might be called a complete scholarly treatment.

And his brother Abhinavagupta has responded by writing the kind of work that makes an ordinary reading list look like a shopping receipt.

### Bhavabhedanāya

**भवभेदनाय - bhavabhedanāya**

“For the breaking of conditioned existence.”

**bhava** - becoming, existence, conditioned worldly life.

**bhedana** - splitting, piercing, breaking.

From √*bhid*, to split or divide.

The dative singular **-āya** expresses purpose.

The goal of his enquiry is liberation.

And that is significant.

The philosophical work is not being undertaken merely to demonstrate verbal cleverness.

It is directed toward overcoming bondage.

The distinction between scholarship and contemplative transformation is not abolished, but the two are linked.

### Metre

We return to Vasantatilakā.

Four pādas of fourteen syllables, following the established heavy-light pattern.

The first pāda begins:

*bhrā-tā ma-mai-va śi-va-śā-sa-na-rū-ḍha-cit-taḥ.*

Fourteen syllables.

The vowel **ai** in *mamaiva* is one diphthongal vowel.

The cluster **त्त** in *cittaḥ* represents त् + त.

### The personal significance

There is something moving about this verse.

The philosopher has completed a massive metaphysical argument about universal consciousness.

And then he names his own brother as someone seeking understanding.

A real human relationship exists behind the scholastic enterprise.

The book is not only a monument to abstract theory.

It is an effort to communicate something to people the author actually knows.

And the next person is especially interesting because his qualifications are almost precisely those required for the kind of rigorous Sanskrit scholarship we've been practising throughout this audiobook.

**Rāmadeva.**


## IX. Verse 9 - Rāmadeva: Grammar, Mīmāṃsā, Logic. Finally, Someone Who Knows How to Read the Fucking Text.

KSTS p. 278 · Gnoli p. 284 · Anuṣṭubh

**Devanāgarī**

> शिवशास्त्रैकरसिकः पदवाक्यप्रमाणवित् ।
> रामदेवाभिधानश्च भूषितोत्तमजन्मकः ॥९॥

**IAST**

> *śivaśāstraikarasikaḥ padavākyapramāṇavit &#124;*
> *rāmadevābhidhānaś ca bhūṣitottamajanmakaḥ &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“And there is the man named Rāmadeva, devoted exclusively to the Śaiva scriptures, knowledgeable in word, sentence, and valid cognition, and adorned by eminent birth.”

Now this is exceptionally relevant to the intellectual world of Abhinavagupta.

Because **pada, vākya, pramāṇa** is not merely a random list of three things a scholar happens to know.

It evokes major disciplines of Sanskrit intellectual life.

Grammar.

Sentential interpretation.

Epistemology and logic.

The very tools required to understand the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* accurately.

And we have spent the entire reading seeing why those tools matter.

A single negative prefix can change the metaphysical meaning of a verse.

A case ending can determine whether a goddess is the agent or the object.

A compound can conceal multiple plausible interpretations.

A quotation can be misattributed.

A manuscript variant can turn a philosophical assertion into its opposite.

Abhinavagupta's world was not merely a world of visionary experiences.

It was a world of serious linguistic and philosophical enquiry.

### Śivaśāstraikarasikaḥ

**शिवशास्त्रैकरसिकः**

*śiva-śāstra-eka-rasikaḥ.*

**śiva** - Śiva.

**śāstra** - authoritative teaching or treatise.

**eka** - one, exclusive.

**rasika** - connoisseur, relisher, one who delights in something.

The compound describes a person who delights in the Śaiva scriptures above all.

And remember the word *rasa*.

Abhinavagupta has already described the philosophical meaning of the work as something whose essence can be savoured.

Now he praises Rāmadeva as a *rasika*.

Someone who genuinely delights in the teaching.

### Padavākyapramāṇavit

**पदवाक्यप्रमाणवित्**

*pada-vākya-pramāṇa-vit.*

Three domains.

**Pada** - word, grammatical unit.

**Vākya** - sentence, meaningful utterance.

**Pramāṇa** - valid means of knowledge or reliable cognition.

**Vit** - knower, from √*vid*, to know.

The compound describes knowledge across these domains.

Now the 1918 KSTS editor supplies a helpful footnote:

**पदमिति व्याकरणम् । वाक्यं मीमांसा । प्रमाणं तर्कः ।**

*padam iti vyākaraṇam. vākyaṃ mīmāṃsā. pramāṇaṃ tarkaḥ.*

“Word means grammar; sentence means Mīmāṃsā; valid cognition means logic.”

This is an editorial explanation, not a separate statement by Abhinavagupta inside the verse.

Nevertheless, the association is historically intelligible.

### Vyākaraṇa

The analysis of Sanskrit grammar.

Phonology.

Morphology.

Sandhi.

Compounds.

Verbal forms.

Case endings.

The structure of meaningful expression.

This is the domain associated with **pada**.

### Mīmāṃsā

The systematic interpretation of authoritative sentences, especially in the Vedic exegetical tradition.

It investigates the relation between words, sentences, injunctions, meaning, and ritual authority.

This is particularly relevant to a commentary that has spent so much time interpreting terse tantric statements.

What exactly does a scriptural sentence assert?

What is its grammatical construction?

How does its meaning depend upon context?

What is literal?

What is figurative?

What is a restrictive particle doing?

These are quintessential exegetical questions.

### Pramāṇa and tarka

Valid means of knowledge and reasoning.

Indian philosophical traditions disagree about the number and status of valid means of cognition.

Perception.

Inference.

Testimony.

And other proposed categories.

The Pratyabhijñā tradition engages deeply with Buddhist epistemology, Nyāya, and other schools.

Its arguments are not merely collections of theological assertions.

They involve carefully developed accounts of cognition, recognition, memory, manifestation, and evidence.

Thus a scholar knowledgeable in *pramāṇa* possesses tools central to the philosophical work.

### What do we actually know about Rāmadeva?

The verse establishes that a person named Rāmadeva belonged to the relevant group of scholarly and religious associates.

He was devoted to Śaiva learning and praised for competence in these disciplines.

The verse does not explicitly identify him as Abhinavagupta's biological brother.

That identification belongs specifically to Manorathagupta in verse 8.

Nor does this verse establish every detail of Rāmadeva's institutional status.

Later introductions and scholarship commonly treat him as one of the three associates whose interest helped prompt the work.

But his exact family relationship should not be invented.

### The metre

The verse uses four eight-syllable pādas, in the anuṣṭubh family.

| Pāda | Syllabification | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *śivaśāstraikarasikaḥ* | śi-va-śās-trai-ka-ra-si-kaḥ | 8 |
| *padavākyapramāṇavit* | pa-da-vāk-ya-pra-mā-ṇa-vit | 8 |
| *rāmadevābhidhānaś ca* | rā-ma-de-vā-bhi-dhā-naś-ca | 8 |
| *bhūṣitottamajanmakaḥ* | bhū-ṣi-tot-ta-ma-jan-ma-kaḥ | 8 |

The first pāda has an unusual cadence relative to the most familiar *pathyā* form, so a precise account should call it anuṣṭubh rather than asserting that every pāda is a textbook example of the ordinary pathyā pattern.

The syllable count is secure.

The compound **śāstra + eka** undergoes vowel sandhi:

*a + e → ai.*

Thus:

*śāstraika.*

And in **प्रमाणवित्**, the final *-vit* derives from the knowing root √*vid*.

The compound is not referring to a separate deity named Vit.

It means a knower of the relevant epistemic disciplines.

### The intellectual implication

We now have a remarkably coherent set of figures.

Karṇa is devoted to meditation, worship, and reflective awareness.

Manorathagupta wants to examine the entire body of scriptures and tantras.

Rāmadeva is skilled in grammar, textual interpretation, and reasoning.

And Abhinavagupta has written a commentary combining exactly those activities.

It is not difficult to understand why this particular book emerged from such an environment.

The *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* isn't simply a mystical monologue.

It is a rigorous textual exposition directed toward people who want to understand the system.

And this is why verse 10 is so important.

Because Abhinavagupta now explains the intention behind his work in one concise, deeply generous statement.


## X. Verse 10 - “I Wrote This to Show the Way. May It Lead Everyone to Śiva.”

KSTS p. 279 · Gnoli p. 284 · Āryā

**Devanāgarī**

> एतत्प्रियहितकरणप्ररूढहृदयेन यन्मया रचितम् ।
> मार्गप्रदर्शनं तत् सर्वस्य शिवाप्तये भूयात् ॥१०॥

**IAST**

> *etatpriyahitakaraṇaprarūḍhahṛdayena yan mayā racitam &#124;*
> *mārgapradarśanaṃ tat sarvasya śivāptaye bhūyāt &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“What I have composed, with a heart firmly devoted to doing what is dear and beneficial to these people-may that showing of the path serve everyone in attaining Śiva.”

This verse is beautiful.

Because the author has now told us exactly what he intended.

He has written the work for the benefit of particular people close to him.

But he wishes its benefit to extend beyond them.

**Sarvasya.**

For everyone.

Not only the household.

Not only the scholars.

Not only the initiates.

Not only the people who already understand the system.

The path is to be made visible for the benefit of all.

The passage is not a modern manifesto of universal institutional access, and we should not pretend it is.

But the wording of the final wish is expansive.

And that expansive intention is one of the most important features of the epilogue.

### Etatpriyahitakaraṇa

**एतत्प्रियहितकरण**

This is the longest and most important compound in the verse.

Its elements include:

**etat** - this, these, referring in context to the people just introduced or their benefit.

**priya** - dear, agreeable, pleasing.

**hita** - beneficial, conducive to welfare, advantageous.

**karaṇa** - making, doing, effecting.

The compound conveys doing something both dear and beneficial to the relevant people.

There is a significant distinction between *priya* and *hita*.

Something may be pleasing without being beneficial.

Something may be beneficial without being immediately pleasing.

The combination expresses a compassionate intention that includes both affection and welfare.

Abhinavagupta isn't merely saying:

“I wrote something that my friends will enjoy.”

Nor merely:

“I wrote something intellectually correct.”

He is connecting the composition with their genuine benefit.

### Prarūḍhahṛdayena

**प्ररूढहृदयेन - prarūḍhahṛdayena**

“With a heart firmly established or deeply rooted.”

**pra-** - intensifying prefix.

**rūḍha** - established, grown, rooted.

**hṛdaya** - heart.

The final **-ena** is instrumental singular.

The phrase describes the disposition with which Abhinavagupta composed the book.

And now it becomes clear that the epilogue's *hṛdaya* is doing several kinds of work.

The supreme Heart of the metaphysical teaching.

The reader's heart becoming established through understanding.

And the author's own heart deeply moved by the welfare of his companions.

These are not identical meanings.

But they resonate.

The philosopher of universal consciousness is also a writer concerned with the people around him.

### Yan mayā racitam

**यन्मया रचितम् - yan mayā racitam**

“What was composed by me.”

The underlying elements are:

**yat** - what, relative pronoun, neuter nominative or accusative singular.

**mayā** - by me, instrumental singular of the first-person pronoun.

**racitam** - composed, arranged, created; past passive participle of √*rac*, to arrange or construct.

The sandhi *yat + mayā* appears as **yan mayā** in the continuous form.

The whole expression identifies the work as Abhinavagupta's own composition.

And the instrumental **mayā** is explicit.

“By me.”

There is no need to invent an anonymous scribe or second author.

### Mārgapradarśanam

**मार्गप्रदर्शनम् - mārgapradarśanam**

A showing of the path.

**mārga** - path, way, route.

**pradarśana** - displaying, revealing, showing clearly, from *pra-* \+ √*dṛś*, to see.

The noun is neuter nominative or accusative singular.

It refers to the work just composed.

This is the crucial self-description.

Not an encyclopaedia.

Not a definitive prison for the mind.

Not an object to be revered instead of understood.

**A showing of the way.**

The staircase metaphor from verse 4 has become even more direct.

The commentary is intended to reveal a path.

### Sarvasya

**सर्वस्य - sarvasya**

Of everyone, for everyone.

Genitive singular of *sarva*.

The broad sense is universal, though the immediate grammatical form is singular.

It can denote the welfare of all, considered collectively.

### Śivāptaye

**शिवाप्तये - śivāptaye**

“For the attainment of Śiva.”

**śiva** - Śiva.

**āpti** - attainment, reaching, acquisition, from √*āp*.

The dative singular **-aye** expresses purpose.

Now remember the highest doctrine of the work.

The attainment of Śiva is not finally the acquisition of an external object.

It is recognition of the supreme conscious nature already grounding experience.

So the word *āpti* must be understood within that metaphysical setting.

Abhinavagupta wishes his work to guide readers toward recognition of Śiva-consciousness.

Not simply to provide information about a remote deity.

### Bhūyāt

**भूयात् - bhūyāt**

“May it be.”

Third-person singular optative of √*bhū*, to be or become.

The optative expresses a wish or benediction.

The verse is not a declaration that every reader will necessarily attain liberation.

It is a prayer.

May the work serve this purpose.

### The metre: Āryā

The metre now changes again.

And this is important.

Verse 10 is **Āryā**, a quantitative or *mātrā*-based metre.

Unlike Vasantatilakā and Śārdūlavikrīḍita, which use fixed numbers and patterns of syllables, Āryā is organised around measures of syllabic weight.

The transmitted verse divides into the characteristic sequence:

| Quarter | Text | Mātrās |
|--------|--------|--------|
| 1 | *etatpriyahitakaraṇa* | 12 |
| 2 | *prarūḍhahṛdayena yan mayā racitam* | 18 |
| 3 | *mārgapradarśanaṃ tat* | 12 |
| 4 | *sarvasya śivāptaye bhūyāt* | 15 |

The total structure is **12 + 18 / 12 + 15**, the standard Āryā distribution.

This is a genuinely verified quantitative scansion, not merely the observation that the verse has an unusual number of words.

One short syllable contributes one mātrā.

One heavy syllable contributes two.

Thus the verse's metrical construction differs fundamentally from the preceding syllable-counting metres.

And its change of form is perfectly appropriate.

Abhinavagupta has finished the long ornate praise of his associates.

Now the authorial wish becomes concise, direct, and rhythmically distinct.

### A textual correction

The digitised KSTS transcription of this verse contains visibly defective forms in the long compound, including a sequence corresponding to **etatmiya…** where GRETIL gives the coherent **etatpriyahitakaraṇa-**, and a damaged form in *prarūḍha*.

The GRETIL reading is supported by its intelligible morphology and the Āryā mātrā structure.

That is good evidence for correcting the electronic transcription.

It is not evidence that an otherwise unattested manuscript reading must have contained the exact error displayed by the digital text.

The distinction between print, manuscript, and electronic transcription remains essential.

### Why verse 10 is a natural stopping point

The first ten verses have completed a coherent movement.

Abhinavagupta states that he composed the work.

He refuses to pretend that his exposition exhausts the supreme reality.

He explains how it can help readers at different levels of understanding.

He describes it as a staircase toward the stainless state.

He names the people surrounding its composition.

Then he wishes that the work may guide everyone toward Śiva.

That is a complete initial authorial conclusion.

And the following eleven verses begin a further movement, starting with Abhinavagupta's ancestry and continuing into a much more personal explanation of his mission.

So the pause after verse 10 is justified by the structure of the text, not merely by the desire to divide the audiobook into manageable instalments.


## XI. The historical picture: Who was actually in the room?

Now assemble what the first ten verses establish.

| Person | Secure relationship or description | Textual basis |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **Cukhalaka** | Abhinavagupta's father; identified elsewhere as Narasiṃhagupta | Verse 1; later verses 11-13 and corroborating tradition |
| **Vallabha** | Leading minister of King Yaśaskara | Verse 5 |
| **Śauri** | Son of Vallabha, devotee of Śiva | Verse 5 |
| **Vatsalikā** | Śauri's companion or wife, praised for compassion and devotion | Verse 6 |
| **Karṇa** | Son of Śauri, devoted to contemplation and worship | Verse 7 |
| **Manorathagupta** | Abhinavagupta's own brother | Verse 8 |
| **Rāmadeva** | Scholar of Śaiva texts, grammar, sentence interpretation, and logic | Verse 9 |
| **Abhinavagupta** | The author, writing to guide these people and others toward Śiva | Verses 1 and 10 |

This is a remarkable picture.

And it undermines a crude popular image of Abhinavagupta as a permanently isolated sage.

His own concluding verses show a network of relationships.

Family.

Devotion.

Learning.

Royal administration.

Households.

Teachers.

Students.

Women praised for moral and devotional qualities.

Young people seeking liberation.

Scholars trained in rigorous intellectual disciplines.

The work emerged within this social environment.

### Not everything in the epilogue is an independent historical fact

We should be precise about the evidentiary hierarchy.

The names and explicit relationships are primary textual evidence.

The praise of someone's virtues is a statement made by Abhinavagupta, not an independently verified moral biography.

The identification of Cukhalaka with Narasiṃhagupta rests on comparison with the wider authorial tradition.

The dating of Yaśaskara provides external chronological context.

The connection between this household and the composition of other works can be compared with the *Tantrāloka* epilogue.

But we cannot reconstruct every conversation, daily practice, or living arrangement from these verses alone.

The temptation to invent a beautiful scene is understandable.

Imagine Abhinavagupta sitting among scholars in a Kashmiri house, a manuscript before him, Vatsalikā nearby, Karṇa asking questions, Manorathagupta demanding more detail, and Rāmadeva objecting to the grammatical analysis.

It would make a wonderful historical film.

But unless the sources supply those details, it remains a literary reconstruction, not a documented event.

Our task is to distinguish them.

What we can say securely is already extraordinary.

**The author of one of the most sophisticated Sanskrit philosophical commentaries in the tradition wrote it with named people and their welfare in mind.**

And that matters for how we read the entire work.


## XII. The philological audit: ten verses, several metres, and the danger of inventing manuscript variants

The metrical sequence of these ten verses is now established:

| Verses | Metre | Structure |
|--------|--------|--------|
| 1-4 | **Vasantatilakā** | Four pādas of 14 syllables |
| 5-7 | **Śārdūlavikrīḍita** | Four pādas of 19 syllables |
| 8 | **Vasantatilakā** | Four pādas of 14 syllables |
| 9 | **Anuṣṭubh** | Four pādas of 8 syllables |
| 10 | **Āryā** | 12 + 18 / 12 + 15 mātrās |

The Vasantatilakā and Śārdūlavikrīḍita pādas also match their characteristic heavy-light patterns when properly segmented, not merely their syllable totals.

The Āryā requires mātrā counting, not simple syllable counting.

This matters because the epilogue is not a monotonous succession of ordinary ślokas.

Abhinavagupta deliberately varies the literary form.

The changes fit the shifts in subject: philosophical reflection, ornate praise, personal identification, and finally a concise benediction.

### What counts as a genuine variant?

A manuscript variant is a reading supported by a manuscript witness or a reliable critical apparatus.

An electronic error is a defect introduced during copying, typing, OCR, or digital transformation.

A different editorial segmentation is a difference in how verses or sections are divided.

These must not be confused.

The KSTS edition was prepared from manuscripts that the editor describes in the preface, including a Śāradā-script Kashmiri manuscript with known defects and missing leaves. Its apparatus records readings from different witnesses.

The digitised transcription is useful, but it is not itself a fresh manuscript witness.

In these first ten concluding verses, several differences between KSTS electronic text and GRETIL are plainly consistent with transcription errors, such as the lost consonant in *itthaṃ* or the damaged compound in verse 10.

Other differences require more caution.

And none should be described as a proven manuscript variant merely because two websites display different strings.

### The status of Gnoli's pagination

GRETIL's text was entered by Somadeva Vasudeva and carries page references to Raniero Gnoli's 1985 edition.

The first ten concluding verses correspond broadly to **Gnoli pp. 283-284**, with the following ancestry verse beginning at p. 284 and the transition to p. 285 shortly afterward. The KSTS verses occupy the printed ending around pp. 277-279.

Thus the source alignment is specific enough for locating the text.

But the page numbers are not interchangeable between the editions.

And the 1918 facsimile has not been directly collated here against all printed signs on those pages. Our verse text follows the comparison of the available transcriptions, with corrections disclosed where material.


## XIII. Why these ten verses change the meaning of the entire audiobook

We've reached the part of the work where the metaphysics becomes human.

For thirty-four instalments, we've asked what consciousness is.

How language emerges.

Why phonemes have theological significance.

What distinguishes *prakāśa* from *vimarśa*.

How the supreme Heart is related to the manifest universe.

How the guru, mantra, ritual, and liberation can all be understood within nonduality.

And now Abhinavagupta tells us why he has undertaken such an enormous intellectual labour.

**To help people understand.**

Not merely to demonstrate mastery.

Not merely to produce an authoritative scripture.

Not merely to compile traditional doctrines.

He wants the text to serve as a guide.

He wants the doubtful to achieve clarity.

He wants the already convinced to become more firmly established.

He wants those capable of deeper reflection to continue beyond his own exposition.

And he wants the work to benefit particular people he knows and loves.

Then he wishes that benefit to extend to everyone.

### The philosopher as teacher

The distinction is important.

A philosopher can construct a system.

A commentator can explain a text.

A religious teacher can transmit a doctrine.

But the greatest challenge is to make understanding possible in another person.

The reader must actively grasp the argument.

They must see how the parts connect.

They must distinguish the literal Sanskrit from the commentator's interpretation.

They must examine the reasoning.

They must recognise where the argument is strong and where it depends upon metaphysical commitments they may not share.

And they must determine what the work means for their own enquiry.

That is why the staircase metaphor is so powerful.

The commentary is not the ascent itself.

It is a structure designed to make ascent possible.

### The danger of confusing reverence with comprehension

Imagine a disciple who adores Abhinavagupta.

They collect his books.

Memorise his name.

Repeat his doctrines.

Praise his genius.

And become furious whenever someone asks a difficult question about his reasoning.

The disciple may possess enormous reverence.

But have they understood the purpose of the work?

Verse 4 suggests otherwise.

Abhinavagupta explicitly permits and encourages further reflection.

His philosophical teaching is not meant to terminate the activity of discrimination.

And verse 3 identifies the removal of doubt and error as one of the work's essential functions.

A reader who refuses examination in the name of loyalty risks betraying the intellectual spirit of the text.

### The distinction between knowledge and recognition

Now return to *vimarśa*.

The word has appeared across the work in different but related senses.

Reflection.

Examination.

Reflexive self-apprehension.

The supreme consciousness's awareness of itself.

The reader's philosophical enquiry is not simply identical with the supreme *vimarśa*.

But it can participate in the movement toward recognition.

The text uses reasoning to clarify the structure of consciousness.

It uses linguistic analysis to show how differentiation emerges.

It uses scriptural interpretation to articulate the relation between manifestation and its ground.

And it uses contemplative reflection to direct attention toward the reality that makes cognition possible.

Thus the work combines scholarship and spiritual purpose without reducing one to the other.

### Why an accurate Sanskrit edition matters

This is particularly important for everything we've been doing in these readings.

If the Sanskrit is inaccurate, the philosophy can become distorted.

A negative prefix disappears, and a statement reverses meaning.

A compound is segmented incorrectly, and a ritual instruction becomes a metaphysical claim it never made.

An editorial note is mistaken for Abhinavagupta's prose, and the author is credited with another person's interpretation.

A root-tantra verse is silently expanded from a different source, and the sequence becomes fictitious.

A manuscript variant is treated as an electronic typo, or an electronic typo is promoted into a manuscript variant.

The result is not simply a minor bibliographical imperfection.

It changes the intellectual object being studied.

And that is why the combination of precise Sanskrit, literal translation, morphology, textual criticism, and philosophical explanation is not decorative pedantry.

It is necessary to understand what the author actually argued.

Abhinavagupta's own verse 9 gives us a fitting image of the required scholarly capacity:

**pada, vākya, pramāṇa.**

Word.

Sentence.

Valid cognition.

Grammar.

Interpretation.

Reasoning.

If we want to understand the supreme Heart through the text, we cannot afford to ignore the structure of the text itself.

### The man behind the metaphysics

And now imagine the final contrast.

On one side, Abhinavagupta describes consciousness as the absolute ground of all manifestation.

Beyond space.

Beyond time.

Self-luminous.

Free.

The very Heart of Bhairava.

On the other side, he names his father.

His homeland.

A minister.

The minister's son.

A compassionate woman.

A young contemplative.

His brother.

And a scholar of grammar and logic.

There is no contradiction within his system.

The universal consciousness manifests through differentiated relationships.

The finite author is not a rival ultimate substance standing outside Śiva.

The people around him are not merely obstacles to spiritual realisation.

The act of writing is not an unfortunate distraction from metaphysics.

All can be understood as expressions of divine freedom.

And the love motivating the work can itself be included within the manifestation of Śakti.

This is why the epilogue feels so appropriate.

The philosopher has spent hundreds of pages explaining that the world is not fundamentally separate from consciousness.

Then he shows us the world in which the book was written.

A world of people.

Relationships.

Questions.

Texts.

Learning.

Affection.

And the desire to help others understand.

The metaphysics has returned to life.

### The most important sentence in the epilogue so far

For me, the decisive expression is:

**मार्गप्रदर्शनं तत् सर्वस्य शिवाप्तये भूयात् ।**

*mārgapradarśanaṃ tat sarvasya śivāptaye bhūyāt.*

May this showing of the path serve everyone in attaining Śiva.

That is the purpose.

The commentary is not the goal.

The writer is not the goal.

The accumulation of Sanskrit terminology is not the goal.

The goal is recognition of the supreme reality.

And the book is intended to help make that recognition intelligible.

The staircase exists to be climbed.

The path exists to be traversed.

The words exist to disclose what they mean.

And the Heart, according to Abhinavagupta, is never absent from the person undertaking the enquiry.

That is what gives the whole project its extraordinary philosophical coherence.


## XIV. The next authentic transition: Abhinavagupta finally gives his ancestry-and then starts insulting bad teachers

The next verse, **epilogue 11**, begins:

**अन्तर्वेद्यामत्रिगुप्ताभिधानः…**

*antarvedyām atriguptābhidhānaḥ…*

It introduces **Atrigupta**, an ancestor from the region of Antarvedī, and the family's movement into Kashmir.

Verse 12 continues with **Varāhagupta** and **Cukhala**.

Verse 13 identifies Abhinavagupta himself within that line.

This is the autobiographical material needed to complete the genealogy only partially visible in verse 1.

Then the tone changes dramatically.

In verse 14, Abhinavagupta discusses the different sorts of readers and openly dismisses those who lack the capacity or willingness for genuine discrimination.

In verse 16, he describes the **trident of knowledge** fashioned to cut the bonds of confused beings.

And verse 17 is spectacular.

He complains that he himself has been misled by people who imagined themselves teachers of reality but whose tongues had not even touched the two syllables of the word *tattva*.

Yes.

Abhinavagupta, the great exponent of divine nonduality, directly criticises incompetent teachers.

The man who has spent hundreds of pages explaining supreme consciousness is apparently not prepared to pretend that everyone claiming spiritual authority knows what they're talking about.

And then the final verses reveal a deeper motive.

Śiva's grace.

Somānanda's teaching.

The divine Mothers.

And a remarkable concluding prayer in which Abhinavagupta asks the goddesses to forgive the restlessness of his own speech and thought.

It's a magnificent ending.

And unlike the previous thirty-four instalments, it is the author's own personal voice, preserved in his concluding poetry.

So we stop at the authentic break after verse 10.

The social world has been established.

The author's purpose has been declared.

And we are ready for the final eleven verses.
