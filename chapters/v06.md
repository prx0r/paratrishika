# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART VI - The Alphabet of the Universe

*Abhinavagupta takes sixteen vowels, twenty-five consonants, the Moon, the Sun, thirty-six principles of reality, and the first letter of the alphabet, then informs us that the whole bloody lot is Śiva.*

## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

Bhairava has finally spoken.

He's told the Goddess that the supreme Kaulika method resides in the space of his Heart.

We've examined *anuttara*, the unsurpassable; *svātantrya*, divine freedom; the four levels of speech; the relation of Kula and Akula; and the strange fact that even the Sanskrit grammatical distinctions between "he," "you," and "I" can be interpreted through Nara, Śakti and Śiva.

And now we're approaching one of the most outrageous ideas in the entire tradition.

**The Sanskrit alphabet is not merely a means of describing the universe. In Abhinavagupta's Tantric interpretation, the phonemes themselves express the powers through which the universe manifests.**

Think about how absurdly ambitious this is.

Normally, you learn an alphabet so you can read books.

Abhinavagupta is about to turn the alphabet into a complete symbolic account of creation.

The vowels become divine powers.

The consonants correspond to ontological principles.

The phases of the Moon become manifestations of consciousness.

And the sounds emitted by the human mouth are interpreted as differentiated expressions of Parā Vāk, Supreme Speech.

You thought learning Devanāgarī was about remembering where to put the little *i* vowel sign.

My brother in Bhairava.

That was merely the tutorial.

Now the alphabet is going to eat the universe.

A textual point: Abhinavagupta introduces verses 5-8 together, then comments on their opening *atha*, the nature of the first vowel, the undivided ground of speech, and only subsequently the detailed arrangement of consonants and tattvas. ## 1. Bhairava reveals the cosmic alphabet - verses 5-8

These four verses form a single connected description. Their grammatical relationships extend across verse boundaries.

### A literal orientation

Bhairava describes the vowels beginning with *a*, related to the lunar *tithis*, and extending to *bindu*. The Moon and Sun are proclaimed in relation to this series through the conjunction of *kāla*, a term that requires careful interpretation.

Then he locates the twenty-five principles from Earth through Puruṣa in the five consonantal classes beginning with *ka* and ending with *ma*.

Above these come four *dhāraṇā* principles and five further principles beginning with *śa*.

Finally, this unfolding is described as a creation rooted in *a* and extending through *kṣa*, constituting the source or matrix of mantras and *vidyās*.

A grammatical caution: the verses do not explicitly supply every individual tattva correspondence. The detailed mapping is furnished by Abhinavagupta's commentary, and not every grouping follows the familiar thirty-six-tattva chart without qualification.

But the overall idea is unmistakable.

**The alphabet, from अ to क्ष, is treated as the articulated structure of manifestation.**

Now let's learn the Sanskrit before we venture any further.

## 2. अथाद्याः - Athādyāḥ: Why the first word contains a grammatical trap

The verse begins:

**अथाद्याः - athādyāḥ**

You can analyse it through the ordinary words:

**अथ - atha**

Now, then, next.

**आद्याः - ādyāḥ**

The first ones, those beginning at the start.

The final form is masculine nominative plural, agreeing with the vowels, *svarāḥ*.

Now the sandhi:

**अथ + आद्याः → अथाद्याः**

*atha + ādyāḥ → athādyāḥ*

Because **a + ā → ā**.

That's why the result doesn't contain two separately pronounced vowels.

But Abhinavagupta will not leave it there.

He also interprets the opening as involving the vowel **अ - a** itself, with **थ - tha** potentially serving a secondary phonetic function in another analysis.

In the commentary he explicitly says the vowels are those whose beginning is *a*, and offers a further interpretation of the *th* sound.

That second reading is not the ordinary syntactic analysis of the adverb *atha*.

It is an intentional esoteric resegmentation.

And this distinction is important:

- **Ordinary Sanskrit grammar:** *atha* is an indeclinable word commonly meaning "now" or "then."
- **Abhinavagupta's phonemic interpretation:** the initial *a* can disclose Anuttara, and the following sound can be interpreted through the special symbolic system.

So even the first word has two jobs.

One establishes the sequence of instruction.

The other opens the metaphysics of sound.

And we're approximately three syllables into the chapter.

Outstanding.


## 3. तिथयः - Tithayaḥ: The Moon enters the philosophical circus

Now look at:

**तिथयः - tithayaḥ**

The lunar days or *tithis*.

A *tithi* is a lunar phase unit used in traditional Indian calendrical astronomy. More precisely, it corresponds to a twelve-degree difference in the geocentric ecliptic longitudes of Moon and Sun.

There are fifteen tithis in a waxing half-month and fifteen in a waning half-month.

The esoteric system associates the fifteenfold pattern with the vowel powers of speech.

But first, the Sanskrit.

**तिथि - tithi**

Lunar day.

Plural nominative:

**तिथयः - tithayaḥ**

A feminine *-i* stem changes shape before the plural ending.

Compare:

| Form | Sanskrit |
|--------|--------|
| Singular nominative | **तिथिः - tithiḥ** |
| Singular accusative | **तिथिम् - tithim** |
| Plural nominative | **तिथयः - tithayaḥ** |

Notice that *tithi* becomes *tithay-* in the plural formation.

This happens through the inflectional pattern of the *-i* stem.

So don't go looking for an extra mysterious root called *tithaya*.

It's the same noun, inflected.

### Why lunar days?

Because a lunar month gives us a natural cycle of differentiation.

The visible Moon appears to increase.

Then decrease.

Its illuminated portion changes according to its geometrical relation with the Sun and Earth.

Now the Tantric symbolic move.

The phases provide a model of one underlying continuity manifesting through differentiated portions.

The Moon isn't being physically manufactured anew every night.

But its appearance changes.

Similarly, the phonemic powers are treated as differentiated manifestations of an underlying conscious reality.

This doesn't make lunar astronomy proof of Kashmir Śaivism.

It means the tradition interprets the numerical and cyclical structures of the calendar through its theology of speech.

And the correspondence becomes especially important when we reach the distinction between the fifteen lunar digits and the transcendent sixteenth.

We'll come to that in the proper order.


## 4. स्वराः - Svarāḥ: The sixteen powers of the Sanskrit vowel system

Now here's the part you've been preparing for.

**स्वराः - svarāḥ**

Vowels.

The singular is:

**स्वरः - svaraḥ**

The plural is:

**स्वराः - svarāḥ**

The word *svara* can also mean musical tone, pitch or accent, depending on context.

In the present passage, it refers to vowels in the sacred phonemic system.

And here is the sixteenfold set relevant to this tradition:

Traditional sixteen-member sacred vowel-series, including the nasal and visarga signs as the final two members. The sign अं is commonly associated here with bindu; अः with visarga.

Now some important qualifications.

Strictly speaking, the final two members are not independent oral vowel qualities in the same sense as *a*, *i* or *u*.

**अं - aṃ** involves a nasal sign, *anusvāra*, represented by the dot **ं**.

**अः - aḥ** involves *visarga*, represented by **ः**.

Their inclusion within the sixteen-member series belongs to the traditional phonemic classification.

This is a theological and grammatical classification, not an assertion that nasalization and visarga are physiologically identical to ordinary vowels.

### The vowel signs you actually write

You've already discovered that consonants in Devanāgarī carry an inherent *a*.

Now see how the other vowels modify a consonant.

| Independent vowel | With क |
|--------|--------|
| अ - a | क - ka |
| आ - ā | का - kā |
| इ - i | कि - ki |
| ई - ī | की - kī |
| उ - u | कु - ku |
| ऊ - ū | कू - kū |
| ऋ - ṛ | कृ - kṛ |
| ॠ - ṝ | कॄ - kṝ |
| ऌ - ḷ | कॢ - kḷ |
| ॡ - ḹ | कॣ - kḹ |
| ए - e | के - ke |
| ऐ - ai | कै - kai |
| ओ - o | को - ko |
| औ - au | कौ - kau |
| अं - aṃ | कं - kaṃ |
| अः - aḥ | कः - kaḥ |

The extremely rare vocalic *ḷ* and long vocalic forms are part of the learned Sanskrit inventory. They are not equally common in ordinary prose.

But they matter enormously to this symbolic system, where the complete phonemic series is treated as an expression of divine powers.

### The philosophical trap

Now here's a distinction I want you to remember throughout the entire rest of this audiobook.

**The written Sanskrit character is not identical with the phoneme, and the phoneme is not identical with its Tantric metaphysical interpretation.**

Three levels:

1. **Writing:** what Devanāgarī represents.
2. **Phonology:** the sounds and their linguistic relations.
3. **Tantric exegesis:** the divine powers symbolized through those sounds.

When Abhinavagupta identifies *a* with Anuttara, he's operating on the third level.

He isn't saying the orthographic conventions of Devanāgarī logically establish the existence of Śiva.

And yet the relationship is extraordinarily evocative.

In a script where consonant letters normally contain an inherent *a*, the primary vowel is already present in the default written syllable.

A beautiful opportunity for contemplation.

But the metaphysical doctrine and the orthographic convention remain different kinds of explanation.

Right.

Now we've got the alphabet.

Next comes the much deeper question:

**How can a sequence of differentiated sounds exist within a consciousness that is not itself divided into successive parts?**

And that's exactly where Abhinavagupta begins his commentary.


## 5. बिन्द्ववसानगाः - Bindvavasānagāḥ: Everything culminates in a dot

Now the next word is magnificently ugly-looking if you haven't learned how compounds work.

**बिन्द्ववसानगाः - bindvavasānagāḥ**

But once you dismantle it, the whole thing becomes wonderfully straightforward.

Start with:

**बिन्दु - bindu**

A dot, drop, point.

Then:

**अवसान - avasāna**

Ending, termination, conclusion.

And:

**ग - ga**

Going, reaching, moving towards.

The final **-āḥ** marks the masculine nominative plural, agreeing with *svarāḥ*, the vowels.

Thus, the expression describes vowels whose sequence reaches its conclusion at *bindu*.

### Why bindu becomes bindv-

Here's a fantastic sandhi rule.

**बिन्दु + अवसान**

*bindu + avasāna*

The final *u* of *bindu* changes to the corresponding semivowel *v* before the following vowel.

**u + a → va**

Thus:

**बिन्द्ववसान - bindvavasāna**

Now add the final element:

**-ga**

And the plural ending:

**बिन्द्ववसानगाः - bindvavasānagāḥ**

The entire compound is behaving exactly as Sanskrit phonology predicts.

There is nothing mysterious about the spelling.

And yet the **symbolic meaning of bindu** is about to become spectacularly metaphysical.

### The dot

# अं

aṃ

# ं

Anusvāra sign, symbolically associated here with bindu

In ordinary writing, the dot represents *anusvāra*. Its phonetic realization depends on linguistic context and recitational tradition.

In the Tantric phonemic system, the point also acquires the significance of concentrated manifestation.

The powers have unfolded.

Yet their multiplicity is gathered into a point.

Think of a seed.

An enormous tree can develop from something very small.

But the seed doesn't contain a fully assembled miniature tree with folded branches, little leaves, and a microscopic squirrel living inside.

It contains the conditions and organization for development.

Similarly, bindu symbolizes concentrated generative possibility.

The analogy is useful.

But Abhinavagupta is not proposing that physical cosmology has been experimentally deduced from the anatomy of a dot.

This is sacred phonemic metaphysics.

### What about the sixteenth?

The traditional series reaches *aṃ* and then *aḥ*, visarga.

The fifteenfold lunar structure and the sixteenth completion are related in this symbolism, but you must not collapse them into one undifferentiated list.

The commentary will later revisit the precise relations among the lunar digits, bindu, visarga and even a seventeenth *kalā*.

That's why I don't want to tell you that the sixteenth member is always simply "the full Moon" and call it a day.

Different interpretive levels are operating.

And Abhinavagupta is quite capable of making a single symbol participate in several of them.


## 6. सोमसूर्यौ - Soma-Sūryau: The Moon and Sun arrive together

The second half of verse five says:

**तदन्तः कालयोगेन सोमसूर्यौ प्रकीर्तितौ।**

*Tadantaḥ kālayogena somasūryau prakīrtitau.*

Now we've got some lovely grammatical features.

### Soma

**सोम - soma**

Moon, Soma, also the sacred Vedic drink and deity in other contexts.

Here the lunar significance is central.

### Sūrya

**सूर्य - sūrya**

Sun.

Now combine:

**सोम + सूर्य → सोमसूर्य**

*soma + sūrya → somasūrya*

A paired compound.

And here's the grammatical surprise.

**सोमसूर्यौ - somasūryau**

Ends in **-au**.

Why?

Because the Sanskrit has a **dual number**.

Not singular.

Not plural.

Dual.

Two entities considered together.

The Moon and the Sun.

Compare the masculine forms:

| Number | Sanskrit |
|--------|--------|
| Singular | **सूर्यः - sūryaḥ** |
| Dual | **सूर्यौ - sūryau** |
| Plural | **सूर्याः - sūryāḥ** |

The compound *somasūryau* names the pair.

So if you see **-au** ending a masculine *-a* stem, always consider whether you've encountered the nominative dual.

Now let's examine the rest.

### Kālayogena

**कालयोगेन - kālayogena**

This expression is particularly important because *kāla* has more than one relevant significance.

Ordinarily:

**काल - kāla**

Time.

And:

**योग - yoga**

Union, conjunction, connection, application.

Instrumental singular:

**योगेन - yogena**

By, through, or with connection.

Thus:

*kāla + yoga + -ena*

By the conjunction of *kāla*.

In the ordinary calendar, Sun and Moon are associated with the measurement and differentiation of time.

But the commentary develops *kāla* within the broader doctrine of divine powers, not merely as clock time.

This matters because in Trika, **Kāla as temporal limitation** and **Kālī or the divine power transcending and manifesting temporal order** belong to related but distinguishable contexts.

We shouldn't arbitrarily substitute one for another.

The text's Sun-Moon symbolism participates in the unfolding of temporal and expressive power from consciousness.

### Prakīrtitau

**प्रकीर्तितौ - prakīrtitau**

They are proclaimed, named, declared.

From:

*pra-* \+ *kīrtita*

*Kīrtita* means mentioned, proclaimed or praised, from √*kīrt*.

And the ending **-au** again marks dual number.

Thus:

**somasūryau prakīrtitau**

The Moon and Sun are declared.

Both words are dual.

The grammar agrees.

Astonishingly civilized, considering that the two things being described are about to represent the generative powers of the universe.

### The symbolic relationship

The Moon and Sun are associated with differentiated phases and complementary powers.

The lunar principle is frequently connected with cooling, nourishing and blissful manifestation.

The solar principle with illumination, heat and active expression.

But don't confuse those broad Tantric associations with a fully worked-out explanation of this exact sentence. Abhinavagupta will explicitly revisit the Sun-Moon-visarga relationship much later in the commentary on these same verses.

At this opening stage, we can establish what the root text actually says:

**The vowel-powers, the lunar tithis, bindu, and the paired Moon-Sun principles belong to one ordered account of manifestation.**

That's enough to understand why verse five is much more than a list of celestial objects.

The very organization of time and sound is being interpreted through Śakti.

And now Bhairava turns to the consonants.

Which is where the metaphysical spreadsheets become absolutely outrageous.


## 7. Verse six - The twenty-five principles hide inside the consonants

The Goddess hears:

**पृथिव्यादीनि तत्त्वानि पुरुषान्तानि पञ्चसु।**

**क्रमात्कादिषु वर्गेषु मकारान्तेषु सुव्रते॥**

*Pṛthivyādīni tattvāni puruṣāntāni pañcasu.*

*Kramāt kādiṣu vargeṣu makārānteṣu suvrate.*

Now the structure is wonderfully clear once you understand it.

The twenty-five principles running from Earth to Puruṣa are associated, in order, with the five consonant classes beginning with *ka* and ending with *ma*.

Let's break down the Sanskrit first.

### Pṛthivī

**पृथिवी - pṛthivī**

Earth.

In the philosophical system, the Earth principle represents solidity or the gross terrestrial element.

The word in the verse is:

**पृथिव्यादीनि - pṛthivyādīni**

Beginning with Earth.

Now here's the sandhi:

**पृथिवी + आदि → पृथिव्यादि**

*pṛthivī + ādi → pṛthivyādi*

The long *ī* becomes the corresponding semivowel *y* before the following vowel.

Thus **ī + ā → yā** at this boundary.

The complete compound takes the neuter nominative plural ending because it describes *tattvāni*.

### Tattvāni

**तत्त्वानि - tattvāni**

Principles of reality.

Singular:

**तत्त्वम् - tattvam**

Plural:

**तत्त्वानि - tattvāni**

Now the morphology of *tattva*.

**तत् - tat**

That.

**-त्व - -tva**

The state or condition of being.

Thus *tattva* originally conveys "thatness," reality or essential nature.

And in philosophical usage it becomes the technical term for an ontological principle.

The doubling of *t* comes from the form of *tat* plus the *-tva* suffix.

Don't read it as *ta-ta-va*.

The written sequence includes the consonant cluster.

### Puruṣāntāni

**पुरुषान्तानि - puruṣāntāni**

Ending with Puruṣa.

Break it into:

**पुरुष - puruṣa**

Person, spirit, or the finite conscious subject in this tattva classification.

**अन्त - anta**

End, limit, conclusion.

So:

*puruṣa + anta → puruṣānta*

Because:

**a + a → ā**

Then the neuter plural ending gives:

**puruṣāntāni**.

Now we know the full range:

From Earth.

Through the intermediate principles.

Up to Puruṣa.

Twenty-five principles.

And twenty-five consonants.

The numbers match.

### The five vargas

**क ख ग घ ङ**

ka-varga

Five gross elements

**च छ ज झ ञ**

ca-varga

Five subtle sensory qualities

**ट ठ ड ढ ण**

ṭa-varga

Five faculties of action

**त थ द ध न**

ta-varga

Five cognitive sense faculties

**प फ ब भ म**

pa-varga

Mind through Puruṣa

This is the phoneme-to-tattva arrangement Abhinavagupta explicitly develops when he returns to the literal explanation of verses 5-8.

And notice how perfectly suited the Sanskrit alphabet is to this kind of classification.

The consonants are already organized into phonetic groups.

Each *varga* contains five sounds sharing a place of articulation.

The Tantric teaching overlays those five-member structures with corresponding five-member metaphysical classifications.

Let's look at the groups.

### Ka-varga: The five gross elements

**क - ka**: Earth, *pṛthivī*.

**ख - kha**: Water, *ap*.

**ग - ga**: Fire, *tejas*.

**घ - gha**: Air, *vāyu*.

**ङ - ṅa**: Space, *ākāśa*.

The order proceeds from the grossest element to the subtlest among the five.

Now the phonetic reason these five letters form a class is quite different from the metaphysical reason they are associated with the elements.

Phonetically, they're traditionally classified as guttural or velar sounds.

In the esoteric interpretation, the five correspond to ontological principles.

Keep those two explanations distinct.

Otherwise you might begin claiming that the consonant *ka* physically contains soil.

And then Sanskrit linguistics will have to stage an intervention.

### Ca-varga: The five tanmātras

**च - ca**: Smell, *gandha*.

**छ - cha**: Taste, *rasa*.

**ज - ja**: Form or colour, *rūpa*.

**झ - jha**: Touch, *sparśa*.

**ञ - ña**: Sound, *śabda*.

These are the five subtle sensory qualities.

Now look at **ञ - ña**.

You encountered it inside:

**ज्ञ - jña**

The conjunct formed from ज् + ञ.

This is a perfect example of why learning individual consonants matters before tackling complex Sanskrit compounds.

The letter ञ is also the nasal of the palatal consonant class.

So the writing system, phonetics and Tantric classification intersect here.

But they remain distinguishable.

### Ṭa-varga: The five faculties of action

**ट ठ ड ढ ण**

The five retroflex consonants.

Abhinavagupta associates them in order with:

**पाद - pāda**: feet.

**पायु - pāyu**: organ of excretion.

**उपस्थ - upastha**: generative faculty.

**पाणि - pāṇi**: hands.

**वाक् - vāk**: speech.

And notice that *vāk* is here considered as a faculty of action, not simply the entire metaphysical hierarchy of supreme speech.

The same Sanskrit word can participate in different technical classifications.

Context matters.

### Ta-varga: The five senses of knowledge

**त थ द ध न**

The dental class.

These correspond in the commentary's order to:

**घ्राण - ghrāṇa**: smell.

**रसना - rasanā**: taste.

**चक्षुस् - cakṣus**: sight.

**त्वच् - tvac**: touch.

**श्रोत्र - śrotra**: hearing.

Now notice the difference between the subtle sensory object and the sense faculty.

*Gandha* is smell as a quality.

*Ghrāṇa* is the faculty through which smell is apprehended.

The distinction is the same as that between something seen and the capacity of sight.

The tattva system is carefully distinguishing the structures of experience.

And the alphabet is being used to represent them.

### Pa-varga: Mind through Puruṣa

Finally:

**प फ ब भ म**

These correspond to:

**मनस् - manas**: mind.

**अहङ्कार - ahaṅkāra**: ego-making function.

**बुद्धि - buddhi**: determinative intellect.

**प्रकृति - prakṛti**: primordial nature.

**पुरुष - puruṣa**: the finite conscious subject.

Now we've arrived at something fascinating.

The last member of the five consonant classes is:

**म - ma**

And the corresponding principle is:

**पुरुष - puruṣa**

The individual subject.

Remember the great phonemic symbolism of *aham*?

The *m* participates in the completion of universal I-consciousness.

Here the consonant sequence reaches the limited subject.

The philosophical relationship is not accidental in the Tantric interpretation.

The full universe of differentiated principles unfolds toward the limited experiencer.

And the limited experiencer remains grounded in the very consciousness from which the sequence is understood to emerge.

This is not an ordinary linguistic explanation of *ma*.

It's an esoteric correspondence.

And it prepares us for the movement beyond Puruṣa into the powers that establish limitation.


## 8. Verse seven - Four dhāraṇās, four semivowels, and the mechanics of limitation

Now we're approaching the really technical Trika material.

**वाय्वग्निसलिलेन्द्राणां धारणानां चतुष्टयम्।**

*Vāyvagnisalilendrāṇāṃ dhāraṇānāṃ catuṣṭayam.*

The verse invokes four *dhāraṇās*, using the names Air, Fire, Water and Indra.

And Abhinavagupta relates these to the four semivowels.

The four semivowels, conventionally called *antaḥsthā* consonants.

Now let's examine the Sanskrit.

### Catuṣṭayam

**चतुष्टयम् - catuṣṭayam**

A group of four.

From *catur*, four, through a collective formation.

The spelling contains a retroflex consonant cluster:

**ष्ट = ष् + ट**

Not the dental *sta*.

This word is extremely useful because Sanskrit philosophical texts constantly classify things into numbered groups.

*Traya*: a group of three.

*Catuṣṭaya*: a group of four.

*Pañcaka*: a group of five.

*Ṣoḍaśaka*: a group of sixteen.

Now you've got the basic pattern.

### Dhāraṇā

**धारणा - dhāraṇā**

Holding, supporting, sustaining, concentration.

The ordinary word comes from √**dhṛ**, to hold or support, through a causative-related formation.

But Abhinavagupta does not simply translate it here as meditation concentration.

He examines the principles that **hold things in differentiated manifestation**.

And this is where his argument becomes especially sophisticated.

The world is sustained by consciousness.

But within differentiated experience, objects appear as though they possess independent identities.

The limiting powers establish the appearance of separation.

Abhinavagupta describes the four powers through:

**राग - rāga**

Attachment or desire.

**विद्या - vidyā**

Limited knowledge.

**कला - kalā**

Limited agency or operative capacity.

**माया - māyā**

The power associated with differentiation.

These four form the particular group identified in his commentary with the semivowels *ya, ra, la,* and *va*.

Now compare this with the familiar five *kañcukas* in the standard thirty-six-tattva scheme.

There are ordinarily five limitations:

Limited agency, limited knowledge, attachment, temporal limitation and spatial or causal restriction.

Here the arrangement is different: Abhinavagupta's phonemic exposition does not independently enumerate all the same principles in the same form.

He later explicitly notes that **kāla** and **niyati** are not separately represented in this particular Trika enumeration.

This is important.

Do not force the text into a modern textbook diagram if the author himself is using a different technical arrangement.

### Why dhāraṇā means more than holding

Abhinavagupta's explanation is ingenious.

All things are sustained in the supreme consciousness.

But the same universal freedom also permits those things to appear as independent or differentiated.

So he examines the causative sense of *dhāraṇā*.

It's not simply:

"Things are held."

It's also:

"Things are made to appear as held in a particular relation."

He even discusses two levels of causative derivation.

This is the technical Sanskrit grammatical category:

**णिच् - ṇic**

A causative suffix.

For example:

√*gam* - to go.

**गमयति - gamayati**

He or she causes someone to go.

Now the causative doesn't always mean someone physically pushing somebody else around.

It changes the verbal construction to express causation.

And Abhinavagupta uses the distinction to discuss how consciousness manifests things as differentiated while remaining their underlying support.

The grammatical analysis is helping express the metaphysics.

Again.

The man is absolutely relentless.

### The philosophical point

Consider a blue object.

It appears as something distinct.

Over there.

Independent.

Not me.

But in Trika, the differentiated object is still manifest within universal consciousness.

The limiting powers establish the condition in which the object is apprehended as separate.

This is why Māyā and the related powers matter.

They're not simply a collection of arbitrary obstacles.

They explain the manifested structure of limited experience.

And through them the universal consciousness can appear as a world of finite subjects and objects.

**The power that sustains manifestation is also the power through which distinction becomes apparent.**

That's a far richer account than merely saying everything is one.

And it is why Abhinavagupta needs the technical tattva system.

The nondual ground doesn't erase the complexity of manifestation.

It makes that complexity philosophically intelligible.

; \]


## 9. The five final phonemes - The Brahmapañcaka

Bhairava continues:

**तदूर्ध्वं शादि विख्यातं पुरस्ताद्ब्रह्मपञ्चकम्।**

*Tadūrdhvaṃ śādi vikhyātaṃ purastād brahmapañcakam.*

Above that, the sequence beginning with *śa* is proclaimed as the five higher Brahma-principles.

Here the alphabet has reached its final five-member group:

**श - śa** Mahāmāyā


**ष - ṣa** Vidyā


**स - sa** Īśvara


**ह - ha** Sadāśiva


**क्ष - kṣa** Śakti

This is the fivefold correspondence given in Abhinavagupta's explanation of the passage.

Now, let's learn why the Sanskrit calls this the *brahmapañcaka*.

### Brahmapañcakam

**ब्रह्मपञ्चकम् - brahmapañcakam**

A group of five Brahma-principles.

Break it apart:

**ब्रह्म - brahma**

Here referring to the elevated divine principles.

**पञ्चक - pañcaka**

A group of five.

And the neuter ending:

**-म् - -am**

Thus *brahmapañcakam*.

The ordinary grammatical derivation is straightforward.

But the theological contents of the group are not identical in every Śaiva classificatory system.

And Abhinavagupta is perfectly aware of that.

### Why Mahāmāyā?

Here's a particularly interesting issue.

If you know the standard thirty-six-tattva system, you might expect the higher principles to run through Śuddhavidyā, Īśvara, Sadāśiva, Śakti and Śiva.

But the passage's arrangement includes **Mahāmāyā**.

Why?

Abhinavagupta introduces it to account for the condition of the beings known as *vijñānākalas*, sometimes called *vijñānakevalas*.

### Vijñānākala

**विज्ञानाकल - vijñānākala**

A technical class of limited conscious subjects in Śaiva cosmology.

These beings possess a form of pure awareness but have not fully recovered divine agency.

In the system's terms, a fundamental limitation associated with Āṇava Mala remains.

Now here's the philosophical problem.

If they are beyond the ordinary field of differentiated Māyā, but have not entered the complete divine freedom of higher knowledge and action, how is their condition to be situated?

Abhinavagupta argues for an intermediate principle associated with Mahāmāyā.

But he also acknowledges that different scriptures classify this condition differently.

Some associate it more closely with Māyā.

Others with Vidyā.

The point is that the classifications represent distinct ways of organizing a complex theological ontology.

And Abhinavagupta doesn't pretend the texts all use identical systems.

This is important historical evidence.

The Trika tradition wasn't simply handed down as one perfectly standardized diagram that everybody copied without disagreement.

Its scriptures preserve different arrangements of powers, levels and categories.

Abhinavagupta is trying to interpret them coherently.

### What does the difference mean?

From the standpoint of systematic philosophy, the distinctions matter.

A particular class of limited subjects is not identical with another.

A particular manifestation of knowledge is not identical with a different power.

But from the standpoint of Anuttara, the whole structure depends upon the same supreme consciousness.

So even the disagreement about classification becomes philosophically interesting.

The scriptures describe the manifestation through different procedures.

Yet the ultimate ground of what is being classified is not fundamentally divided.

**The map can vary without requiring a second Absolute.**

Now that is an important qualification to the familiar thirty-six-tattva chart.

Abhinavagupta's immediate classification contains thirty-four differentiated principles in the consonantal groups, while the *a*-led vowel field represents the supreme Śiva dimension. He also explicitly discusses the treatment of Kāla, Niyati and Mahāmāyā when reconciling this arrangement with other schemes.

So we should not pretend every phoneme corresponds mechanically to one and only one of the familiar thirty-six tattvas in every scripture.

The author himself is telling us it isn't that simple.

And, naturally, he will devote an enormous amount of commentary to the implications.


## 10. अमूला - Amūlā: Creation begins with A, yet has no ultimate beginning

Now the eighth verse becomes magnificent.

**अमूला तत्क्रमाज्ज्ञेया क्षान्ता सृष्टिरुदाहृता।**

*Amūlā tatkramāj jñeyā kṣāntā sṛṣṭir udāhṛtā.*

We have already seen the entire alphabet being mapped onto manifestation.

Now the Goddess is told how that creation is to be understood.

### The Sanskrit: Amūlā

**अमूला - amūlā**

This is one of the loveliest ambiguities in the whole passage.

It can be interpreted in two ways.

First:

**अ-मूला - a-mūlā**

Having *a* as its root.

Second:

**अमूला - amūlā**

Rootless, without a prior independent root.

The difference isn't necessarily visible in the written word.

It emerges through interpretation.

Now, grammatically, the second follows a normal privative formation:

*a-* \+ *mūlā*, having a root.

Negate the condition.

Rootless.

But the first interpretation treats the initial sound **अ - a** as the root of manifestation.

This is Tantric phonemic exegesis.

And here's the philosophical beauty.

**Creation is rooted in Anuttara, yet Anuttara itself is not a product requiring a further cause.**

The two interpretations converge.

From the standpoint of manifestation, there is a root.

The unsurpassable *a*.

But from the standpoint of ultimate reality, there is no prior independent root from which Anuttara itself must arise.

No cosmic factory.

No higher creator.

No source behind the source.

Now imagine somebody asking:

"What caused the Absolute?"

And someone replies:

"An even more absolute Absolute."

No.

We've already been through this.

We're not launching an infinite metaphysical franchise.

The *a* symbolizes the reality beyond which no further cause is required.

And the universe unfolds through that reality's own freedom.

### Kṣāntā

Now:

**क्षान्ता - kṣāntā**

In this context, ending at *kṣa*.

Be careful here.

The written word looks identical to another Sanskrit form associated with patience or forbearance.

But Abhinavagupta interprets it through the final phoneme of the alphabet.

**क्ष - kṣa**

The conjunct:

**क् + ष = क्ष**

And:

**अन्त - anta**

End, conclusion.

The compound *kṣa-anta* yields the sense of reaching the final phoneme.

Thus the creation extends:

**अ → क्ष**

From the initial *a* to the final *kṣa*.

The entire phonemic universe.

Now this is astonishingly appropriate within the tradition's doctrine of *mātṛkā*.

The alphabet expresses the differentiated powers of manifestation.

And those powers are gathered within the ultimate awareness symbolized by *a*.

The beginning is Anuttara.

The unfolding is Śakti.

And the entire manifestation is not outside their identity.

### Sṛṣṭi

**सृष्टि - sṛṣṭi**

Creation, emission, projection, manifestation.

From √**sṛj**, to release, send forth or emit.

Now compare:

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission, release.

Also connected with √*sṛj* through a different formation.

The root-family is worth remembering because it connects Sanskrit vocabulary for creation with the Tantric symbolism of emission.

And:

**उदाहृता - udāhṛtā**

Declared, stated, mentioned.

From *ud-ā-√hṛ*, in a formation meaning to cite or declare.

The feminine nominative singular agrees with *sṛṣṭiḥ*.

So the verse describes the creation as something declared or taught.

### Mantrāṇām and Vidyānām

Then:

**सर्वेषामेव मन्त्राणां विद्यानां च यशस्विनि।**

*Sarveṣām eva mantrāṇāṃ vidyānāṃ ca yaśasvini.*

Of all mantras and *vidyās*, O illustrious one.

Let's learn the endings.

**मन्त्र - mantra**

Mantra.

Genitive plural:

**मन्त्राणाम् - mantrāṇām**

Of mantras.

And:

**विद्या - vidyā**

Knowledge, sacred formula, divine wisdom, or a class of mantras, depending on context.

Genitive plural:

**विद्यानाम् - vidyānām**

Of *vidyās*.

Now the beautiful word:

**यशस्विनि - yaśasvini**

O illustrious one.

From:

**यशस् - yaśas**

Glory, fame, renown.

Through the possessive formation *yaśasvin*, illustrious.

The feminine form *yaśasvinī* gives the vocative:

**yaśasvini**.

Again, the Goddess is directly addressed.

The final **-i** rather than **-ī** matters.

Sanskrit inflection is doing real work here.

### The entire phonemic world as the matrix of mantra

The verses indicate that this phonemic manifestation is the basis for mantras and sacred *vidyās*.

And this is where the text leads into the doctrine of the alphabet as a generative matrix.

**मातृका - mātṛkā**

The Mother, or the alphabetic Mother-power.

The word is associated with *mātṛ*, mother.

Within Śaiva Tantra, *Mātṛkā* names the power manifested as the letters that constitute language and mantra.

Now think about this.

A mantra isn't simply an arbitrary sequence of noises.

Within the theological system, its phonemes are interpreted as expressions of divine powers.

The power of the mantra is therefore grounded in the universal expressive consciousness.

But the tradition also distinguishes conventional linguistic sound from mantra as an actual means of spiritual realization.

So don't jump to the claim that every random syllable combination automatically constitutes an effective mantra.

The doctrinal context matters.

The phonemic system reveals how language can be interpreted as participating in manifestation.

And the scripture is leading us toward a more detailed explanation of the relation between letters, divine powers and the supreme Heart.

; \]


## 11. But Abhinavagupta hasn't actually reached the consonant chart yet

Now here's the delightful textual twist.

Everything we've just covered is the initial structure of the four verses, supplemented by the correspondences Abhinavagupta eventually gives when he reaches their detailed explanation.

**In his actual commentary, he doesn't immediately start by filling in that chart.**

Instead he goes back to the first word.

*Atha.*

And then *ādyāḥ*.

And then the nature of Parā Vāk.

And then a long philosophical argument about nonconceptual cognition, the nature of self-awareness, and whether differentiation is already contained within the supreme consciousness.

Because, of course, before you classify the phonemes, you have to explain why phonemes can exist at all.

The man has a point.

If the letters are supposed to express the powers of consciousness, we must understand the relationship between an undivided ground and differentiated expressions.

So let's follow that argument.

### Atha - Does creation come after Anuttara?

The ordinary word:

**अथ - atha**

Then, now, subsequently.

Abhinavagupta asks what sort of succession the word indicates.

We just learned that the supreme consciousness doesn't depend upon ordinary temporal sequence.

So if the scripture now says "then," does that imply creation occurs after Anuttara in clock time?

Was there first an empty supreme consciousness, patiently waiting?

Then, after a few billion years, it decided to make some consonants?

"Right, it's Tuesday. Time to invent ṭha."

No.

The *atha* indicates a relation within the teaching and the manifestation.

Abhinavagupta allows succession as part of differentiated expression without treating the supreme reality as something temporally produced.

He says that while the Lord freely manifests the distinction between teaching and what is taught, the appearance of before and after is also possible.

But that succession doesn't establish a prior independent cause of the Absolute.

### Somānanda's esoteric reading

Abhinavagupta then recalls Somānanda.

Somānanda gives another interpretation of *atha*, involving *a* as Śiva and *tha* as Śakti.

The sounds are made to disclose their inseparability.

Abhinavagupta expressly acknowledges this interpretation, but declines to develop it fully.

He says he is not directly acquainted with the relevant underlying Āgama in the manner Somānanda was.

And I find this remarkable.

Abhinavagupta is perfectly capable of elaborate esoteric derivations.

But here he distinguishes what he has himself established from what he receives through his teacher's authority.

That's an intellectually significant moment.

He doesn't pretend to possess firsthand access to every source behind every traditional explanation.

And he refuses to make the present discussion depend entirely upon speculative arrangements of letters.

He even warns that differences of script and convention across places and times complicate such explanations.

Now that is an extraordinary observation from a Tantric metaphysician of language.

**Sacred phonemic interpretation matters, but not every contingent written arrangement can be treated as an unquestionable universal fact.**

The underlying recognition must be more fundamental than a particular orthographic convention.

And now Abhinavagupta returns to the actual matter:

The first vowel.


## 12. आद्य - Ādya: Not merely first in a list

Now comes one of the central claims of the whole chapter.

Abhinavagupta explains that *ādya* doesn't simply mean first in an arbitrary sequence.

It means pertaining to the beginning or origin.

**आद्य - ādya**

First, original, primordial.

From:

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning, origin.

Through a derivative formation.

Now the crucial distinction.

If I say *a* is the first letter of an alphabet, that's an ordering convention.

If I say *a* symbolizes the primordial ground of all phonemic manifestation, I'm making a metaphysical claim.

Abhinavagupta insists that the latter is what matters here.

The letters are not originally separate physical objects existing in some celestial storage cupboard.

Their ultimate reality is grounded in **Parā Vāk**.

### Parā Vāk - परा वाक्

Supreme Speech.

Now remember the four levels.

Parā.

Paśyantī.

Madhyamā.

Vaikharī.

The supreme level is not an articulated sequence of external sounds.

It is the undivided power of consciousness from which the differentiated expression of speech is understood to arise.

And Abhinavagupta says the letters possess, at this level, an eternal, nonconventional, unconstructed nature consisting of consciousness.

The vocabulary is important:

**अकृत्रिम - akṛtrima**

Not artificial, not manufactured.

**नित्य - nitya**

Eternal, enduring.

**संविन्मय - saṃvinmaya**

Consisting of consciousness.

Now we're finally at the heart of the argument.

The letters, in their ultimate nature, are not merely the externally articulated sounds produced by a tongue and vocal tract.

Their reality is grounded in the expressive freedom of consciousness.

And the written Sanskrit alphabet is a conventional representation of those sounds.

The metaphysical doctrine concerns the powers represented through them, not the ink marks themselves.

### The entire universe is already present within Parā

Abhinavagupta now describes Parā as containing the entire diversity of manifestation within her undivided consciousness.

He uses the argument that nothing can exist elsewhere independently of what is grounded in supreme awareness.

And here's an extraordinary implication.

If the differentiated universe is ultimately a manifestation of consciousness, then its possibilities cannot arise from an external source completely independent of consciousness.

They must be understood through its own freedom.

The world is present in Parā not as a collection of already separated physical objects packed inside a tiny metaphysical box.

But as the undivided ground of their possible manifestation.

Now imagine someone packing the entire universe into a suitcase.

Stars.

Planets.

Trees.

People.

Geckos.

Everything.

And then trying to explain how it all fits.

You'd have a fairly serious luggage problem.

Abhinavagupta's model is not that kind of spatial containment.

It concerns the ontological relation between undivided consciousness and its possible differentiated appearances.

**Multiplicity is not alien to the supreme reality simply because it has not yet manifested as a separated collection of objects.**

And now the commentary introduces a wonderful word.


## 13. प्रतिभा - Pratibhā: The first flash before the mind starts dividing everything

**प्रतिभा - pratibhā**

Intuitive illumination, immediate apprehension, the flash of understanding.

The word is associated with:

**प्रति - prati**

Towards, corresponding to, in relation to.

And √**भा - bhā**

To shine.

Now, don't assume the entire technical philosophical meaning can be obtained by mechanically adding those English glosses.

But the sense of manifestation and illumination is genuinely important.

Abhinavagupta describes supreme consciousness as free from the stains of contraction and limitation.

The distinctions that later appear as separate objects are not fundamentally outside its nature.

And this leads to an epistemological problem.

How can the experience of differentiated things arise from an undivided conscious ground?

### The blue-and-yellow problem

Imagine looking at a painting.

It contains blue.

Yellow.

Red.

Green.

Now you can focus on one colour.

You can say:

"This patch is blue."

Then:

"That patch is yellow."

And you've established two distinct determinate cognitions.

But there is also the possibility of apprehending the whole painting as one integrated scene.

The colours are distinguishable.

Yet the experience can be apprehended as a unity.

Abhinavagupta discusses this through examples of variegated cognition and the view of a whole city from an elevated position.

You may see numerous buildings, roofs and streets while simultaneously apprehending the city as one scene.

The unity does not require that all internal distinctions disappear.

Nor does differentiation require the annihilation of the original conscious field.

### Nirvikalpa and Savikalpa

Here come two absolutely essential terms.

**निर्विकल्प - nirvikalpa**

Without conceptual differentiation.

**सविकल्प - savikalpa**

With conceptual differentiation.

Break down the second:

**sa-** - with.

**vikalpa** - conceptual determination.

So *savikalpa* means accompanied by differentiation or conceptual construction.

And *nirvikalpa* contains the privative prefix *nir-*, indicating the absence of such differentiation.

Now here's the mistake you absolutely must avoid.

**Nirvikalpa does not simply mean unconscious blankness.**

Abhinavagupta's argument concerns a form of awareness prior to the explicit differentiation of conceptual objects.

This is not the same as the absence of awareness.

Nor is every ordinary nonconceptual experience automatically the complete realization of Śiva.

The point is that determinate cognition presupposes a conscious ground that cannot be reduced to the determinations themselves.

### The continuity problem

Suppose you see blue.

Then yellow.

Then remember having seen blue.

How is recognition of that sequence possible?

If each cognition were entirely disconnected from every other, how would memory and continuity be established?

Abhinavagupta argues that differentiated cognitions require a common conscious ground through which their relationships are apprehended.

He develops the issue in conversation with Buddhist epistemological accounts, including positions associated with *ālayavijñāna*, storehouse consciousness.

But his own conclusion isn't merely that there must be a hidden psychological memory-storage facility.

He argues for the self-revealing unity of consciousness as the ground of temporal and conceptual differentiation.

The distinction matters.

A memory store would itself require explanation.

What is the conscious reality in which both remembering and what is remembered are manifest?

That's the deeper Pratyabhijñā question.

### The limitation of conceptual thought

Now imagine you're looking at the painting again.

Before you start naming the individual colours, the entire scene is available.

Then the mind begins making distinctions.

Blue.

Yellow.

Red.

Left.

Right.

Foreground.

Background.

Those distinctions are useful.

But the unified manifestation of the painting isn't created by the labels.

Similarly, Abhinavagupta argues that the power of awareness is more fundamental than the individual conceptual constructions arising within it.

This doesn't mean concepts are false.

It means they're not independently self-sufficient.

**Every determinate cognition depends upon the conscious manifestation that makes determination possible.**

And now the connection with the alphabet becomes apparent.

The phonemes are differentiated.

They can be ordered.

Named.

Pronounced.

Grouped.

But their ultimate ground, in the Tantric system, is the undivided power of speech.

So Parā already contains the possibility of all phonemic differentiation.

The alphabet isn't an invasion of something foreign.

It's an articulation of what was not ultimately separate from consciousness in the first place.

; ; \]


## 14. उन्मेष - Unmeṣa: The opening in which recognition becomes possible

Now the commentary turns to the Spanda tradition.

And this is a particularly beautiful connection with the Śiva Sūtras.

Abhinavagupta invokes **unmeṣa**, the opening or emergence of conscious manifestation.

**उन्मेष - unmeṣa**

Opening, blossoming, emergence, the opening of the eyes.

Related to *ud-* and √*miṣ*, to open or close the eyes.

Now here's an attested passage he quotes from the Spanda teaching.

SPANDA VERSE QUOTED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA

**एकचिन्ताप्रसक्तस्य यतः स्यादपरोदयः।**

**उन्मेषः स तु विज्ञेयः स्वयं तमुपलक्षयेत्॥**

*Ekacintāprasaktasya yataḥ syād aparodayaḥ.*

*Unmeṣaḥ sa tu vijñeyaḥ svayaṃ tam upalakṣayet.*

The teaching concerns the emergence of another cognition while one is engaged with a particular thought. That opening is to be recognized directly.

### Let's break down the Sanskrit

**एक - eka**

One.

**चिन्ता - cintā**

Thought, reflection, consideration.

**प्रसक्त - prasakta**

Attached, engaged, absorbed.

So:

**एकचिन्ताप्रसक्तस्य - ekacintāprasaktasya**

Of one absorbed in a single thought.

The final **-sya** marks genitive singular.

Then:

**अपर - apara**

Another, further, subsequent.

**उदय - udaya**

Arising, emergence.

So:

**अपरोदय - aparodaya**

The emergence of another.

Now:

**विज्ञेय - vijñeya**

To be known or recognized.

From *vi-* and √*jñā*, with a gerundive formation.

And:

**उपलक्षयेत् - upalakṣayet**

One should notice, observe or recognize.

A third-person singular optative form.

Now why is this so important?

Because the teaching directs attention toward the transition of cognition itself.

You are occupied with a thought.

Another cognition arises.

The transition reveals something about the power of awareness.

The point isn't merely that thoughts change.

Everybody knows thoughts change.

The point is to recognize the conscious power through which their arising and changing become manifest.

### The Kallaṭa connection

Abhinavagupta also cites Kallaṭa on the possibility of supreme knowledge and agency associated with a subtle moment or *tuṭi*.

**तुटि - tuṭi**

A minute interval, tiny division of time.

The associated teaching speaks of extraordinary capacities; those should be understood as traditional spiritual claims, not experimentally established results.

But the philosophical point concerns the relationship between temporal differentiation and an undivided conscious ground.

A minute interval is still a division.

Yet the reality of awareness is not necessarily exhausted by the succession of intervals.

The discussion therefore connects the analysis of cognition with the larger doctrine of time.

And that's where the lunar symbolism will eventually return.

Because the phases of time, the sequence of vowels, and the manifestations of consciousness are being interpreted through a common metaphysical framework.

### The great danger: Taking blankness for realization

Abhinavagupta is particularly concerned about confusing the absence of differentiated cognition with genuine recognition.

Simply eliminating thoughts is not sufficient.

A momentary blank doesn't necessarily reveal the fullness of conscious freedom.

The crucial issue is whether the nature of consciousness itself is recognized.

And this is why he invokes Spanda.

*Spanda* isn't just physical vibration.

Nor is it simply a measurable oscillation of the nervous system.

It refers to the living dynamism of consciousness, the power through which manifestation and self-awareness are inseparable.

The opening of cognition can become an occasion for recognizing that power.

But the metaphysical recognition is not reducible to a particular bodily sensation.

Now imagine someone saying:

"I have eliminated every thought. Therefore I have attained the Absolute."

And Abhinavagupta asks:

"Have you recognized the nature of consciousness, or have you simply managed to make your mind very quiet?"

And the meditator replies:

"I don't know, but my knees are absolutely fucked."

A surprisingly common epistemological difficulty.


## 15. The three Malas return - Why the infinite looks finite

The commentary now reconnects the argument to the fundamental limitation of the individual subject.

The decisive impurity is:

**आणवमल - āṇavamala**

The limitation associated with finite subjectivity.

Abhinavagupta treats this as the root of the other principal limitations.

Remember the pattern:

A limited subject takes itself to be incomplete.

The apparent world of independent objects arises.

Action becomes governed by the drive to acquire, avoid, possess and defend.

The resulting condition reinforces bondage.

The Sanskrit for ignorance:

**अज्ञान - ajñāna**

Not-knowing.

From:

*a-* \+ *jñāna*.

The initial **a-** negates knowledge.

And in this context, ignorance is not merely a shortage of information.

It's a fundamental misrecognition of the nature of the experiencer.

You can know an extraordinary amount of philosophy and still experience yourself as a completely isolated, deficient individual.

That's why accumulated intellectual knowledge is not automatically liberation.

### The aged tree of becoming

Abhinavagupta composes a verse comparing the condition of bondage to an old tree whose seed is ignorance.

And he describes the burning away of that seed in the fire of supreme consciousness.

Now, the image is extremely evocative.

The tree of conditioned existence has branches.

Leaves.

Roots.

And an enormous network of consequences.

But the seed contains the principle through which the whole structure develops.

If the fundamental misrecognition is removed, the basis of that bondage is transformed.

The same imagery appears in multiple Indian traditions, though with different metaphysical interpretations.

Here the key is recognition of the universal consciousness.

### The mirror image

Abhinavagupta describes the supreme Śakti as containing the manifold universe in the immaculate mirror of her own being.

And he brings the discussion back to Parā Vāk.

All the differentiated powers of speech, from the supreme level down into articulated phonemes, remain grounded in the same self-luminous consciousness.

Their apparent multiplicity does not establish independently existing sources.

And he concludes this preliminary philosophical discussion by saying that the text beginning with *athādyāḥ* is explaining the complete creative power of Parā.

**Only then does he begin the explicit consonant-to-tattva mapping we've already learned.**

That's the actual argumentative sequence:

From the first word *atha*, to the primordial *a*, to Parā Vāk, to the nature of nonconceptual awareness, to recognition and the malas, and finally to the precise distribution of tattvas through the alphabet.

It's not simply a decorative cosmological chart.

The chart rests upon a philosophical account of how consciousness can manifest differentiation without becoming ultimately divided.

## 16. What the whole alphabet is really saying

Now put everything together.

We began with:

**अ - a**

The symbol of Anuttara.

Then the vowel powers.

The lunar phases.

Bindu.

The Sun and Moon.

Then the five classes of consonants.

The gross elements.

The subtle qualities.

The faculties of action and knowing.

The mind.

The individual subject.

The powers of limitation.

And the higher divine principles.

Finally, the whole phonemic manifestation is associated with the powers extending through **क्ष - kṣa**.

And the question becomes:

What is the reality of the entire system?

Abhinavagupta's answer is still the same.

**The differentiated powers of language and manifestation are not ultimately separate from the supreme consciousness.**

Now this is the most important thing to understand before moving deeper into the doctrine of Mātṛkā.

The alphabet isn't the ultimate reality in the sense that the world is physically manufactured by an independent collection of written signs.

The letters symbolically disclose the powers of consciousness.

Their arrangement reflects the unfolding of manifested distinctions.

And the entirety of that unfolding is grounded in Anuttara.

So the ultimate philosophical movement runs from undivided consciousness toward the differentiated universe.

And through recognition, from the apparent independence of the universe back into its conscious ground.

This is why the Tantric phonemic system can connect mantra, cosmology and liberation.

They aren't three unrelated activities.

They're three ways of approaching the relation between consciousness and its expressive power.

### The Moon and Sun are not an afterthought

And now we can see why the tithis and solar-lunar powers were introduced before the consonant chart.

The tradition is not merely interested in a static hierarchy of objects.

It also understands manifestation through cycles, succession, temporal differentiation and reabsorption.

The Moon waxes and wanes.

The Sun and Moon establish calendrical relations.

The vowel sequence unfolds and culminates.

The consonants articulate differentiated principles.

And the whole manifestation arises within the nonsequential freedom of consciousness.

So time is not independent of the metaphysics.

Nor is sound.

Nor are the senses.

Nor is the individual subject.

All of them are structures of manifestation.

And this is exactly what Abhinavagupta is about to investigate more fiercely in the continuation.

### Why the differing tattva schemes matter

We're now standing at a technical difficulty that he explicitly acknowledges.

The Parātrīśikā's phonemic order does not map onto all other Śaiva scriptural systems in precisely the same manner.

Some arrangements begin with Śiva and proceed through the elements.

Others work backward from the grossest principles.

Others assign letters differently or treat certain tattvas as included within broader categories.

For a lesser commentator, this would be a catastrophe.

A whole system built on correspondences, and suddenly different scriptures don't agree.

But Abhinavagupta's metaphysics has an answer.

The ultimate ground of manifestation is not itself restricted to one contingent ordering of the powers.

And the diversity of the scriptural arrangements must be interpreted in relation to the larger doctrinal system.

He will examine this problem rather than simply ignoring it.

The question is:

**If the letters express the powers of consciousness, why do different Tantric scriptures assign those letters to different ontological principles?**

That is the precise problem awaiting us next.

And it's much more interesting than pretending there has only ever been one universally accepted chart.

## The final insight - The universe is a sentence the Absolute never finished speaking

The image is irresistible.

Not literally a sentence in a human language.

But a manifestation of expressive power.

The undivided consciousness becomes apparent as differentiated meaning.

The differentiated meaning becomes apparent through sound.

The sounds are organized into powers.

Those powers are associated with the tattvas.

And the tattvas constitute the manifold structures of experience.

The entire process belongs to Śakti.

Yet Śakti is inseparable from Śiva.

So the universe can be understood as the self-expression of consciousness without requiring a second ultimate substance.

That's the deeper purpose of this phonemic cosmology.

Not to replace empirical science with a Sanskrit alphabet.

Not to claim that twenty-five consonants mathematically prove the existence of twenty-five metaphysical principles.

But to interpret the manifold structure of experience through a sacred system in which language, cognition and manifestation share a common conscious ground.

And the really beautiful thing is that we're learning the grammar at the same time.

When you look at:

**अथाद्यास्तिथयः सर्वे स्वरा बिन्द्ववसानगाः।**

You're no longer simply memorizing an exotic mystical sentence.

You can begin to see:

*Atha.*

*Ādyāḥ.*

*Tithayaḥ.*

*Svarāḥ.*

*Bindvavasānagāḥ.*

The sandhi begins to separate.

The compounds become transparent.

The grammatical endings reveal their functions.

And gradually the Sanskrit itself becomes meaningful.

Which is precisely why the tradition places so much weight on speech.

Not because sound magically excuses the need for understanding.

But because understanding the living structure of expression can become an entry into understanding how consciousness manifests.

And now we've arrived at the next great question.

**If the entire alphabet is a manifestation of one undivided reality, why does its order change between different Tantric scriptures?**

Abhinavagupta is about to take this problem very seriously.

And the answer will bring us much deeper into Mālinī, Mātṛkā, alternative tattva arrangements, and the supreme freedom of Śiva.

The alphabet has opened.

Now the metaphysical arguments begin.
