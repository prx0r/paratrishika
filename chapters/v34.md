## I. Establishing the text: this is the conclusion of the root tantra

We resume exactly at the transition:

**किमेवमुपासायां भवति? इत्यवतरति ।**

*kim evam upāsāyāṃ bhavati? ity avatarati.*

“What happens through worship in this manner? The following passage is introduced.”

This begins on printed **KSTS p. 269**, corresponding to **Gnoli p. 279**.

There is an important correction to the previous instalment's anticipated numbering. In the 1918 KSTS commentary, the next half-verse is followed by prose, and then a four-pāda verse is labelled **33**, the next **34**, and the concluding verse **35**. Somadeva Vasudeva's GRETIL transcription follows that commentarial segmentation. Marino Faliero's independent root text, however, combines the initial half-verse with the beginning of the subsequent passage and finishes with verse **36**.

These are distinct editorial segmentations of substantially the same sequence, not two separately composed endings.

| Material | KSTS commentary | Independent root |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *kṛtapūjāvidhiḥ…* | Unnumbered introductory half-verse | First half of verse 33 |
| *ādyantarahitaṃ bījam…* | Verse 33 | Spans verses 33-34 |
| *yān yān kāmayate…* | Verse 34 | Spans verses 35-36 |
| *evaṃ mantraphalāvāptiḥ…* | Verse 35 | Final part of verse 36 |

The two witnesses also differ substantively in the line about obtaining knowledge: the KSTS commentary reads **asmāt pratyakṣatām eti**, whereas the independent root reads **ajñaḥ pratyakṣatām eti**. We'll examine that difference rather than quietly choosing whichever sounds more impressive. ; ; ; \]

Another distinction matters enormously: the eight Heart-verses occurring within Abhinavagupta's explanation are **not eight further numbered root-tantra verses**. They are an inserted verse sequence in the running commentary, whose source is not securely named at that point. The GRETIL text identifies them as eight individual verses; the KSTS transcription preserves their Sanskrit but contains significant alternate readings.

And the extraordinarily elaborate lunar meditation that follows is not one uniform instruction attributed to Abhinavagupta himself. The commentary distinguishes explanations attributed to **Bhaṭṭa Dhaneśvaraśarman**, **Abhinavagupta's own teachers**, and **other interpreters**.

Those voices must remain separate.

Now let's finish this magnificent bastard's argument properly.


## II. “Whoever Remembers the Seed Becomes Accomplished.” But What Does Remembering Mean?

### KSTS p. 269 - Gnoli p. 279

The root tantra says:

**कृतपूजाविधिः सम्यक्स्मरन्बीजं प्रसिद्ध्यति ।**

*kṛtapūjāvidhiḥ samyak smaran bījaṃ prasiddhyati.*

**Literal translation:**

“One who has performed the procedure of worship, properly remembering the seed, attains accomplishment.”

That looks simple enough.

Perform the worship.

Remember the seed.

Become accomplished.

Excellent. Three steps. We finally have a manageable spiritual instruction.

Except Abhinavagupta immediately asks what the verse means by having “performed the worship.”

And gives an answer that completely changes the relation between meditation and ordinary life.

### Start with the grammar

**कृत - kṛta**

Done, performed, accomplished; the past passive participle of √*kṛ*, to do or make.

**पूजा - pūjā**

Worship, honour, reverence; related to the verbal family of *pūj*, to honour or worship.

**विधि - vidhi**

Prescription, method, ordinance, prescribed procedure.

**kṛta-pūjā-vidhiḥ**

A compound describing someone by whom the method of worship has been performed.

The final **-ḥ** marks masculine nominative singular.

This is not a complete independent sentence by itself.

It describes the subject of the following finite verb.

**सम्यक् - samyak**

Properly, correctly, fully.

An adverb.

Before the following *smaran*, its final *k* remains represented within the continuous utterance.

**स्मरन् - smaran**

Remembering, recollecting.

A masculine nominative singular present active participle of √*smṛ*, to remember.

The participle is contemporaneous with the main verb: the person is remembering the seed while attaining the result.

**बीजम् - bījam**

Accusative singular neuter of *bīja*, seed.

Here specifically the supreme Heart-seed discussed throughout the commentary.

**प्रसिद्ध्यति - prasiddhyati**

Third-person singular present, from *pra-* \+ √*sidh*, to succeed, accomplish, become established.

This is an important distinction.

It is not the same verbal form as *prasidhyati* in a hypothetical simplified reading; the transmitted **ddhy** sequence reflects the relevant consonantal structure.

**प्रसिद्ध्यति** contains द् + ध् + य in the conjugated form.

The verb signifies accomplishment or becoming established.

But Abhinavagupta is about to distinguish two very different senses of that accomplishment.

### The metre

Two pādas, each of eight syllables:

| Pāda | Syllables | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *kṛtapūjāvidhiḥ samyak* | kṛ-ta-pū-jā-vi-dhiḥ-sam-yak | 8 |
| *smaran bījaṃ prasiddhyati* | sma-ran-bī-jaṃ-pra-sid-dhya-ti | 8 |

The first pāda begins with syllabic **ṛ**.

In **कृत**, the little sign beneath क is the dependent form of ऋ, not a decorative mark indicating a consonantal R.

The second contains **स्म**, स् + म, in *smaran*.

And the cluster **द्ध्य** in *prasiddhyati* is a conjunct sequence, not three separately syllabified consonants with inherent vowels.

The root half-verse is metrically regular.

No philological acrobatics required.

Unfortunately the metaphysics are about to resume their usual behaviour.


## III. Two ways of fulfilling the ritual: the liberated person and the practitioner who still needs a path

### Abhinavagupta's prose, Gnoli pp. 279-280

Now the commentator introduces the distinction upon which the entire ending depends.

**एवमनवरतं व्यवहारेष्वपि बीजं स्मरन्नेव**

*evam anavarataṃ vyavahāreṣv api bījaṃ smarann eva.*

“Thus, remembering the seed uninterruptedly, even amid ordinary activities…”

There.

**Even amid ordinary activities.**

Not exclusively during worship.

Not exclusively during meditation.

Not exclusively while sitting motionless with an elaborate arrangement of flowers.

The word is **vyavahāra**.

Ordinary dealings.

Activity.

Conduct.

Practical life.

The entire business of being a person in the world.

And Abhinavagupta says that continuous remembrance of the Heart remains possible there.

This isn't an afterthought. It is the culmination of the distinction between a ritual that must be performed in a particular setting and the recognition that grounds every setting.

### Anavarata - अनवरत

**an-** - negative prefix.

**avarata** - ceased, stopped, interrupted; associated with *ava-* \+ √*ram*, to cease or rest.

**anavarata** - uninterrupted, unceasing.

The adverbial accusative **anavaratam** means continuously.

The same word can express the constancy of an activity without requiring that the activity be mechanically repeated in the same outward form every second.

And in the present case, Abhinavagupta immediately specifies what is being continued.

**Smaraṇa.**

Remembrance.

But what sort?

### Vyavahāreṣu - व्यवहारेषु

Locative plural of *vyavahāra*.

The suffix **-eṣu** marks the plural locative.

It means “in the activities,” “amid transactions,” or “in ordinary dealings.”

The locative is important.

He is not requiring a complete withdrawal from *vyavahāra* as the preliminary condition.

He explicitly includes it.

One can be involved in differentiated activity while the recognition of the Heart continues.

Now remember Utpaladeva's earlier declaration that a person recognising the universe as their own manifestation does not lose divine sovereignty when conceptual thought arises.

The same principle has returned in practical form.

**Thought does not necessarily abolish recognition. Activity does not necessarily abolish recognition.**

The question is how the subject apprehends the activity.

### The astonishing explanation of remembrance

Abhinavagupta says:

**स्मरणादेव कृतपूजाविधिः**

*smaraṇād eva kṛtapūjāvidhiḥ.*

“By remembrance itself, the procedure of worship has been performed.”

The ablative **smaraṇāt** means “from remembrance” or “because of remembrance.”

Before *eva*, sandhi produces *smaraṇād eva*.

This is not merely a poetic compliment paid to someone who thinks about Śiva while washing dishes.

It follows from the metaphysics established in Parts XXXII and XXXIII.

If the supreme Heart is the reality of the worshipper, the worshipped, the offering, the action, and the entire field of manifestation, then recognising that Heart is not one more restricted ceremonial activity.

It is recognition of the principle that makes all the activities possible.

And this is why the text can describe remembrance itself as the fulfilment of worship.

But be precise.

The Sanskrit **does not** imply that every casual recollection of the word *Śiva* equals full liberation.

The relevant remembrance is that of the supreme Heart already known *tattvataḥ*-according to its actual reality.

The mere sound of a name and the realisation of what that name signifies are not automatically identical.

### The extraordinary status of the liberated person

Abhinavagupta now describes the practitioner as having entered the form of the venerable Lord Bhairava:

**भगवद्भैरवभट्टारकरूपसमाविष्टः**

*bhagavadbhairavabhaṭṭārakarūpasamāviṣṭaḥ.*

Then the crucial expression:

**जीवन्मुक्त एव**

*jīvanmukta eva.*

“Liberated while living.”

There it is.

Not liberated only after the body dies.

Not liberated by travelling to another cosmic realm.

Not liberated by obtaining a new body made of particularly expensive divine light.

**Liberated while living.**

And the ordinary activity continues.

### Jīvanmukti - जीवन्मुक्ति

This is a central Sanskrit philosophical concept.

**jīvat** - living, alive, from √*jīv*, to live.

**mukta** - released, liberated, a past passive participle of √*muc*, to release.

The compound *jīvanmukta* describes one who is liberated while still alive.

The feminine abstract noun *jīvanmukti* means liberation during life.

In Abhinavagupta's system, that is perfectly intelligible because liberation does not require the destruction of the manifest universe.

It concerns the recognition of one's supreme nature.

The body can continue.

The senses can continue.

Thought can continue.

Activity can continue.

But the limited identification that constituted bondage is no longer taken as ultimate.

### The magnificent description of the world as play

Abhinavagupta describes a person whose manifold appearances are produced through the wonder of their own supreme consciousness.

The passage uses the compound:

**भावक्रीडाडम्बर - bhāvakrīḍāḍambara**

The splendid or elaborate play of manifested beings and states.

**bhāva** - being, state, manifest entity.

**krīḍā** - play, sport, activity undertaken in freedom.

**āḍambara** - display, splendour, elaborate manifestation.

The compound does not mean that an awakened person literally becomes the personal author of every event in the empirical universe.

It expresses the recognition that the entire field of differentiated manifestation belongs to the freedom of the supreme consciousness.

That is why the world can be described as *krīḍā*.

Play.

Not because suffering is trivial.

Not because every event is pleasant.

But because the supreme consciousness is not understood as acting from an externally imposed deficiency.

Its manifestation belongs to its freedom.

### Remembrance is not retrieving an absent object

Here Abhinavagupta delivers a particularly subtle explanation.

**अनुभव एवायमावर्तते न त्वन्यत्किंचित्**

*anubhava evāyam āvartate na tv anyat kiṃcit.*

“This very experience recurs; nothing else whatsoever.”

Now that is extraordinary.

Ordinary memory seems to work by recalling something absent.

You saw a tree yesterday.

Today you remember it.

The tree is not presently in front of you, but a representation of it arises.

Yet the supreme Heart is not an absent object that must be fetched back into consciousness.

It is the ever-present ground of experience.

Therefore *smaraṇa* here is not simply retrieving a lost spiritual experience.

It is the renewed or continuing recognition of the awareness that is already present.

**The remembrance does not manufacture the Heart. It is the Heart's own reality becoming manifest through recognition.**

That is what distinguishes it from endlessly trying to reproduce some extraordinary state you once experienced.

And that brings us to the second type of practitioner.

### The practitioner who has not yet entered the Heart's potency

Abhinavagupta explicitly introduces:

**उपासकस्तु**

*upāsakas tu.*

“But the practitioner…”

He describes someone whose heart has not yet entered the full potency of the supreme reality.

The key distinction is **ananupraviṣṭa**-not yet entered into or penetrated by the power in question.

This person may nevertheless engage in:

**क्रमपूजा - kramapūjā**

Sequential worship.

The prescribed steps.

One after another.

Mantra.

Nyāsa.

Offerings.

Contemplation.

Repetition.

And through this process, the practitioner may come to apprehend the true Heart.

The commentary says that the potency may arise gradually, through the greatness of sequential worship, or through the guru's gracious transmission.

It uses the memorable expression:

**प्रसन्नगुरुभट्टारकवदनकमलात्**

*prasannagurubhaṭṭārakavadanakamalāt.*

“From the lotus-mouth of the gracious venerable guru.”

The ablative ending **-āt** marks the source.

The teacher's mouth is compared to a lotus.

A conventional and beautiful religious image.

But the philosophical point is not merely that the teacher pronounces special syllables.

The teacher can communicate the *mantravīrya*, the potency of the mantra, through the instruction that brings its meaning to realisation.

### Mantravīrya - मन्त्रवीर्य

**mantra** - the sacred expression.

**vīrya** - potency, strength, power, efficacy.

The compound describes the special capacity associated with the mantra in the tantric system.

We should distinguish three things.

Correctly producing the phonemes.

Understanding their doctrinal meaning.

Actually recognising the reality they express.

These are related but not interchangeable.

Abhinavagupta's distinction between the spontaneously liberated person and the gradual practitioner is fundamentally concerned with the last of these.

And he ends the explanation by affirming that the gradual practitioner too may become *jīvanmukta*.

This is the important continuity.

There are different ways in which the relevant recognition may arise.

But the recognised reality is not a different substance in each case.

The highest recognition and the sequential practice are not rival ultimate metaphysical systems.

They are different relations to the same Heart. ; \]

### The deliciously uncomfortable consequence

Imagine two practitioners.

The first has spent years performing meticulous worship.

They know the precise placement of every mantra.

They can recite the twenty-sevenfold powers without hesitation.

They have memorised the ritual instructions.

The second has no elaborate outward performance, but possesses the complete recognition described by the text.

Which one has achieved the supreme purpose?

Abhinavagupta's answer is not ambiguous.

The second, if their knowledge is genuine.

But now imagine a third person.

They have read the doctrine.

They like the sound of immediate liberation.

They have decided that performing any practice would be beneath someone of their spiritual greatness.

Yet they remain completely identified with the limited autobiographical ego.

That person cannot simply claim the status of the first kind of liberated practitioner.

The text's criterion is **recognition**, not disdain for practice.

And this is why Abhinavagupta maintains both possibilities.

The supreme reality does not need to be produced.

But the limited subject's understanding may nevertheless require clarification, reflection, instruction, or discipline.

The absolute is not made more absolute by practising.

The practitioner may become better able to recognise what the absolute means.

That is the difference.

And now we come to the eight-verse meditation.

The beautiful, baffling, ridiculously ambitious description of the Heart as the one awareness that expands into the world and contracts it back into itself.


## IV. The Eight Verses of the Heart: First, Everything Appears in It

### Gnoli p. 280 onward

Immediately after distinguishing spontaneous recognition from sequential worship, the commentary introduces an eight-verse sequence.

The first verse begins:

**यत्रान्तरखिलं भाति**

*yatrāntar akhilaṃ bhāti.*

“Within which the entirety shines…”

And continues with the decisive expression:

**हृदयं परमं**

*hṛdayaṃ paramam.*

“The supreme Heart.”

The verse identifies the supreme Heart with the single *sphurattā*-the flashing or self-manifesting luminosity-within which everything shines and which is manifest everywhere.

This is the whole Trika doctrine in concentrated form.

### Sphurattā - स्फुरत्ता

From √*sphur*, to flash, throb, quiver, become manifest, or shine forth.

The abstract suffix *-tā* creates a feminine noun referring to the condition of flashing or manifestation.

It is related to the broader Spanda vocabulary, but the words are not always interchangeable technical synonyms.

The important point is that awareness is not treated as an inert container.

It is self-manifesting.

Things appear within it.

And their appearing is inseparable from it.

The verse says that this flashing reality is one.

**ekā** - one, feminine nominative singular.

That is not a numerical assertion that only one empirical object exists.

It is a metaphysical assertion about the undivided nature of consciousness.

### Two directions of manifestation

The verse expresses a reciprocal relation.

Everything appears within awareness.

And awareness shines throughout everything.

These are not two independently existing processes.

They describe the same nondual relation from different perspectives.

From the perspective of a particular experience, one may say that the object appears in consciousness.

From the perspective of supreme manifestation, one may say consciousness expresses itself as the entire field of appearing objects.

The first sounds like a phenomenological description.

The second is the stronger Trika metaphysical claim.

And we mustn't confuse them.

The fact that experience is conscious does not automatically establish every aspect of the Trika ontology.

But within Abhinavagupta's system, the self-manifesting nature of awareness is the foundation from which the broader metaphysics proceeds.

### Why the Heart cannot simply be a spatial location

Suppose you search for this Heart.

You notice sensations in the chest.

You visualise a radiant sphere.

You experience warmth.

You hear a subtle internal sound.

You perceive an expanded sense of awareness.

These may all be meaningful experiences.

But each remains a manifestation.

The supreme Heart is not exhausted by any one of them.

The bodily heart can be a ritual and contemplative locus.

It is not an independently existing container into which universal consciousness has somehow been squeezed.

The same distinction was crucial to the banyan-seed analogy.

The seed's causal power illustrates manifestation.

The supreme Heart is the ground of the very possibility of manifestness.

And that is why it can be called omnipresent without being physically spread out like a gas.

It is not another object occupying a measurable volume.

Now the verse has established the fundamental luminosity.

And the second verse introduces the animals.

The donkeys and horses have arrived.

God help us.


## V. The She-Ass and the Mare: Why Abhinavagupta's Metaphysics Has Suddenly Become a Veterinary Examination

The second Heart-verse begins:

**रासभी वडवा वापि**

*rāsabhī vaḍavā vāpi.*

“A she-ass or even a mare…”

This is genuinely present in the Sanskrit.

And the verse goes on to describe the movement of **vikāsa**, expansion, and **saṃkoca**, contraction, followed by delight in the Heart.

Now, what on earth are we supposed to do with that?

The answer is illuminated by an independently verifiable parallel in Abhinavagupta's *Tantrāloka* 5.58-60.

There, he explicitly invokes the she-ass and mare as comparisons while explaining the conjunction of expansion and contraction, the joy associated with bodily movement, and the higher contemplative symbolism of Śiva-Śakti. ](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/tantraloka-sanskrit-text/d/doc486110.html); (https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/tantraloka-sanskrit-text/d/doc487157.html); (https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/tantraloka-sanskrit-text/d/doc487158.html)\]

So this is not a randomly introduced animal proverb.

It belongs to a larger tantric physiological and metaphysical vocabulary.

### Rāsabhī - रासभी

A female donkey.

From *rāsabha*, donkey, with a feminine formation.

### Vaḍavā - वडवा

A mare, female horse.

The spelling with **ḍ** is important.

Do not automatically Sanskritise the form into an unrelated word because the retroflex consonant looks unusual.

### What is the actual comparison?

The passage invokes the bodily processes of expansion and contraction, associated with pleasure and generative capacity.

These are the phenomena through which Abhinavagupta illustrates the relation between manifestation and reabsorption.

Within the larger Kaula setting, sexual physiology can serve as a concrete example of creative power, bodily enjoyment, and the union of differentiated forces.

But the philosophical interpretation is not simply that sexual pleasure is identical with liberation.

Nor does the occurrence of muscular contraction establish the metaphysical truth of Śiva-consciousness.

The bodily example functions as an analogy.

### Vikāsa - विकास

Expansion, opening, unfolding.

Related to *vi-* \+ √*kas*.

### Saṃkoca - संकोच

Contraction, drawing together, restriction.

Related to *sam-* \+ √*kuc*.

### Hṛṣyati - हृष्यति

“He or she rejoices,” “becomes delighted,” from √*hṛṣ*.

The third-person singular present describes delight.

And these three elements provide the structure of the analogy.

Expansion.

Contraction.

Delight.

In the tantric theological interpretation, manifestation expands from the undivided ground into differentiated forms.

Those forms can be reabsorbed into the ground.

And consciousness's relation to its own manifestation is understood through *ānanda*, self-relish or bliss.

### Why not simply use a flower?

A flower unfolds.

It would provide a perfectly respectable image of expansion.

A seed develops.

An ocean produces waves.

Why use animals and bodily pleasure?

Because the Kaula tradition does not confine the sacred to aesthetically respectable examples.

The body itself participates in manifestation.

Desire.

Pleasure.

Movement.

Generativity.

The expressive powers of physical life.

These can all become occasions for contemplating Śakti.

The she-ass and mare are not intrinsically outside the divine reality because they happen to be ordinary animals.

Their forms and processes are also manifestations.

Indeed, the deliberately unglamorous character of the example makes the metaphysical point more forceful.

The supreme Śakti is not restricted to golden statues, lotus flowers, expensive incense, and unusually photogenic yogins.

If the entire universe is her manifestation, the universe includes animals behaving like animals.

Even a donkey.

Especially a donkey.

The absolute doesn't get to declare itself omnipresent and then suddenly become embarrassed by equine anatomy.

### But is the Heart the sexual organ?

Not simply.

The example must be interpreted in its specific Kaula context.

The bodily locus can function as a site of generative and expressive power.

But the supreme Heart is not reducible to the anatomical organ.

The physiological event illustrates the dynamic relation between manifestation, contraction, and delight.

The ontological principle is broader.

And that is where the verse's reference to the world's birthplace becomes significant.

### Jagajjanmadhāma

**jagat** - world.

**janma** - birth, arising.

**dhāman** - abode, locus, seat, domain.

The compound concerns the locus or ground of the world's arising.

In the metaphysical interpretation, that ground is consciousness.

The bodily analogy is not a literal description of animals physically generating the entire cosmos.

It uses the relation between generative power and bodily manifestation to illuminate a more fundamental claim.

The whole universe arises within the supreme Heart.

And the same Heart encompasses its withdrawal.

That is the movement being contemplated.

### The parallel in Tantrāloka

The *Tantrāloka* passage is especially valuable because it places the animal example directly within the contemplation of **visarga**, emission.

And we've spent many instalments examining visarga.

It is the final component of **सौः**.

The two dots of the written visarga are associated with the emitting power of consciousness.

But the metaphysical significance is not exhausted by the graphic sign.

Visarga is the dynamic possibility of manifestation itself.

The animal analogy supplies a bodily image of generativity and contraction.

The metaphysical doctrine concerns Śiva-Śakti.

And the contemplative practice concerns recognising their unity.

Now Abhinavagupta moves from the animals to the **sauṣumna Heart**.

The central channel.

The great bliss of the two.

And the vibration through which creation is manifest.


## VI. The Third Heart-Verse: The Central Channel, the Two Great Blisses, and the Vibration of Creation

The third verse places the Heart within the extraordinary phrase:

**सौषुम्नहृदयान्तरे**

*sauṣumnahṛdayāntare.*

“In the interior of the Heart associated with Suṣumnā.”

And describes the Heart as:

**स्पन्दमानं … सृष्टिलक्षणम्**

*spandamānaṃ … sṛṣṭilakṣaṇam.*

“Vibrating … characterised by creation.”

The verse also invokes the **great bliss of the two**, continuing the preceding imagery of expansion and contraction.

Now we enter a particularly delicate part of the philosophy.

Because there is a temptation to interpret every reference to *spanda*, *suṣumnā*, and bodily bliss as a description of measurable physiological energy moving through a specific anatomical tube.

That is not what the text establishes.

### Sauṣumna - सौषुम्न

The word is an adjective derived from **Suṣumnā**, the central channel in yogic subtle-body discourse.

The exact anatomy and significance of Suṣumnā vary across tantric systems.

In some it is related to the central channel of vital movement.

In others, its interpretation becomes deeply metaphysical.

Here the compound associates the Heart with the central principle of manifestation and reabsorption.

The locative ending **-antare** means “within the interior.”

Thus the phrase does not simply designate a coordinate in physical space.

It belongs to a contemplative body understood through divine powers.

### Spandamāna - स्पन्दमान

Present middle participle from √*spand*, to throb, move, pulsate, or quiver.

The neuter accusative singular **spandamānam** qualifies the Heart that is to be contemplated.

Now recall our earlier investigation.

At the supreme level, *spanda* is not physical motion from one location to another.

If consciousness were to move from one coordinate to another in order to manifest, then it would already be confined within space.

But space itself belongs to the differentiated manifestation.

Therefore the ultimate *spanda* concerns the self-expressive dynamism of consciousness, not merely displacement through space.

Its apparent movement is manifestation.

Its withdrawal is reabsorption.

But consciousness does not become an independently separate object in either phase.

### The extraordinary phrase *ubhayamahānanda*

**ubhaya** - both.

**mahā** - great.

**ānanda** - bliss.

The compound expresses the great bliss of the twofold power or their conjunction.

In the relevant Trika setting, the twofoldness of Śiva and Śakti is not a metaphysical separation into two ultimate substances.

The distinction expresses consciousness as luminous and self-aware, possessing the power of manifestation.

The supreme bliss is the self-relish of that conscious freedom.

And the Heart is the locus of that relation in the contemplative formulation.

### Upāsīta - उपासीत

The verb in the verse is an optative middle form of *upa-* \+ √*ās*, to sit near, attend upon, worship, or contemplate.

“One should contemplate.”

The optative supplies a prescription.

But the object being contemplated is not an inert thing.

It is the vibrating Heart characterised by creation.

Thus the contemplative act is directed toward the conscious ground of the very act of contemplation.

A recursive structure.

The person meditates on consciousness.

Through consciousness.

Within consciousness.

And the philosophical purpose is to recognise that the apparently separate meditator is not ultimately independent of the reality contemplated.

The meditation doesn't create the Heart.

It serves to disclose it.


## VII. The Fourth Heart-Verse: Meditating, Remembering, Thinking, or Just Doing Things

Now we encounter perhaps the most practically important verse of the eight.

It begins:

**ध्यायन् स्मरन् प्रविमृशन् कुर्वन् वा यत्र कुत्रचित्**

*dhyāyan smaran pravimṛśan kurvan vā yatra kutracit.*

“Whether meditating, remembering, reflecting, or acting, anywhere whatsoever…”

And it culminates in the Heart's emergence and the repose found within it.

This is the clearest articulation so far of **recognition during ordinary activity**.

Not a state exclusively tied to a particular posture.

Not a prohibition on thinking.

Not the requirement that the world disappear.

The verse includes actual human functioning.

### Dhyāyan - ध्यायन्

Meditating, contemplating.

Masculine nominative singular present participle from √*dhyai*, to contemplate or meditate.

The participle describes the subject as engaged in meditation.

### Smaran - स्मरन्

Remembering.

The same word we encountered in the root half-verse.

Again from √*smṛ*.

The repetition connects the inserted Heart-verses directly with the immediate commentary on *kṛtapūjāvidhiḥ*.

### Pravimṛśan - प्रविमृशन्

Reflecting, examining, apprehending.

The prefix sequence *pra-* \+ *vi-* with √*mṛś*, to touch or examine, belongs to the linguistic family central to **vimarśa**.

This is particularly significant.

The verse does not merely include the cessation of thought.

It includes reflective activity.

The subject can actively enquire.

Distinguish.

Consider.

Understand.

And recognition may remain present.

### Kurvan - कुर्वन्

Doing, acting.

Masculine nominative singular present active participle of √*kṛ*.

The same verbal root underlying *karman*, action, and *kartṛ*, agent.

And notice the participial ending **-van** in the strong stem.

The verse includes actual performance.

The subject acts.

The body moves.

The world remains differentiated.

But the activity is not necessarily taken as ultimately separate from consciousness.

### Yatra kutracit

Anywhere whatsoever.

The expression is deliberately expansive.

Not only in a temple.

Not only at a sacred site.

Not only in the presence of a teacher.

Not only during a prescribed hour.

Anywhere.

This follows directly from the doctrine of the Heart.

If supreme consciousness is the ground of manifestation, its reality cannot be confined to a particular sacred location.

The sacred location can be meaningful.

But it cannot own consciousness.

### Viśrānti - विश्रान्ति

Rest, repose, settling, finding one's ground.

From *vi-* \+ √*śram*, to rest or become weary, through the nominal formation.

In Abhinavagupta's philosophical vocabulary, *viśrānti* has a deeper significance.

It is not merely physical relaxation.

It is the resting of consciousness in its own nature.

The end of the need to establish oneself through something external.

The subject ceases to take the differentiated manifestations as independent foundations of identity.

And recognises its ground in the supreme Self.

That is why *viśrānti* can coexist with activity.

The body may be moving.

The mind may be working.

But the fundamental identification has changed.

### A phenomenological example

Suppose you are walking.

A sound appears.

A sensation appears.

You notice the movement of the body.

A thought arises.

Another thought follows.

Ordinarily you may experience the whole situation through a small, enclosed sense of self:

“I am inside here, managing these events.”

But in the Pratyabhijñā enquiry, you examine what makes the whole situation manifest.

The sound is known.

The bodily sensation is known.

The thought is known.

Even the sense of being a little controller is known.

The question becomes:

What is the nature of the awareness within which these appear?

A recognition of that awareness need not require all the particular contents to stop.

That is the point of the verse.

**Dhyāna, smaraṇa, vimarśa, and kriyā are not automatically enemies of the Heart.**

They can become modes through which its reality is recognised.

### The crucial textual variant

The KSTS electronic transcription gives a difficult form resembling **mollased** in the last pāda, whereas the GRETIL transcription has **prollased**, “should shine forth” or “arise radiantly.”

The GRETIL form is grammatically and contextually more intelligible, but I cannot establish from the electronic transcription alone whether the KSTS difference is a printed variant or a character-recognition error.

The philosophical sense is the emergence of the Heart's manifestation.

We should not build a new technical doctrine upon the doubtful electronic form. ; \]

And now we turn to the fifth verse.

The entire universe of conceptual differentiation is brought into the same Heart.


## VIII. The Fifth Heart-Verse: Even Conceptual Thought Is Not Outside the Absolute

The fifth verse brings together three domains.

**jñānaṃ vaikalpikam** - conceptual knowledge.

**tattvāni** - the fundamental principles of manifestation.

**bhuvanābhogāḥ** - the expanses of worlds.

It also includes the hierarchies of knowers and divine powers, beginning with Śiva and extending toward limited beings and the Mothers.

This is not a casual list.

It returns us to the entire architecture of the preceding commentary.

The thirty-six tattvas.

The cosmic spheres.

The stages of subjectivity.

The powers of perception and action.

The divine Mothers.

All of them.

And the verse places their reality within the one supreme Heart.

### Vaikalpika - वैकल्पिक

Related to **vikalpa**, conceptual distinction or determinate thought.

The adjective *vaikalpika* means conceptual, conditioned by determinate cognition, or belonging to conceptual construction.

The word is not necessarily a synonym for error.

Conceptual thought can be correct or incorrect.

The relevant distinction is that it involves determinate articulation.

A concept distinguishes one thing from another.

A sentence asserts a relation.

A judgement identifies a property.

A memory reconstructs an earlier event.

Such operations are differentiated.

But differentiation itself is not ultimately outside consciousness.

This was one of the foundational discoveries of the earlier instalments.

### The mistake of spiritual thought-suppression

Imagine a practitioner who believes:

“Śiva is nondual, therefore I must stop thinking.”

A thought arises.

They attempt to suppress it.

Another thought appears:

“I must not think.”

They try to suppress that.

Then:

“Am I succeeding at not thinking?”

Congratulations.

The mind has invented an administrative department dedicated to the suppression of its own activity.

Abhinavagupta's doctrine is much sharper.

The issue is not the mere existence of *vikalpa*.

It is the misrecognition of the differentiated activity as something fundamentally separate from consciousness.

The liberated person can think.

The mind can distinguish.

Language can operate.

The philosophical understanding is that the entire activity belongs to the manifestation of the one supreme Self.

That does not make every thought true.

It means that the occurrence of thought does not destroy the conscious reality in which it occurs.

### Tattvāni - तत्त्वानि

Neuter nominative or accusative plural of *tattva*.

**tat** - that.

**-tva** - abstract condition or state.

Thus *tattva* can be explained as “that-ness,” or fundamental reality.

In Śaiva cosmology, it designates the ontological principles through which manifestation is classified.

There are thirty-six in the developed Trika account.

But the Heart is not simply an additional thirty-seventh object sitting above them.

It is the supreme reality through which the entire hierarchy becomes manifest.

### Bhuvanābhoga

**bhuvana** - world, realm, inhabited domain.

**ābhoga** - expanse, extent, enjoyment, encompassing range, depending on context.

The compound evokes the expanses of worlds.

And this is important because the verse doesn't restrict the Heart to a single person's psychological state.

Its metaphysical scope includes the entire cosmic hierarchy.

The ultimate subject is not merely the little empirical ego.

It is the universal consciousness.

And now the verse prepares its conclusion.

The differentiated forms have their own remarkable appearances.

But those appearances return to the one supreme *saṃvid*.

Consciousness.


## IX. The Sixth Heart-Verse: The Universe Is a Spectacularly Elaborate Painting Made of Awareness

The sixth verse is one of the most textually interesting.

The KSTS transcription reads, in part:

**स्वं स्वं विचित्रं विन्दन्तः**

*svaṃ svaṃ vicitraṃ vindantaḥ.*

“Each finding its own variegated…”

It continues with **svarūpaṃ pāramārthikam**, its ultimately real intrinsic nature, and concludes with the various manifestations approaching the supreme consciousness.

GRETIL instead reads:

**svaṃ svaṃ vicitram evāntaḥ-**

and has different wording in the final half, including **eṣanti** instead of KSTS **yānti**.

The KSTS apparatus itself reports an alternative corresponding to part of this divergence. These are substantive differences, because they alter how the participles and the finite verb connect. The two textual forms cannot simply be recombined into one sentence. ; \]

### Vicitra - विचित्र

Variegated, diverse, extraordinary, remarkable, beautifully differentiated.

From *vi-* \+ *citra*, with *citra* signifying brightness, variety, or remarkable appearance.

The word belongs naturally to Abhinavagupta's account of manifestation.

The world is astonishingly varied.

Different forms.

Different sensations.

Different capacities.

Different levels of cognition.

Different beings.

Different powers.

But the variation does not imply independent ultimate substances.

### Citrīkaroti

The verse uses the causative or denominative formation associated with *citra*.

To make variegated.

To render extraordinary.

To make into a wondrous display.

The universe is therefore not merely a static list of objects.

It is an ongoing display of differentiated manifestation.

And this is directly related to **camatkāra**.

The wonder or relish of consciousness.

The supreme consciousness manifests a field of endlessly differentiated appearances.

The appearances possess distinct forms.

But their manifestness remains inseparable from the one consciousness.

### The painting analogy

Imagine an enormous painting.

Mountains.

Rivers.

Animals.

Cities.

Stars.

People.

Light.

Shadow.

Movement suggested through form.

Each detail is different.

But all belong to the same painted field.

This is not a perfect analogy for Trika.

A painting is made from material pigments and a substrate.

Consciousness in Abhinavagupta's system is not another material substrate beneath objects.

But the analogy reveals the relevant distinction.

**Differentiation does not automatically destroy the unity of the field in which it occurs.**

The universe can contain indefinitely varied phenomena while remaining grounded in one conscious reality.

That is what the verse's imagery of *vicitra* expresses.

### Why this is not a denial of the world

A shallow nondual teaching might say:

“The world is unreal. Ignore it.”

But Abhinavagupta's position is more sophisticated.

The world is real as manifestation of Śiva's freedom.

Its differentiated forms are not necessarily independent ultimate substances.

But their appearing is not nothing.

The richness of experience is not an embarrassing mistake that consciousness must abandon.

It is part of the expressive capacity of Śakti.

This is why the verse celebrates the variegated display.

And why the liberated person need not become indifferent to all manifested forms.

A thought.

A landscape.

An animal.

A work of art.

A sensation.

A conversation.

A philosophical enquiry.

Each may become an occasion for recognising the same conscious ground.

The point is not to insist that they are identical in every empirical respect.

It is to see that their ultimate manifestness is not separate.

And now the seventh verse provides what may be the most practical formulation in the entire eight-verse sequence.


## X. The Seventh Heart-Verse: Worship While Eating, Walking, Working, Knowing, and Doing Absolutely Anything

The seventh verse begins with a remarkable compound:

**दशाद्रव्यक्रियास्थानज्ञानादिष्वपि**

*daśādravya-kriyā-sthāna-jñānādiṣv api.*

“Even amid states, substances, activities, places, knowledge, and so forth…”

And ends with the declaration that such worship is **satatoditā**-ever-arisen, continuously manifest.

This is one of the sharpest expressions of living nonduality in the whole passage.

### Daśā - दशा

State, condition, phase, situation.

Not **daśa**, ten.

The distinction is a long final ā.

This is especially important because we've recently been discussing ten directions and numerous numerical arrangements.

Here the word refers to conditions or states, not the number ten.

### Dravya - द्रव्य

Substance, material, thing, object of a particular type.

The term has highly developed philosophical meanings in systems such as Vaiśeṣika.

Here it belongs to the broad classification of differentiated experiences and their contents.

### Kriyā - क्रिया

Action, activity, operation.

From √*kṛ*, to do.

### Sthāna - स्थान

Place, location, situation.

From √*sthā*, to stand.

### Jñāna - ज्ञान

Knowledge, cognition, understanding.

From √*jñā*, to know.

Now notice the range.

States.

Things.

Actions.

Places.

Knowledge.

The text is not limiting recognition to one particular sort of experience.

It is explicitly extending the scope across different categories of activity and manifestation.

### Aśaṅkayaiva - अशङ्कयैव

Without doubt, apprehension, or hesitation.

The precise transmitted spelling differs in the digitised witnesses, but the GRETIL reading is grammatically clear.

**a-** - negation.

**śaṅkā** - doubt, suspicion, hesitation.

The adverbial form expresses proceeding without the obstructing doubt relevant to the experience.

It does not mean recklessness in ordinary life.

Nor does nondual recognition justify ignoring practical danger.

The doubt being overcome is the misrecognition of ultimate separation.

### Saṃkrāma - संक्राम

Crossing, transition, movement into or across.

Related to *sam-* \+ √*kram*, to step, move, cross, or proceed.

The verse associates this movement through the differentiated domains with worship.

And its key predicate is:

**सततोदिता - satatoditā.**

Continuously risen.

Ever manifest.

Always present.

From:

**satata** - continuous, uninterrupted.

**udita** - risen, arisen, appeared, from *ud-* \+ √*i*, to rise or go forth, in the relevant participial formation.

The feminine form agrees with **pūjā**, worship.

Thus:

**Worship is continuously manifest.**

That is the philosophical claim.

And it is much stronger than saying:

“Try to think of Śiva occasionally throughout the day.”

The real point is that the entire field of activity can be apprehended as a manifestation of the supreme consciousness.

### Does that mean every action is automatically sacred?

A crucial distinction.

In the ultimate metaphysics, every event arises within consciousness.

But it does not follow that every action is ethically correct.

Nor that every impulse is spiritually wise.

Nor that harmful conduct is justified simply because it is a manifestation.

The metaphysical reality of an event and its ethical evaluation are distinct questions.

Abhinavagupta's passage concerns the possibility of recognising the conscious ground of activity.

It does not establish that violence, deception, or exploitation become acceptable when performed by someone who uses the vocabulary of nonduality.

This distinction is indispensable.

Otherwise the doctrine of divine freedom would become a theological excuse for the unrestricted ego.

And that would be precisely the kind of misrecognition the whole work is trying to overcome.

### How ordinary activity becomes worship

Suppose you eat.

There is hunger.

There is the sight of food.

There is the movement of the body.

Taste appears.

Pleasure or displeasure appears.

The mind recognises the experience.

Ordinarily the subject may understand this entirely through a small, separate sense of self confronting objects.

But a Trika contemplative enquiry asks whether the whole process can be recognised as a manifestation within consciousness.

The food is not denied.

The senses are not denied.

The action is not denied.

The person is not instructed to pretend the meal is unreal.

Rather, the question concerns the ultimate status of the entire experience.

What makes it manifest?

What is the nature of the subject to whom it appears?

And is that subject ultimately separate from the conscious ground?

This is precisely the movement expressed by the meal prayer we've examined elsewhere:

**ब्रह्मार्पणं ब्रह्म हविः**

*brahmārpaṇaṃ brahma haviḥ.*

The offering is Brahman.

The oblation is Brahman.

The act of offering belongs to the same supreme reality.

That Bhagavadgītā verse has its own philosophical context, but the resemblance to Abhinavagupta's nondual interpretation of ritual is clear.

The ordinary act becomes an occasion for contemplating the relation between agent, action, object, and consciousness.

The important thing is the recognition-not merely the verbal declaration.

And the same enquiry can be applied to walking, speaking, studying, writing, or working.

### The life of a liberated person

This is why the earlier declaration of *jīvanmukti* matters.

The liberated person need not cease to function.

They may engage with the world.

They may perceive.

They may remember.

They may teach.

They may laugh.

They may perform religious practices.

They may undertake ordinary responsibilities.

But the activity no longer requires the assumption that consciousness is a little object trapped inside a world fundamentally outside itself.

The metaphysical identification has changed.

That is the point of the verse.

The world doesn't need to disappear.

The person doesn't need to become a vegetable.

The supreme Heart doesn't lose its reality because somebody starts doing their laundry.

And then the eighth verse introduces the crucial qualification.

Because, after proclaiming that worship is continuously present, Abhinavagupta still explains why sequential worship has a place.

Of course he does.

The man is determined that neither the contemplatives nor the ritualists should get away with an oversimplification.


## XI. The Eighth Heart-Verse: The Continuous Worship and the Ritual Calendar Are Not Enemies

The final verse contrasts **kramapūjana**, sequential worship, with the higher sense of worship already described.

It explicitly mentions:

**कुलपर्वपवित्रकैः**

*kulaparvapavitrakaiḥ.*

“Together with Kula observance-days and the pavitraka ceremonies.”

Then it identifies the full or correct mode of worship within the Trika teaching.

This matters because it prevents us from turning the previous seven verses into a wholesale rejection of differentiated practice.

### Kramapūjana - क्रमपूजन

**krama** - sequence, order, progression, arrangement.

**pūjana** - worship.

The compound means sequential worship.

The kind of worship that proceeds through distinct prescribed actions.

And the preceding ritual block provided exactly such a sequence.

Nyāsa.

Mantra.

Binding.

Direction-sealing.

Sprinkling.

Flowers.

Seat.

Goddess.

Self-offering.

Fire.

Now the eighth Heart-verse says that this sequential worship has a place within the full Trika system.

It is not identical with the uninterrupted recognition of the Heart.

But neither is it automatically meaningless.

### Kulaparva - कुलपर्व

**kula** - family, group, aggregate, or the specialised Kaula religious system.

**parva** - joint, division, festival, sacred observance-day, or significant occasion.

Here the relevant sense concerns Kula observances or prescribed festival periods.

The term does not merely mean an ordinary family holiday.

### Pavitraka - पवित्रक

A purification or consecration observance, particularly associated with the completion or rectification of ritual worship.

Within tantric ritual literature, *pavitraka* rites can have detailed procedural requirements.

They belong to the maintenance of an initiatory and ritual discipline.

This is exactly the kind of material one might expect Abhinavagupta to reject after identifying knowledge with the highest initiation.

But the text does not do that.

It distinguishes the different levels.

### The philosophical point

The supreme Heart is continuously manifest.

Its reality is not produced by ritual activity.

But a practitioner who has not yet established the relevant recognition may engage in sequential worship.

And within a particular religious discipline, that worship can include calendrical observances and consecration rites.

The two claims are compatible.

**The ultimate reality is independent of the rite as a product, while the rite may be meaningful as a means of recognition or as a specific religious practice.**

The distinction prevents two opposite errors.

The first is ritual absolutism: the belief that supreme consciousness is manufactured by a prescribed series of actions.

The second is ritual nihilism: the belief that because supreme consciousness is already present, no differentiated practice can have any meaning.

Abhinavagupta refuses both.

His higher teaching concerns recognition.

His practical religious system still accommodates prescribed observances.

And the eighth Heart-verse places both within the larger Trika framework.

### The significant editorial complication

The 1918 KSTS edition adds a lengthy explanatory footnote here, discussing different classes of *parva*, the *pavitraka* rite, its timing, ritual materials, and the consequences of neglecting prescribed observances.

That note is **not an additional paragraph of Abhinavagupta's prose**.

The running text supplies the eighth verse and then introduces an external quotation with **yathoktam**, “as has been said.”

The editor's extensive ritual elaboration belongs to the explanatory apparatus. It must not be imported into Abhinavagupta's voice or used to construct a contradiction that the running passage itself does not contain. ; \]

This distinction is especially important here.

Because the editor's note discusses specific ritual obligations in considerable detail.

Abhinavagupta's own argument is more focused.

The supreme recognition remains the ultimate principle.

The differentiated rites possess their proper place within the religious system.

And now the commentary introduces a further quotation that sets out a hierarchy of religious practices.


## XII. The Anonymous Quotation: The Highest Teaching, the Highest Liberation, and the Ritual That Completes the Others

Abhinavagupta introduces:

**यथोक्तम् - yathoktam.**

“As has been said.”

The ensuing quotation contains a striking series of comparisons.

It associates the highest form among different classes of things with their respective qualities and names **Trika** as the supreme teaching among systems of instruction.

The crucial expressions include:

**शासनानां त्रिकं शास्त्रं**

*śāsanānāṃ trikaṃ śāstram.*

“Among teachings, the Trika scripture…”

And:

**मोक्षाणां भैरवी स्थितिः**

*mokṣāṇāṃ bhairavī sthitiḥ.*

“Among liberations, the Bhairava state.”

It then identifies **samāpatti**, contemplative absorption or attainment, with the highest form of worship, and refers to heroic observances and the Kula festival and *pavitraka* rites.

The quotation's immediate original source is not securely named in the available GRETIL witness. It should remain an unattributed quotation, not be assigned to the *Mālinīvijayottara*, *Śivadṛṣṭi*, or some convenient lost scripture without evidence.

### The distinction between śāsana and śāstra

**śāsana** - teaching, instruction, doctrine, religious system.

From √*śās*, to instruct, govern, or discipline.

**śāstra** - treatise, authoritative teaching, discipline, system of instruction.

Also connected with √*śās*.

The quotation's hierarchy is theological.

It asserts the superiority of Trika from within the Trika religious worldview.

It is not a neutral academic demonstration that every other Indian religious system has been objectively disproved.

But the internal principle is intelligible.

The highest teaching is held to encompass the powers explained by more restricted systems while revealing their ultimate unity.

### Bhairavī sthiti

**Bhairavī** - belonging to Bhairava, or the divine feminine form associated with him, depending on context.

**sthiti** - standing, condition, abiding, state.

The phrase concerns the Bhairava-state as the highest liberation.

It is not simply a geographical destination.

Nor a sensory pleasure.

Nor a temporary visionary state.

Its meaning belongs to the recognition of universal consciousness.

### Samāpatti - समापत्ति

Attainment, convergence, absorption, complete entry into a state.

From *sam-* \+ *ā-* \+ √*pad*, to attain or arrive.

In different Indian philosophical and contemplative traditions, *samāpatti* has distinct technical meanings.

Here it belongs to the higher understanding of worship.

The practitioner enters into or rests in the reality being contemplated.

The distinction between worshipper and worshipped is no longer taken as ultimate.

Thus the worship is not merely a sequence of outward acts.

It is a mode of conscious identification with the divine ground.

### Why the quotation still praises pavitraka

The quotation also affirms the special place of *pavitraka* within differentiated ritual systems.

That may initially seem inconsistent with the previous declarations of the supremacy of recognition.

But once again, the distinction is one of levels.

A particular rite can have an important function within a ritual discipline.

It may complete or rectify the performance of worship in that system.

But it does not thereby become the producer of the supreme consciousness.

The higher metaphysical reality remains independent of its conditioned manifestations.

The differentiation of religious means is included, not absolutised.

And that is why Abhinavagupta can cite the quotation without abandoning his preceding argument.

### The omitted Apabhraṃśa material

Immediately after the external quotation, the 1918 KSTS text contains a short nonclassical-language passage, beginning in its electronic transcription with a form resembling:

**फुरइ फुरणम्…**

The GRETIL *Parātriṃśikāvivaraṇa* explicitly states in its header that it **omits the Apabhraṃśa passages**.

This is a real limitation of that transcription, not evidence that the passage was absent from the 1918 edition.

The available digitised Devanāgarī is too uncertain in several places for me to present a complete secure text, grammatical reconstruction, or independent literal translation. I will not invent a cleaned-up Apabhraṃśa verse and attribute it to Abhinavagupta.

Its presence and position are established: it appears after the quotation on Kula observances and before the next substantial prose transition. A proper edition would require checking the printed page image and, ideally, the source manuscripts. ; \]

That is a philological limitation, not a philosophical licence.

And now we finally reach the transition that changes the remaining direction of the work.

Abhinavagupta has completed the account of the supreme Heart and continuous recognition.

But he still needs to explain why the tantra provides a detailed meditation technique promising particular accomplishments.

This is where Somānanda returns.

And the distinction is much sharper than the modern opposition between “effort” and “effortlessness.”


## XIII. Somānanda's Intervention: If You've Recognised Śiva, Why Are We Still Giving Instructions?

### Gnoli p. 281 - KSTS pp. 272-273

Abhinavagupta now states that the unsurpassable nature has been explained extensively.

He describes a level at which the usual *bhāvanā*, deliberate contemplative construction, has no further role as a producer of the ultimate reality.

The relevant work is the firm establishment of recognition.

He speaks of **prasaṃkhyāna**, repeated or thorough consideration, reaching the point of stable *camatkāra* and a deeply assimilated understanding of the Heart.

But then he says:

**सिद्धिप्रेप्सुषु तु योगो वक्तव्यः**

*siddhiprepsuṣu tu yogo vaktavyaḥ.*

“But for those desiring accomplishments, yoga must be explained.”

This is a major turn.

The highest reality has already been established.

The practitioner who possesses its recognition does not need another action to create it.

But a person seeking particular *siddhis*, or whose recognition has not yet become established, may undertake specific practices.

And Abhinavagupta explicitly invokes the role of **niyati**, determinate order, in the attainment of particular results.

### Why freedom doesn't eliminate causality

The supreme consciousness is understood as free.

But its manifestation includes structured relations.

The differentiated world has regularities.

Actions have consequences.

Specific processes have particular requirements.

The fact that these processes are ultimately manifestations of consciousness does not mean the empirical ego can ignore all their conditions.

This is why Abhinavagupta does not simply say:

“Imagine something and it will happen because you are Śiva.”

That would confuse the universal freedom of consciousness with the restricted abilities of a particular person.

The distinction is foundational.

The ultimate subject is not the little autobiographical ego.

And the limited ego doesn't become an omnipotent independent entity by learning the word *svātantrya*.

### Somānanda, Śivadṛṣṭi 7.9

Abhinavagupta quotes a half-verse:

**तथापि चित्रकर्मार्थमुपायो वाच्य आदरात्**

*tathāpi citrakarmārtham upāyo vācya ādarāt.*

“Nevertheless, for the sake of varied or particular activity, the means should be carefully explained.”

The quotation is independently verifiable in **Śivadṛṣṭi 7.9**. Its surrounding verses distinguish recognition of the omnipresent Śiva from the means required for particular effects. Somānanda explicitly continues by saying that a method should be taught for the accomplishment of observable results.

The distinction is magnificent.

Because it resolves an apparent contradiction.

On one hand:

Śiva is already the ultimate reality.

Recognition of that reality is liberation.

No action can manufacture the supreme consciousness.

On the other hand:

Particular results can depend on particular actions.

If you want to learn a skill, you generally need the relevant training.

If you want to produce an object, you need the relevant means.

If a traditional yogic system describes a particular accomplishment, it specifies its own procedure.

That doesn't imply the supreme consciousness has become dependent on the procedure.

### The grammar of the quotation

**tathā api** - nevertheless, even so.

Through sandhi: *tathāpi*.

**citra** - varied, differentiated, remarkable.

**karman** - action, activity, work.

**citrakarmārtham** - for the sake of varied activity or particular results.

The compound contains **artha**, purpose.

**upāyaḥ** - means, method, approach.

**vācyaḥ** - to be stated, to be explained, a gerundive from √*vac*, to speak.

**ādarāt** - from care, attentiveness, earnest consideration; ablative singular of *ādara*.

Thus the teacher should explain the means properly.

Not because the supreme reality needs constructing.

But because different forms of activity remain distinguishable within manifestation.

### The incredibly important final qualification

Abhinavagupta says that the person pursuing such specific attainments may be described, relative to the *jīvanmukta*, as possessing **mandaśaktipāta**-a weaker descent of grace.

He associates that with a condition that is not yet fully complete.

This is not a modern psychological diagnosis.

Nor is it a reliable way to classify strangers according to how many energetic sensations they report.

It is an internal theological distinction concerning the relation between liberation, grace, and practice.

The fully recognised supreme reality does not need an additional result.

A person still seeking particular conditioned attainments remains oriented toward something to be acquired.

That is the contrast.

It does not mean that every useful action indicates a deficient spiritual state.

Nor does it imply that a liberated person never undertakes practical projects.

The distinction concerns ultimate dependence upon the desired result.

And that is why Somānanda's verse is so relevant.

The highest knowledge doesn't abolish differentiated action.

It places differentiated action within the freedom of consciousness.

Now Abhinavagupta introduces the remaining yoga instructions.

And the textual transition is explicit.

### The authentic transition

**तं योगमार्गं निरूपयितुं ग्रन्थशेषोऽवतरति**

*taṃ yogamārgaṃ nirūpayituṃ granthaśeṣo 'vatarati.*

“To explain that path of yoga, the remaining portion of the text is introduced.”

This sentence matters enormously.

The remainder of the root tantra is not simply more of the same exposition.

Abhinavagupta has distinguished the supreme recognition from the practical yoga undertaken for particular results.

He now interprets the closing verses in that light.

And they are extraordinary.

A seed without beginning or end.

A blossoming lunar centre.

The Heart-lotus.

The sixteen lunar portions.

The seventeenth *kalā* beyond them.

The breath.

The supreme visarga.

And several distinct explanations transmitted through different teachers.

The commentary is about to become an esoteric astronomical, phonological, and subtle-body nightmare.

Glorious.


## XIV. The Heart-Lotus: Meditate on the Beginningless Seed in the Centre of the Lunar Phases

### KSTS verse 33 - Gnoli pp. 281-282

The root tantra now says:

**आद्यन्तरहितं बीजं विकसत्तिथिमध्यगम् ।**

*ādyantarahitaṃ bījaṃ vikasat tithimadhyagam.*

The first half describes a seed without beginning or end, blossoming in the middle of the *tithis*.

The following half instructs the practitioner to contemplate it in the **hṛtpadma**, the Heart-lotus, as a **somāṃśa**, a lunar portion, and to cultivate that contemplation continually.

In the independent root text, this material follows the opening half-verse of the preceding section as part of its differently numbered verses 33-34. In KSTS it is the four-pāda verse **33**. ; ; \]

And now we have to slow down.

Because nearly every word can be understood on several levels.

Ordinary Sanskrit.

Mantra phonology.

Tantric cosmology.

Subtle-body symbolism.

And the metaphysics of the supreme Heart.

If we collapse them, the commentary becomes incoherent.

### Ādyantarahita - आद्यन्तरहित

**ādi** - beginning.

**anta** - end.

**rahita** - without, deprived of, free from.

The compound means “without beginning and end.”

But Abhinavagupta gives more than one interpretation.

At the supreme level, the Heart-seed is beginningless and endless because its reality is not produced by an external cause or destroyed by a subsequent event.

He gives the reasons:

**gamāgamaśūnyatva** - the absence of coming and going.

**satatoditatva** - the condition of being ever manifest.

The Heart does not arrive from somewhere else.

It does not travel away when the practitioner stops contemplating it.

It is the ground of experience itself.

And that's the philosophical meaning of beginninglessness.

### The problem of the beginningless sound

An ordinary mantra has a beginning and an end.

You pronounce it.

The sound starts.

It continues.

It stops.

If we identify the ultimate Heart with the physical acoustic event, then it would obviously be temporally limited.

But Abhinavagupta distinguishes the audible phoneme from its supreme expressive ground.

The physical utterance is a manifestation.

Its ultimate nature is not exhausted by the temporal limits of the sound-wave.

Thus the Heart-seed can be described as without beginning and end in the higher sense.

Its acoustic form may begin and cease.

The consciousness through which it appears is not thereby made into a second acoustic object.

### Vikasat - विकसत्

Blossoming, expanding, opening.

A present participial form from *vi-* \+ √*kas*.

The word is linked with the Heart's manifestation of fullness.

Recall *vikāsa*.

Expansion.

And *saṃkoca*.

Contraction.

The whole eight-verse meditation prepared us for this.

The Heart's dynamic self-manifestation is contemplated through opening and withdrawal.

But the Heart is not merely the opening phase.

It is the ground of both phases.

### Tithi - तिथि

A lunar day, conventionally determined through the angular relationship between the Sun and Moon.

In tantric phonemic symbolism, *tithi* also refers to the vowel-powers associated with lunar manifestation.

The relevant system includes sixteen vocalic or lunar powers.

We've encountered the initial sixteenfold vowel sequence in our Mātṛkā studies.

The traditional phonemic order includes:

Sixteenfold traditional vowel series

The final two signs are included in the traditional sixteenfold Mātṛkā enumeration; they are not independent vowels in the same phonetic sense as a and ā.

The metaphysical interpretation does not require these categories to correspond one-to-one with modern phonetic vowel analysis.

They belong to a tantric scheme of sound-powers.

And the text's mention of *tithi* invokes that scheme.

### Madhyaga - मध्यग

**madhya** - middle, centre.

**ga** - going, existing, situated, from √*gam*.

**tithimadhyaga** - situated in the centre of the lunar portions or tithis.

But here *madhya* is not merely a coordinate.

The supreme centre is the ground of the differentiated powers.

The Heart does not need to be somewhere halfway between two physical objects.

It is the central principle through which the powers become manifest.

### Hṛtpadma - हृत्पद्म

**hṛd** - heart.

**padma** - lotus.

The compound means Heart-lotus.

The imagery is familiar in yogic and tantric literature.

But its philosophical role here is specific.

The lotus possesses a centre and petals.

It can be contemplated as opening and closing.

That makes it an appropriate symbol for the relation between the undivided Heart and differentiated manifestation.

The commentary explicitly connects the lotus with the properties of contraction and expansion.

The image is useful.

But the supreme Heart is not reducible to a physical lotus-shaped structure inside the chest.

### Dhyāyet - ध्यायेत्

One should meditate or contemplate.

Third-person singular optative from √*dhyai*.

Its prescriptive force is unmistakable.

We have now moved from the explanation of immediate recognition into a specific contemplative procedure.

And that distinction is essential.

The text is not saying that someone must perform this visualisation in order for supreme consciousness to exist.

It is offering a yogic method for engaging the Heart-seed.

### Somāṃśa - सोमांश

Now the lunar mystery.

**soma** - Moon, lunar power, or the sacred soma in other contexts.

**aṃśa** - portion, part, share, ray.

The compound means a portion of Soma or the Moon.

But within the tantric interpretation it concerns the lunar *kalās*, the powers of fullness and manifestation.

The Moon has phases.

It waxes.

It wanes.

It becomes apparently full.

It disappears.

The symbolism is suited to the dynamic relation between manifestation and withdrawal.

And here the root text instructs the practitioner to contemplate a particular lunar aspect within the Heart-lotus.

Now the commentary becomes more complicated.

Because different teachers explain the same verse differently.

And those explanations are not interchangeable.


## XV. Three Interpretations of the Lunar Seed: Dhaneśvaraśarman, Abhinavagupta's Teachers, and the Other Yogins

### Gnoli pp. 281-283

The commentary now preserves a remarkable little history of interpretation.

It first supplies a detailed explanation attributed to:

**भट्टधनेश्वरशर्मा - Bhaṭṭa Dhaneśvaraśarman.**

Then another introduced by:

**इत्यस्मद्गुरवः - ity asmadguravaḥ.**

“Thus our teachers.”

And then a third introduced by:

**अन्ये तु - anye tu.**

“But others…”

This is a gift to the textual historian.

We are not looking at a single monolithic instruction.

We are watching Abhinavagupta transmit different exegetical approaches to an extraordinarily compressed tantric verse.

The same Sanskrit supports multiple contemplative interpretations because it functions as a **sūtra-like** text.

But that flexibility is not unlimited permission to invent whatever one prefers.

Each interpretation must be grounded in its linguistic, cosmological, and doctrinal context.

### Interpretation A: Bhaṭṭa Dhaneśvaraśarman

The first explanation treats the lunar portion through the imagery of the full Moon, the Heart-lotus, the *dvādaśānta*, subtle sound, nectar, and the movement of solar and lunar powers.

The commentary associates this interpretation with a sequence including the **kākacañcupuṭamudrā**, the crow-beak gesture, and bodily signs such as trembling, gooseflesh, tears, and stillness.

These are described as phenomena arising in the relevant yogic context.

But the passage does not establish them as reliable medical or scientific indicators of liberation.

Nor should someone forcibly induce unusual breathing, prolonged retention, or bodily distress merely because the text describes a traditional practice.

The interpretive issue is the relation between sound, breath, lunar symbolism, and the supreme Heart.

#### Dvādaśānta

**द्वादशान्त - dvādaśānta**

**dvādaśa** - twelve.

**anta** - end or limit.

The term designates a subtle-body locus associated with the extension of vital activity or awareness, but its precise meaning and location differ among tantric contexts.

It is not simply a universally agreed anatomical organ.

In this passage, it appears within an explanation of the relation between the Heart, sound, and the lunar portion.

#### Nāda

**नाद - nāda**

Sound, resonance, subtle vibration.

Related to √*nad*, to sound.

Within tantric practice, it may denote levels of subtle sound rather than ordinary external acoustic events.

The distinction matters.

The physiology of hearing a sound is one subject.

The contemplative interpretation of *nāda* as a manifestation of consciousness is another.

#### The textual variant: soma or lotus filament?

The KSTS digital transcription reads a form corresponding to **somarūpam**, “having the form of Soma.”

The parallel GRETIL transcription instead reads **kesararūpam**, “having the form of a filament or stamen.”

The difference is not insignificant: one foregrounds lunar identity, the other lotus imagery. The surrounding prose contains both lunar and lotus symbolism, so neither can be confidently dismissed solely on contextual grounds. The original printed page and manuscript evidence would need further collation. ; \]

The point is not merely typographical.

The interpretation of the contemplative object changes.

And the fact that the commentary contains several exegetical traditions makes it especially important not to silently standardise the reading.

### Interpretation B: “Thus our teachers”

Now comes the most extraordinary phonemic analysis.

Abhinavagupta's teachers interpret the beginningless and endless seed through the structure of the Heart-mantra.

They discuss the removal or analytic separation of particular phonemic components, including **sakāra** and **aukāra**.

The resulting emphasis falls upon **visarga**.

This is the supreme emitting power we have examined throughout the audiobook.

The Heart-mantra is **सौः - sauḥ**.

Its constituent analysis includes *sa*, *au*, and *visarga*.

The interpretation does not treat the written sequence as a mere physical collection of sounds.

It uses the components to articulate the relation between conscious fullness, manifested powers, and emission.

#### The visarga as the dhruva

**ध्रुव - dhruva**

Fixed, stable, enduring, constant.

The adjective can designate the stable or immutable principle.

Here the visarga is associated with the enduring reality beyond the differentiated lunar phases.

This is not the claim that the two graphic dots of the Devanāgarī visarga are literally a physical celestial object.

The written sign represents a phonemic principle given a theological interpretation.

#### The sixteen lunar portions

The teachers interpret the lunar fullness through sixteen *kalās*.

**kalā** means a portion, part, digit, art, or power.

In lunar symbolism, it can denote a phase or portion of the Moon.

In tantric metaphysics, it can denote a differentiated power of consciousness.

And the sixteenfoldness has already been connected with the vowel-powers.

Thus the same contemplative object can be understood through phonology, lunar symbolism, and the metaphysics of manifestation.

But then the explanation goes beyond sixteen.

### The seventeenth kalā

The commentary uses the expression:

**सप्तदशी कला - saptadaśī kalā.**

“The seventeenth portion.”

Now this is fascinating.

Because the ordinary sixteenfold cycle is being transcended.

The sixteenth can represent fullness or completion within the relevant lunar structure.

The seventeenth signifies a principle beyond the differentiated sequence.

Not simply another ordinary day of a lunar month.

Nor a newly discovered astronomical Moon phase.

It is the symbolic transcendence of the cycle of manifestation and withdrawal.

The supreme Heart is not exhausted by the sixteenfold sequence.

It is the ground through which the sequence occurs.

This is the philosophical point.

And the phonemic interpretation relates that transcendence to the supreme visarga.

### The Moon is not merely a ball of rock

We should be precise here.

Abhinavagupta is not offering a modern astronomical model of the Moon.

He is interpreting a traditional symbolic system.

The Moon's waxing and waning represent the differentiated unfolding of powers.

The solar and lunar relation represents opposed or complementary modes of manifestation.

The visarga signifies emission.

The Heart is the undivided ground.

The symbolic system can be internally coherent without its correspondences constituting experimental evidence for extraordinary physiological phenomena.

### Why the teachers' interpretation matters

It reveals how the highest meaning of the mantra can be contemplated through its components.

The practitioner begins with differentiated phonemes and symbols.

The analysis proceeds toward their common ground.

The sixteen lunar portions are considered.

Then the principle beyond them.

The result is not merely a more complicated visualisation.

It is an attempt to recognise the ground of the entire sequence.

And Abhinavagupta says the repeated contemplation is the true **Heart-japa**.

Not merely mechanical pronunciation.

But the repeated turning of awareness toward its own nature.

### Interpretation C: “But others…”

The third explanation uses the movement of vital power between the Heart and *dvādaśānta*, solar and lunar phases, and a scheme of sixteen temporal portions.

It discusses the relation of the lunar fullness to a cycle of measured movement, contemplation, and the eventual transcending of the differentiated phases.

Here the text contains a useful numerical discrepancy.

The GRETIL transcription describes a movement of **thirty-six aṅgulas**.

The KSTS electronic transcription initially gives **twenty-six aṅgulas**, while later describing the complete cycle as thirty-six.

That internal mismatch makes an electronic or printed error plausible, but the exact origin cannot be established without direct inspection of the printed and manuscript witnesses.

It would be careless to announce that the correct original must be whichever number best fits a later yogic practice. ; \]

#### Aṅgula - अङ्गुल

A finger-breadth, a traditional measure of length.

Such measurements are used in various yogic accounts of the movement or extension of breath.

Their practical interpretation varies.

They should not be treated as universal physiological constants.

#### Tuṭi - तुटि

A small unit or division of time.

The text's explanation employs this measure in its account of sixteenfold temporal progression.

Again, the scheme belongs to a particular yogic model.

It is not an independently established measurement of how consciousness travels through the body.

### What do the three interpretations have in common?

They all attempt to explain the same compressed root instruction through different combinations of sound, lunar power, the Heart, and contemplative movement.

But they do not agree in every detail.

Dhaneśvaraśarman foregrounds one mode of subtle sound and lunar practice.

Abhinavagupta's teachers emphasise the phonemic extraction and transcendence of the sixteenfold cycle.

The other interpretation develops a measured account of the movement between Heart and *dvādaśānta*.

The differences are real.

And Abhinavagupta explicitly allows such interpretive plurality because the root verses function as compressed statements with many possible doctrinal applications.

This is a crucial point about Sanskrit tantric exegesis.

**A short verse can function as a sūtra-a condensed statement inviting systematic expansion.**

But the expansions belong to named interpretive traditions.

They must not be flattened into one fictitious single instruction.


## XVI. The True Japa: Repeating Consciousness's Recognition, Not Merely Repeating a Sound

After presenting the interpretations, Abhinavagupta returns to the core philosophical point.

The repeated contemplation is described through the continual turning of one's own nature, associated with the cycles of manifestation and withdrawal.

And then comes the decisive expression:

**सततोदितो हृदयजपः**

*satatodito hṛdayajapaḥ.*

“The ever-arisen japa of the Heart.”

Now that is extraordinary.

Because the ordinary notion of *japa* involves repetition.

You pronounce a mantra.

Then repeat it.

Then repeat it again.

Perhaps silently.

Perhaps mentally.

Perhaps with a mālā.

But the supreme Heart is not an acoustic event that must be manufactured anew with each repetition.

It is the conscious ground in which each repetition occurs.

So what can repetition mean at the highest level?

**The repeated recognition of what never ceased to be present.**

### Japa - जप

From √*jap*, to mutter, whisper, recite softly, repeat.

The noun can designate vocal, whispered, or mental mantra repetition, depending on the tradition.

But Abhinavagupta's higher interpretation concerns the continuity of recognition.

The sound may recur.

The conscious reality does not need to be recreated.

### The cosmic rosary

The commentary uses an image of the recurring cycle of creation and dissolution as a kind of **cakrākṣamālikā**, a circular rosary.

Ordinary beads succeed one another.

The practitioner counts repetitions.

But here the entire cycle of manifestation and reabsorption becomes the repetition.

The world appears.

It withdraws.

A thought emerges.

It ceases.

A sensation appears.

It changes.

A memory arises.

It disappears.

And through all these differentiations, the manifestness of consciousness remains the ground.

The cycle itself becomes a contemplative expression of the Heart.

### Is the point to think “I am Śiva” endlessly?

No.

A sentence can be useful.

But a sentence is still a differentiated linguistic event.

A person could repeat “I am Śiva” for hours while remaining completely identified with the little empirical ego.

The actual recognition concerns the reality of the subject.

The proposition can direct attention.

It cannot manufacture the universal consciousness it describes.

This is why Abhinavagupta distinguishes *mantravīrya*, the realised potency of the mantra, from mere phonemic possession.

A sound may be repeated.

Its meaning may be understood.

But the supreme point is the recognition of the conscious power through which the sound, the meaning, and the subject are manifest.

That is the real Heart-japa.

And the text's insistence on **satatodita** is the clue.

Ever-arisen.

Not occasionally manufactured.

Not spatially transported.

Not acquired from somewhere else.

The Heart has never ceased to be the ground of the experience.

### The grammatical note on abhyasyet

The root verse ends with **abhyasyet**, “one should practise repeatedly.”

This is an optative form from *abhi-* \+ √*as*, to repeat or practise, in the relevant verb formation.

Abhinavagupta explicitly notes the optative's force and connects the process with contemplative cultivation.

The grammar is important.

A prescription is being given.

But what is prescribed is the repeated contemplative engagement with the Heart, not the creation of the Heart itself.

Again the two levels remain distinct.

The ultimate reality is unproduced.

The practice is a conditioned activity intended to disclose it or cultivate the relevant capacity.

And now the final two verses of the tantra make the expected promise.

Desired results.

Accomplishment.

Omniscience.

And, as always, the claim becomes much more interesting when we examine exactly what the Sanskrit says.


## XVII. The Final Promise: Whatever You Desire, You Obtain. Surely This Needs a Qualification.

### KSTS verse 34 - Gnoli p. 283

The root tantra begins:

**यान्यान्कामयते कामांस्तांस्ताञ्छीघ्रमवाप्नुयात्**

*yān yān kāmayate kāmāṃs tān tāñ chīghram avāpnuyāt.*

“Whatever objects of desire he desires, those very things he may quickly attain.”

Then the verse makes its extraordinary declaration concerning **sarvajñatva**, omniscience.

Now let's examine what is actually being claimed.

The text does assert an unusual capacity.

The practitioner who repeatedly engages the Heart-seed is said to obtain desired results.

The commentary relates this to the expansion of intention grounded in the potency of the all-encompassing Heart.

But the passage does not independently demonstrate that an ordinary person can make any desired external event occur merely by thinking about it.

It is a traditional tantric assertion concerning *siddhi*.

We must analyse it as such.

### The beautiful relative-correlative structure

The Sanskrit construction is:

**yān yān … tān tān.**

Whatever things … those very things.

This is a repeated relative-correlative construction.

The repetition intensifies the distributive sense.

Whatever individual objects fall within the scope of desire are matched with corresponding objects of attainment.

The same construction is extremely common in Sanskrit.

It is not a special mantric grammar.

### Kāmayate - कामयते

A denominative verb associated with *kāma*, desire.

Third-person singular present middle.

“He desires.”

**kāmān** is accusative plural masculine, objects of desire.

Through external sandhi before the following dental or palatal sounds, its ending changes in the continuous verse.

The phonological details matter.

**kāmāṃs + tān** preserves the nasal-sibilant structure in the sandhi environment.

**tān tān + śīghram** produces the palatalised sequence represented in the transmitted text.

The written form is a continuous Sanskrit utterance, not an arbitrary concatenation of dictionary forms.

### Śīghram - शीघ्रम्

Quickly, rapidly.

An adverbial accusative.

The long ī matters.

The aspirated **gh** matters.

**घ - gha** is not the same consonant as **ग - ga**.

### Avāpnuyāt - अवाप्नुयात्

Third-person singular optative of *ava-* \+ √*āp*, to reach or obtain.

“May obtain,” “would obtain,” or, in a prescriptive/resultative context, “shall obtain.”

The optative is significant.

The verse is describing a traditionally expected result of the practice.

It is not a report of a controlled experiment in which every desire has been verified.

### The textual difficulty in the next line

The KSTS commentary reads:

**अस्मात्प्रत्यक्षतामेति**

*asmāt pratyakṣatām eti.*

“From this, $$something$$ becomes directly manifest.”

The independent GRETIL root transcription instead has:

**अज्ञः प्रत्यक्षतामेति**

*ajñaḥ pratyakṣatām eti.*

“The ignorant one comes into direct manifestation or apprehension…”

The second reading creates interpretive difficulties in the relation between the subject *ajñaḥ*, the abstract accusative *pratyakṣatām*, and the following *sarvajñatvam*.

The KSTS reading more readily allows the interpretation that omniscience becomes directly manifest through this practice, but it too is a compressed construction.

The SanskritDocuments collation explicitly records both forms. Neither should be suppressed simply because one yields a more convenient translation. ; ; \]

This is a genuine example of a potentially consequential textual difference.

It alters the grammatical subject and the way the promised result is expressed.

And the metre remains compatible with both readings.

Therefore metre cannot settle the issue.

### Sarvajñatva - सर्वज्ञत्व

**sarva** - all.

**jña** - knowing.

**-tva** - abstract state or condition.

Thus:

“the condition of being all-knowing.”

And this is the extraordinary promise at the conclusion of the tantra.

But what does Abhinavagupta mean by it?

His prose gives the crucial qualification.

He associates the supreme omniscience with **parabhairavātmakatva**.

Having the nature of supreme Bhairava.

And says this occurs **anenaiva dehena**-with this very body.

That is the astonishing part.

Not merely after death.

Not merely after travelling to another realm.

In the present embodied condition.

### Is omniscience the ability to answer every factual question?

A literal reading of *sarvajñatva* can certainly suggest universal knowledge.

And tantric literature sometimes attributes precisely such extraordinary capacities to accomplished practitioners.

But Abhinavagupta's commentary also interprets omniscience through identity with the supreme Lord.

These are related yet distinguishable claims.

The metaphysical assertion is that universal consciousness is the ground of all manifest knowledge.

The empirical assertion that a particular individual can accurately retrieve every possible fact would require independent evidence.

The first does not logically establish the second without additional argument.

We therefore shouldn't simply announce that anyone who has achieved recognition can answer every mathematical, historical, medical, or scientific question.

That would turn a sophisticated metaphysics into an extremely dubious examination technique.

Imagine a practitioner declaring themselves omniscient.

You ask them a difficult Sanskrit grammatical question.

They answer incorrectly.

“Well,” they explain, “the supreme consciousness transcends grammar.”

Magnificent.

The universe is one, but your accusative plural is still wrong.

### What happens to desire?

This is another subtle problem.

If the practitioner has recognised the fullness of consciousness, why would they still pursue particular desires?

The text distinguishes between the attainment of particular results and the ultimate resting of consciousness in its own nature.

A person may still engage in activity.

Desires may still arise as differentiated events.

But their occurrence need not imply that the subject fundamentally lacks its own nature.

The liberated person is not necessarily someone in whom every possible preference or motivation has disappeared.

The issue is identification and bondage.

A desire can arise.

An action can be undertaken.

But the ultimate ground of the subject is not dependent upon the result for its fullness.

This is how the text can include *siddhi* without making conditioned acquisition the definition of liberation.

And Abhinavagupta now moves to the final verse.

The concluding declaration of the *Rudrayāmala*.


## XVIII. The Final Root Verse: The Rudrayāmala Returns to the Silence from Which It Began

### KSTS verse 35 - Gnoli p. 283

The closing root verse reads:

**एवं मन्त्रफलावाप्तिरित्येतद्रुद्रयामलम् ।**

*evaṃ mantraphalāvāptir ity etad rudrayāmalam.*

**एतदभ्यासतः सिद्धिः सर्वज्ञत्वमवाप्यते ॥३५॥**

*etad abhyāsataḥ siddhiḥ sarvajñatvam avāpyate.*

My literal translation:

“Thus the attainment of the fruit of mantra is explained: this is the Rudrayāmala. Through practice of this, accomplishment and omniscience are attained.”

The independent root text places this conclusion at the end of its verse 36. SanskritDocuments records another verbal variant, *avāpnuyāt*, in place of *avāpyate*. The former is an optative, whereas the latter is present passive. The meaning of attainment remains similar, but the grammatical formulation differs. ; ; \]

And there.

After the entire magnificent philosophical detour through consciousness, Sanskrit phonology, erotic symbolism, the thirty-six tattvas, divine powers, initiation, ritual, meditation, and the Heart, the root tantra reaches its conclusion.

**This is the Rudrayāmala.**

The dialogue of Rudra and his Śakti.

And Abhinavagupta now explains what that means.

### Mantraphalāvāpti - मन्त्रफलावाप्ति

An exceptionally compact compound.

**mantra** - the sacred expression.

**phala** - fruit, result, consequence.

**avāpti** - attainment, acquisition, reaching.

From *ava-* \+ √*āp*, to reach or obtain.

Thus:

“Attainment of the fruit of mantra.”

The compound concerns the efficacy of the mantra in the framework of the tantra.

But Abhinavagupta has spent hundreds of pages demonstrating that the ultimate fruit is not merely an independently existing external object.

It is recognition of the supreme Heart.

A particular mantra can be associated with particular attainments.

The Heart-mantra is interpreted as the ground of the entire field of mantric powers.

And its highest fruit is the recognition of universal consciousness.

### Abhyāsataḥ - अभ्यासतः

From repeated practice, through cultivation.

The suffix **-tas** creates an adverbial or causal expression.

The word is derived from *abhyāsa*, repetition, sustained practice.

This is significant because the conclusion retains the practical perspective of the final yoga verses.

The root text has not merely ended with the statement that the supreme is already present.

It has also explained a form of cultivation through which the practitioner may seek specific results.

Abhinavagupta interprets that within his distinction between immediate recognition and practice-dependent accomplishment.

### Avāpyate - अवाप्यते

Third-person singular present passive of *ava-* \+ √*āp*, to reach or obtain.

“Is attained.”

This differs from *avāpnuyāt*, the optative of the active verbal formation, which would express “one may attain” or “one would attain.”

The distinction is small in the broad narrative but substantial for grammatical analysis.

The original wording should not be silently harmonised.

Now for the extraordinary final word of the verse.

**Rudrayāmala.**


## XIX. Rudrayāmala: The Entire Text Has Been Śiva and Śakti Talking to Themselves

### Abhinavagupta's closing prose

The commentator explains the name through:

**रुद्रस्य रुद्रायाश्च यद्यामलम्**

*rudrasya rudrāyāś ca yad yāmalam.*

“The conjunction of Rudra and Rudrā…”

And he develops that conjunction from the undivided question-and-answer relation at the beginning of the text through the immeasurably differentiated processes of creation and withdrawal.

This is one of the most beautiful returns to the opening dialogue.

Remember how the work began.

The Goddess asked Bhairava about the *anuttara*, the unsurpassable reality.

How can it be known?

How can it confer the supreme accomplishment?

What is the Heart?

What is the power through which all manifestation occurs?

Bhairava answered.

And then Abhinavagupta spent an extraordinary amount of intellectual energy explaining the answer.

But now he says that the very relationship between question and answer is itself a manifestation of the supreme consciousness.

The Goddess's question.

Bhairava's reply.

The differentiation between speaker and listener.

The emergence of language.

The process of understanding.

All belong to the one conscious reality.

### Yāmala - यामल

A pair, couple, union, or twofold conjunction.

In tantric religious contexts, it can designate the union of divine powers or a scriptural text associated with such a relationship.

Here Abhinavagupta interprets the *Rudrayāmala* through the undivided unity of Rudra and Rudrā.

This is not simply a title chosen for marketing purposes.

The name expresses the philosophical structure of the work.

The supreme consciousness manifests a polarity.

The polarity allows expression.

Expression permits question and answer.

The dialogue discloses the reality that grounds the dialogue itself.

An extraordinary recursive structure.

### Saṃghaṭṭa - संघट्ट

Conjunction, encounter, striking together.

We previously encountered the same term in the discussion of Śiva-Śakti and *visarga*.

Here it explains the union or meeting of Rudra and Rudrā.

But the conjunction does not require two ultimately independent substances.

Their twofoldness is a manifestation of the freedom of the one supreme consciousness.

### The undivided question and answer

Abhinavagupta describes the relation as beginning with a condition in which question and response are not ultimately separate.

Then differentiation unfolds.

The Goddess asks.

Bhairava replies.

The teaching is articulated.

Phonemes emerge.

Meaning becomes manifest.

The student understands.

And at the end the entire process is recognised as belonging to the same supreme consciousness.

The question is not ultimately foreign to the answer.

The teacher is not ultimately foreign to the student.

The speech is not ultimately foreign to its conscious ground.

And this is why the conclusion returns to **Akula**, the supreme reality beyond the differentiated Kula.

### Two conclusions in one

Abhinavagupta explicitly distinguishes the philosophical conclusion from the yogic conclusion.

The first concerns the **prasaṃkhyāna-nigamana**, the conclusion of reflective recognition.

The second concerns the **yogaphala-nigamana**, the conclusion describing the fruit of yogic practice.

That is enormously important.

It means the commentator himself preserves the distinction we've been tracing.

The highest recognition concerns the supreme reality.

The final yogic instructions concern particular means and their results.

They are related because both occur within the same conscious manifestation.

But they are not identical in function.

The supreme consciousness is not manufactured by practice.

Practice can nevertheless be prescribed within the differentiated order.

And then Abhinavagupta says the final words of the running commentary.

**सततोदितं ह्येतत्सर्वस्येति शिवम् ।**

*satatoditaṃ hy etat sarvasyeti śivam.*

“For this is ever manifest for everyone. Thus-auspiciousness.”

Or, with the full resonance of the final word:

**Śivam.**

The supreme reality is ever manifest.

For everyone.

Not because everyone necessarily recognises it.

But because its presence is not created by the event of recognition.

That is the culmination of the whole *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*.

And it is almost absurdly elegant.

We've spent hundreds of pages trying to reach the ultimate consciousness.

And the final statement is that the reality under discussion has never ceased to be present.

The text doesn't end by announcing the arrival of Śiva.

It ends by recognising that Śiva was never absent.


## XX. The Final Metrical Audit

The three concluding KSTS-numbered verses use the eight-syllable-pāda anuṣṭubh structure.

The first half-verse on remembrance is regular, as already established.

The subsequent complete verses scan as follows.

| Pāda | Syllabic division | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *ādyantarahitaṃ bījam* | ā-dyan-ta-ra-hi-taṃ-bī-jam | 8 |
| *vikasat tithimadhyagam* | vi-ka-sat-ti-thi-madh-ya-gam | 8 |
| *hṛtpadmāntargataṃ dhyāyet* | hṛt-pad-mān-tar-ga-taṃ-dhyā-yet | 8 |
| *somāṃśaṃ nityam abhyasyet* | so-māṃ-śaṃ-ni-tyam-a-bhyas-yet | 8 |

The alternative **ajñaḥ pratyakṣatām eti** likewise contains eight syllables, so it cannot be rejected metrically.

The variant **avāpnuyāt** in the concluding verse also preserves the eight-syllable pattern.

For the eight inserted Heart-verses, the transmitted four-pāda constructions are likewise anuṣṭubh in both the principal electronic witnesses, though several variant readings alter the wording.

Consider the particularly beautiful fourth verse:

*dhyāyan smaran pravimṛśan* - 8.

*kurvan vā yatra kutracit* - 8.

*viśrāntim eti yasmāc ca* - 8.

*prollased hṛdayaṃ tu tat* - 8.

The metre survives an almost astonishing amount of philosophical compression.

Meditating.

Remembering.

Reflecting.

Acting.

Repose.

The shining of the Heart.

All within thirty-two syllables.

The Sanskrit is doing extraordinarily efficient work.

### Why metrical accuracy matters

Because a reading can appear philosophically attractive while being textually unsupported.

A copied verse may contain an electronic error.

A conjunct may be misread.

A vowel length may be altered.

A quotation may have a variant that changes the grammar.

Metrical analysis can expose some of those problems.

But it cannot resolve every variant.

A different word can occupy exactly the same metrical position.

That is why we need the manuscript witnesses, the syntax, and the broader philosophical context.

The metre is evidence.

Not an oracle.


## XXI. The Next Authentic Transition: Abhinavagupta Stops Explaining the Tantra and Begins Speaking About Himself

And now we have reached the authentic transition that must not be confused with the conclusion of the root tantra.

After:

**… इति शिवम् - iti śivam.**

The running explanation is complete.

But the 1918 KSTS text continues with **ten concluding verses**, beginning with a verse in which Abhinavagupta speaks of composing the commentary after being born in Kashmir.

This is a different literary mode.

No longer the explanation of another root verse.

No longer the running exegesis of Bhairava's dialogue with the Goddess.

Now the commentator speaks in his own authorial voice.

The opening words are:

**इत्थं प्रपन्नजनतोद्धरणप्रवृत्त…**

*itthaṃ prapannajanatoddharaṇapravṛtta…*

The verse invokes the Lord's feet and describes the composition of the commentary containing the secret reflection of Trika.

Then the remaining concluding verses refer to the purpose of the work, the role of knowledge in removing doubt, and biographical details involving Kashmir, Abhinavagupta's family, and those for whom the exposition was undertaken.

This is important historical material.

It includes figures such as Vallabha, Śauri, Vatsalikā, Karṇa, Manorathagupta, and Rāmadeva.

These verses require their own source-critical treatment because the names, relationships, and biographical statements are easily distorted if we treat the entire closing section as simply another anonymous praise-hymn.

The next instalment will therefore examine the **ten authentic concluding verses**, their metres, their manuscript differences, and the historical information they reveal about Abhinavagupta himself.

That is the next genuine transition in the text. ; \]

And we stop here.

Because the root tantra has ended.

The doctrinal commentary has ended.

The final word has been spoken.

**Śivam.**


## Closing: The Entire Universe Has Been Reciting Its Own Name

Let's return to where we began.

A question.

The Goddess asks Bhairava:

What is the unsurpassable reality?

How can it confer immediate accomplishment?

What is the secret of the supreme Heart?

And Bhairava answers.

The alphabet unfolds.

The sounds differentiate.

The powers of consciousness appear.

The thirty-six tattvas manifest.

The limited subject arises.

The universe appears as something external.

The subject experiences desire, knowledge, activity, limitation, and bondage.

The practitioner searches for liberation.

They turn to scripture.

They seek a teacher.

They learn a mantra.

They perform worship.

They contemplate the Heart.

They examine the nature of the knowing subject.

And eventually they recognise that the very consciousness through which the entire search occurred was never separate from the supreme reality they were seeking.

That is the magnificent philosophical structure of the work.

And now the final verses have drawn the distinction between two modes of practice.

In the first, recognition is established.

The Heart is not something that must be retrieved from an absent location.

Ordinary activity can continue.

The subject acts.

Thinks.

Remembers.

Speaks.

Experiences the world.

But the ultimate nature of the experiencing field is no longer misrecognised as a separate collection of independent substances.

That is the meaning of *jīvanmukti* in this context.

In the second, the practitioner still engages in a sequential contemplative discipline.

They work with the Heart-lotus.

The mantra.

The lunar portions.

The powers of sound.

The movement of expansion and contraction.

The distinction between manifestation and reabsorption.

And through such practice they seek the realisation or accomplishments described by the tantra.

Abhinavagupta does not treat the two as producing different ultimate consciousnesses.

They differ in the relation of the practitioner to recognition and practice.

### The difference between remembering and acquiring

This is perhaps the most powerful idea in the entire ending.

Ordinary acquisition adds something to what you possess.

You acquire an object.

A skill.

A piece of information.

A new experience.

But recognition is different.

You recognise something that was already the case.

The event of recognition may occur at a particular time.

But the recognised reality is not thereby produced at that time.

And if the supreme consciousness is genuinely the ground of manifestation, its reality cannot be dependent on the finite subject's act of recognising it.

Thus the highest liberation is not the production of Śiva-consciousness.

It is the removal of the misrecognition of that consciousness.

The whole teaching turns on that distinction.

### The great practical reversal

Imagine you're sitting quietly.

A thought arises.

You notice it.

Another appears.

A sound outside.

A sensation in the body.

A memory.

An intention.

An impulse to move.

You can examine them as separate phenomena.

But the Pratyabhijñā enquiry asks a deeper question.

What makes them manifest?

What is the nature of the awareness through which their differences are known?

Does that awareness itself appear as another independent object?

Or is its self-manifestation already the condition of the entire field?

Abhinavagupta's answer is the supreme conscious Self.

The Heart of Bhairava.

And the decisive recognition is not simply:

“I am having an unusual experience.”

It is:

“The ultimate nature of the subject and its manifestation is this self-aware consciousness.”

That's a metaphysical claim, not a scientifically established conclusion.

But it is a sharply articulated philosophical position.

And the entire *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* has been devoted to explaining it.

### The final absurdity

Consider what we've done.

We've examined a Sanskrit alphabet in which individual phonemes can represent cosmological powers.

We've investigated how consciousness manifests the distinction between speaker and listener.

We've discussed the relation between words, meanings, and cognition.

We've explored the body as a configuration of divine powers.

We've studied extraordinary ritual substances.

We've examined the distinction between the physical letter and the ultimate phonemic power.

We've analysed the stages of speech.

We've investigated bliss, desire, expression, memory, and conceptual thought.

We've watched Abhinavagupta argue that even the most ordinary perceptual event is a manifestation of consciousness.

We've contemplated death, initiation, liberation, and the divine hierarchy.

We've put the whole universe inside a banyan seed.

We've offered the universe into a cosmic fire.

We've examined the Goddess as the power of manifestation.

We've counted twenty-seven repetitions.

We've counted sixteen lunar portions.

We've reached the seventeenth *kalā*.

We've encountered a female donkey and a mare.

And now the commentator tells us that the supreme Heart is **satatodita**.

Ever-arisen.

Always present.

It was there before the commentary began.

It was there during every Sanskrit explanation.

It was there while you tried to work out why a consonant cluster contains three letters but only one syllabic onset.

It was there when you were fascinated.

It was there when you were bored.

It was there when you were confused.

It was there when you understood something.

And, according to the metaphysics, it does not become absent simply because the ordinary mind becomes distracted.

The entire universe can unfold into limitless differentiation.

And the supreme reality is not diminished.

A thought can arise.

A sound can occur.

A body can move.

A person can speak.

An animal can call.

A star can shine.

A Sanskrit scholar can spend forty years arguing about a manuscript variant.

And none of these differentiated events has to be outside the ultimate consciousness.

The practical question is not how to manufacture a new awareness.

It is how to recognise the nature of the awareness in which everything is already manifest.

And so the final word of the running commentary is astonishingly appropriate.

Not a lengthy new doctrinal formula.

Not another cosmological calculation.

Not another mantra requiring seventeen footnotes.

Just:

**शिवम् - śivam.**

Auspiciousness.

Śiva.

The reality that has been present throughout the entire enquiry.

The divine has not arrived.

The universe has not suddenly become conscious.

The supreme Heart has not just been installed.

Rather, the commentary has been a vast effort to make intelligible what, in Abhinavagupta's view, was never absent.

And the final joke is almost unbearable.

The supreme consciousness has manifested an entire universe.

Within that universe it has manifested Sanskrit.

Within Sanskrit it has manifested a philosophy of consciousness.

Within that philosophy it has manifested the idea that consciousness is the ground of the universe.

And now some poor bastard has spent thirty-four audiobook instalments trying to understand why the universe needed to explain itself to itself.

Abhinavagupta's answer?

Because manifestation is the freedom of consciousness.

And the recognition of that freedom is its own delight.

**The universe was not waiting to become Śiva. It was waiting, as it were, to recognise what it had always been.**
