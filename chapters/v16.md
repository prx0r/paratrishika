# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XVI - The Divine Triangle and the Flower of Śakti

*Abhinavagupta takes Śiva's primordial seed, transforms it into the generative flower of the Goddess, discovers that every act of knowing contains a triangle, summons the Moon, Sun and Fire, introduces the three subtle channels, and explains why the entire universe is essentially the self-expression of divine consciousness.*

Commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series, pp. 183-186


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

We've made it through fifteen installments.

We've learned how the vowels express the powers of consciousness, how those powers condense into consonants, and how the consonantal matrix corresponds to the thirty-six *tattvas*.

The whole Sanskrit alphabet has become a theology.

Not because Sanskrit phonetics somehow proves that the universe was manufactured by a supernatural spelling bee.

But because Abhinavagupta interprets the structured relations of speech through the nondual metaphysics of Śiva and Śakti.

And then, at the end of Part XV, we reached his calculation:

Five principal phonemic groupings, expanded through six divine powers, together with the six higher principles, yield thirty-six.

The differentiated universe.

Earth to Śakti.

Consonant to consonant.

An entire cosmos organized through sacred sound.

Now an obvious question arises.

**How does the undivided conscious power become a differentiated universe without turning into an independent second substance?**

Abhinavagupta has answered this repeatedly through *svātantrya*, sovereign freedom.

But now he gives the answer a form.

A seed.

A flower.

A generative matrix.

And then something extraordinary happens.

The flower becomes a triangle.

Its three corners are:

**ग्राह्य - grāhya:** what is apprehended.

**ग्रहण - grahaṇa:** apprehension.

**ग्राहक - grāhaka:** the apprehender.

Think about that.

Every time you know anything, you can distinguish an object, a knowing, and a knower.

And Abhinavagupta is about to show why that threefold structure becomes a sacred symbol of the Goddess herself.

Then come several more triads: Moon, Sun and Fire; creation, maintenance and withdrawal; the three subtle channels; and the relation between *dharma*, *adharma* and their mixture.

The crucial point is that these aren't isolated decorative correspondences.

They belong to his attempt to explain **how one conscious reality manifests as differentiated experience**.

One textual caution before we start: the Kashmir Series transcription contains obvious spelling or copying problems around *prasūtipada*, *pūrvokta*, and one word in the *Trikatantrasāra* quotation. I'll give the readings supported by the parallel Sanskrit transcription and identify substantial uncertainties instead of silently manufacturing a flawless critical edition. ; \]

Right.

The Goddess is flowering.

And the Sanskrit is about to become spectacularly Tantric.


## 1. तदेवं शिवबीजमेव - Thus the very seed of Śiva

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · KASHMIR SERIES P. 183

**तदेवं शिवबीजमेव स्वातन्त्र्यात् घनीभूततया क्वचिद्वपुषि शाक्तरूपे कुसुमतया तिष्ठत् योनिरित्यभिधीयते।**

*Tad evaṃ śivabījam eva svātantryād ghanībhūtatayā kvacid vapuṣi śāktarūpe kusumatayā tiṣṭhad yonir ity abhidhīyate.*

A close rendering:

Thus the very seed of Śiva, through sovereign freedom, when it takes a condensed form within a particular Śākta manifestation and exists as *kusuma*, is called the *yoni*, the generative matrix.

That is the actual beginning of our new passage.

And it is an extraordinary transition.

We've been dealing with phonemes, philosophical categories and powers of consciousness.

Now Abhinavagupta suddenly introduces a seed and a generative flower.

But he's not abandoning the argument.

He's giving its central relationship a more concrete symbolic expression.

### Śivabījam - शिवबीजम्

**शिव - śiva**

Śiva.

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

Thus:

**शिवबीज - śivabīja**

Śiva's seed.

Neuter nominative singular:

**शिवबीजम् - śivabījam**.

Now, *bīja* has several relevant meanings in Tantric Sanskrit.

It can mean an ordinary seed.

A generative potency.

A seed-syllable in mantra.

Or, in explicitly sexual and reproductive symbolism, semen.

And here we cannot politely pretend the last meaning is irrelevant.

The passage deliberately employs the union of **seed** and **generative female substance** as an image of Śiva-Śakti manifestation.

The metaphor is simultaneously reproductive, ritual, phonemic and metaphysical.

Don't flatten it into botany.

But don't reduce it to physiology either.

The theological claim is that the divine power of generation is intrinsic to consciousness.

### Svātantryāt - स्वातन्त्र्यात्

From or because of sovereign freedom.

We've learned:

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Independence, autonomous freedom.

The ablative singular:

**स्वातन्त्र्यात् - svātantryāt**.

Now the ablative is doing genuine philosophical work.

It gives the ground or reason.

Why can the seed take this differentiated form?

**Because of svātantrya.**

Not because Śiva was overpowered by an independent external material.

Not because an alien substance invaded consciousness.

The manifestation belongs to his own freedom.

### Ghanībhūtatayā - घनीभूततया

Through becoming condensed.

Let's dismantle it.

**घन - ghana**

Dense, compact, concentrated.

**घनीभूत - ghanībhūta**

Having become dense or compact.

**घनीभूतता - ghanībhūtatā**

The condition of having become condensed.

And the feminine instrumental singular:

**घनीभूततया - ghanībhūtatayā**

Through or in the condition of condensation.

Now this continues the exact terminology of Part XV.

The vowels became consonantal matrices through *ghanatāpatti*, the acquisition of density.

And now Śiva's seed becomes the generative form of Śakti through *ghanībhūtatā*.

The image is continuous.

**The subtle power becomes a more explicitly differentiated manifestation without ceasing to belong to its conscious ground.**

### Kvacid vapuṣi - क्वचिद्वपुषि

**क्वचित् - kvacit**

Somewhere, in some particular instance.

**वपुस् - vapus**

Form, body, embodiment.

Locative singular:

**वपुषि - vapuṣi**.

So:

*kvacid vapuṣi*

In a particular form or manifestation.

Notice the **ष - ṣ** in *vapuṣi*.

That's part of the actual inflection.

### Śāktarūpe - शाक्तरूपे

**शाक्त - śākta**

Pertaining to Śakti.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form.

Locative singular:

**शाक्तरूपे - śāktarūpe**.

In a Śākta form.

The seed of Śiva manifests within the form of Śakti.

Again, don't imagine two ultimate substances travelling toward each other through physical space.

The distinction is being articulated within one conscious reality.

### Kusumatayā - कुसुमतया

Now here's the dangerous word.

**कुसुम - kusuma**

Flower.

But in this particular Tantric context, it also denotes the female generative or menstrual substance used in Kaula symbolism.

The lexical and contextual meanings are both important.

The floral imagery of blossoming and generation is intentional.

The reproductive meaning is also intentional.

The abstract:

**कुसुमता - kusumatā**

The condition of being *kusuma*.

Instrumental:

**कुसुमतया - kusumatayā**

In the form or condition of *kusuma*.

### Yoni - योनि

Womb, source, generative matrix.

And now the principal statement:

**योनिरित्यभिधीयते।**

*Yonir ity abhidhīyate.*

It is called *yoni*.

Break it down:

**योनिः - yoniḥ**

Womb, source.

**इति - iti**

Thus, so, by the name.

**अभिधीयते - abhidhīyate**

Is named, designated, called.

A passive form related to *abhi-√dhā*, to designate or name.

Now that's the first major result.

The Śiva-seed, in a condensed Śākta manifestation, is designated the generative matrix.

But why?

What exactly makes this a *yoni*?

Abhinavagupta immediately answers.

**Because it possesses a three-cornered structure involving the object, knowing, and knower.**

And now the triangle arrives.


## 2. The first triangle - Grāhya, Grahaṇa, Grāhaka

Here is the crucial Sanskrit compound.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 183

**ग्राह्यग्रहणग्राहककोणत्रयमयम्।**

*Grāhyagrahaṇagrāhakakoṇatrayamayam.*

Consisting of the three corners constituted by what is apprehended, apprehension, and the apprehender.

Now this is one of the most important compounds in the entire *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*.

Because it joins the theology of Śakti to the philosophical analysis of cognition.

Let's dismantle every part.

### Grāhya - ग्राह्य

What is to be grasped or apprehended.

From:

**√ग्रह् - √grah**

To grasp, seize, apprehend.

The **-ya** formation gives the sense of what can or should undergo the action.

Thus:

**ग्राह्य - grāhya**

The apprehensible object.

In epistemological terminology, it can refer to the known content.

Now imagine a pot.

The pot is the *grāhya*.

### Grahaṇa - ग्रहण

Grasping, apprehension, the act or means of apprehending.

From the same root:

**√ग्रह् - √grah**.

The noun:

**ग्रहण - grahaṇa**

Apprehension.

Now here's the important distinction.

*Grāhya* and *grahaṇa* are not synonyms.

One concerns the apprehended.

The other the apprehending activity.

### Grāhaka - ग्राहक

The apprehender, grasper, receiver.

Again from √*grah*, with an agent-forming suffix.

**ग्राहक - grāhaka**

The one who apprehends.

Now we've got the complete triad.

| Sanskrit | Grammatical function | Philosophical role |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **ग्राह्य - grāhya** | What is apprehended | Object |
| **ग्रहण - grahaṇa** | Apprehending | Process or means |
| **ग्राहक - grāhaka** | The apprehender | Subject |

Notice something beautiful.

All three words come from the same verbal root.

The *grasping* relationship is expressed through different grammatical formations.

And those formations distinguish three functional roles within one intelligible act.

This is not an accidental feature of the Sanskrit.

It's precisely why the vocabulary is so useful for the philosophical analysis.

### Koṇatraya - कोणत्रय

**कोण - koṇa**

Corner, angle.

**त्रय - traya**

A group of three.

So:

**कोणत्रय - koṇatraya**

Three corners.

And:

### -Maya - मय

Consisting of, constituted by.

Thus:

**ग्राह्यग्रहणग्राहककोणत्रयमय**

Consisting of the triangle of object, apprehension and apprehender.

Now we understand the symbolism.

The generative matrix has three corners.

Those corners correspond to the fundamental threefold structure of differentiated cognition.

### The pot returns one more time

Imagine looking at a pot.

There's the pot.

There's the act of seeing or knowing it.

And there's the one who sees.

Ordinarily we distinguish these three.

They seem obvious.

But Abhinavagupta asks what makes the entire event intelligible.

If the object existed as a completely independent ultimate substance, how would it enter the field of awareness?

If knowing were entirely separate from the knower, how would the knower possess it?

And if the knower were wholly disconnected from the known, how would apprehension occur?

The three are distinguishable.

Yet their intelligible manifestation belongs to one consciousness.

**That is why the triangle is sacred here.**

It expresses the threefold articulation of experience within a nondual ground.

### The Goddess as the triangle

Now here's the deeper significance.

Śakti isn't simply the object being known.

Nor merely the process of knowing.

Nor only the subject who knows.

Her expressive power encompasses all three.

The differentiated cognitive relation is one mode of her manifestation.

And the three corners of the triangle make that structure visible.

That's the theological move.

The Goddess is the generative condition through which the relational universe appears.

Now this doesn't imply that an ordinary geometric triangle physically creates consciousness.

The triangle is a symbolic arrangement.

But it's not meaningless decoration.

It represents a very specific philosophical structure.

**One conscious freedom manifesting through the differentiated relation of subject, knowledge and object.**

And that is why this passage matters so much.

The triangle is not an arbitrary religious ornament.

It's a picture of the structure of experience.


## 3. Prasūtipada - When does the flower actually become generative?

Now Abhinavagupta makes a subtle qualification.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 183

**वस्तुतः प्रसूतिपदं बीजसंमिश्रतयैव भवति।**

*Vastutaḥ prasūtipadaṃ bījasaṃmiśratayaiva bhavati.*

In the strict or actual sense, it becomes a generative locus through intermixture with the seed.

Now that's the crucial qualification.

The female generative matrix and the seed must be related for the manifestation in question.

This is why the passage doesn't simply identify Śakti as an isolated second creative substance.

The generative manifestation belongs to the union.

### Vastutaḥ - वस्तुतः

Really, actually, in truth.

Related to:

**वस्तु - vastu**

Thing, reality, entity.

The adverbial **-taḥ** formation gives the sense of actuality or strict consideration.

So:

**वस्तुतः - vastutaḥ**

In reality, strictly speaking.

### Prasūtipada - प्रसूतिपद

A place or condition of generation.

**प्रसूति - prasūti**

Birth, procreation, generation.

From *pra-√sū*, to bring forth or give birth.

**पद - pada**

Place, station, condition.

Thus:

**प्रसूतिपद - prasūtipada**

Generative locus.

Now, the Kashmir Series transcription has an anomalous spelling at this point resembling **प्रसऊतिपद**. The parallel Sanskrit witness gives the normal **prasūtipadaṃ**, and that's the reading used here.

### Bījasaṃmiśratā - बीजसंमिश्रता

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

**संमिश्र - saṃmiśra**

Mixed together, intermingled.

Related to *sam-√miśr*, to mix.

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract condition.

Thus:

**बीजसंमिश्रता - bījasaṃmiśratā**

The condition of being intermingled with the seed.

Instrumental:

**बीजसंमिश्रतया - bījasaṃmiśratayā**

Through intermixture with the seed.

And:

**एव - eva**

Precisely, indeed, only.

In connected speech:

**संमिश्रतयैव - saṃmiśratayaiva**.

Because:

**ā + e → ai**.

That's a genuine Sanskrit vowel-sandhi transformation.

And it's rather appropriate in a passage about divine intermixture.

### The important qualification: actuality versus capacity

Abhinavagupta distinguishes the actual generative condition from the mere capacity to become generative.

The text says that at another time the designation applies through:

**योग्यता - yogyatā**

Fitness, suitability, capacity.

Thus the matrix may be called generative because it possesses the appropriate capacity, even when the full generative conjunction is not presently manifest.

This is a subtle distinction.

An entity can possess a capacity without that capacity being actively exercised at every moment.

And the terminology can reflect either the actual operation or the potential for it.

Now, the philosophical analogy has limits.

Śiva and Śakti aren't finite biological organisms whose fertility can be straightforwardly equated with metaphysical freedom.

But the reproductive symbolism expresses the relationship between generative potential and manifest creation.

**The union is operative, not merely decorative.**

And therefore the triangle's generative meaning belongs to the convergence of seed and matrix.

Now the next sentence transforms that triangle into an entire family of triads.

Moon.

Sun.

Fire.

Creation.

Maintenance.

Withdrawal.

And the three subtle channels.

The text is about to become an enormous theological switchboard.

But because the underlying structure is clear, we can follow it without getting lost.


## 4. One triangle, several worlds - Moon, Sun and Fire

Now we cross onto Kashmir Series p. 184.

And the commentary takes the triangle we've just established and unfolds it into several interrelated triads.

The Sanskrit contains the long compound:

**सोमसूर्याग्निसृष्टिस्थितिसंहृतीडापिङ्गलासुषुम्नाधर्माधर्मशबलादिकोणत्रितया**

*Somasūryāgni-sṛṣṭi-sthiti-saṃhṛti-iḍā-piṅgalā-suṣumṇā-dharma-adharma-śabalādi-koṇatritayā.*

This is an enormous piece of Sanskrit.

Almost indecently enormous.

The sort of compound that makes an English comma feel like one of civilization's greatest achievements.

But the underlying structure is remarkably clear.

Abhinavagupta is presenting several triads as ways of interpreting the threefold structure of the Goddess.

Let's unpack them in the order in which they occur.

### Soma - सोम

Moon.

We've already studied Soma as the lunar expression of divine power.

In the preceding vowel analysis, Abhinavagupta associated lunar symbolism with fullness, repose and blissful manifestation.

Now the Moon appears as one member of a triad.

### Sūrya - सूर्य

Sun.

The solar power.

Associated earlier with illumination and a particular mode of manifestation and reabsorption.

And finally:

### Agni - अग्नि

Fire.

Now this is a major addition.

We've had the Moon and Sun for several installments.

And now a third principle joins them.

**Soma. Sūrya. Agni.**

Moon. Sun. Fire.

Together they form one traditional triadic arrangement.

But we must not assume that the three names always possess exactly one unchanging ritual assignment in every Tantra.

Different Śaiva and Śākta systems can organize their correspondences differently.

The present passage invokes them as a threefold manifestation.

And the broader philosophical function is to reveal differentiation through a triad.

### Why Fire matters

Fire is not merely a third astronomical object placed alongside the Sun and Moon.

In Indian ritual symbolism, *Agni* has associations with illumination, transformation, sacrifice and the conveying of offerings.

Within the present Tantric interpretation, it participates in a sacred triad of powers.

So we shouldn't reduce this to a modern physical classification of luminous objects.

The Sun is a star.

The Moon reflects sunlight.

Fire is a combustion process.

Those distinctions are scientifically valid, but they're not the level at which Abhinavagupta is organizing the symbolism.

He's interpreting three modes of divine manifestation.

And their intelligibility belongs to the same underlying consciousness.

### The philosophical connection

Now remember the first triangle.

The apprehended.

Apprehension.

The apprehender.

The three can be distinguished.

But no single member independently constitutes the totality of experience.

Similarly, the Moon-Sun-Fire triad distinguishes three powers or modes within the larger divine structure.

The three-cornered form becomes a symbolic container for multiple relational triads.

**It's the recurrence of threefold manifestation that matters.**

Not the accidental resemblance between a deity and a physical object.

And now Abhinavagupta immediately names another triad.

One we've encountered constantly.

Creation.

Maintenance.

Withdrawal.


## 5. Sṛṣṭi, Sthiti, Saṃhṛti - The universe has three verbs

The next sequence is:

**सृष्टि - sṛṣṭi**

Creation, emission.

**स्थिति - sthiti**

Maintenance, abiding.

**संहृति - saṃhṛti**

Withdrawal, reabsorption.

Now this is a beautiful progression.

And each word deserves a proper grammatical analysis.

### Sṛṣṭi - सृष्टि

From √**सृज् - sṛj**.

To release, emit, create.

The nominal formation:

**सृष्टि - sṛṣṭi**

Creation or emission.

Notice the consonant cluster:

**ष्ट = ष् + ट**

We encountered this in *sṛṣṭi* repeatedly.

The written form is not simply **सृस्ति**.

It's **सृष्टि**.

### Sthiti - स्थिति

From √**स्था - sthā**, to stand or abide.

The noun:

**स्थिति - sthiti**

Standing, continuance, stability, maintenance.

Now the initial conjunct:

**स्थ = स् + थ**.

And:

### Saṃhṛti - संहृति

From:

**सम् - sam**

Together, inward, completely.

**√हृ - hṛ**

To take, carry or gather.

The noun:

**संहृति - saṃhṛti**

Gathering in, withdrawal, reabsorption.

This is closely related in meaning to:

**संहार - saṃhāra**

But the lexical forms aren't identical.

Now the theological sequence.

Manifestation appears.

It is sustained.

It is reabsorbed.

Three distinguishable operations.

Yet within Trika's nondual metaphysics, they're not ultimate processes imposed upon Śiva from outside.

They belong to his freedom.

### Why maintenance is philosophically interesting

Imagine a universe being created.

That's one event.

Now it persists.

Ordinarily we might think persistence is merely the absence of further action.

But the Śaiva cosmological interpretation treats maintenance as a meaningful divine operation.

The universe remains manifest through the power of consciousness.

And when manifestation is withdrawn, the same conscious ground remains.

Thus the three operations express different relations between consciousness and its manifestations.

### The problem with a crude chronology

Now be careful.

Don't imagine the supreme Goddess literally performing these three operations one after another in a single universal clock-time, with nothing happening between them.

Remember Abhinavagupta's earlier distinction.

**क्रम - krama**

Sequence.

**अक्रम - akrama**

Nonsequence.

The triad can be described sequentially for instruction.

But the supreme consciousness is not metaphysically constituted by that temporal succession.

That is why the same threefold structure can operate at many levels.

For example, consider an ordinary perception.

Something appears.

Its presentation continues.

It disappears.

That resembles a creation-maintenance-withdrawal sequence.

But we shouldn't claim every perceptual episode is physically identical to the cosmic dissolution of the universe.

The analogy concerns the structural relation.

### The cosmic operations department

Imagine Śiva running a universe.

Creation:

"Right. Manifest the cosmos."

Maintenance:

"Keep the cosmos available."

Withdrawal:

"Gather the cosmos back."

Now the departments are distinguishable.

But they don't report to three independent absolutes.

They're manifestations of one conscious power.

And the point is not that Śiva is a project manager with a cosmic spreadsheet.

The point is that **one freedom can appear through several distinguishable operations**.

Now comes another triad.

One that belongs to the internal bodily and contemplative symbolism of Tantra.

**Iḍā, Piṅgalā and Suṣumṇā.**


## 6. इडा पिङ्गला सुषुम्णा - The three subtle channels

Now we reach:

**इडा - iḍā**

**पिङ्गला - piṅgalā**

**सुषुम्णा - suṣumṇā**

The three channels familiar from Śaiva and wider yogic subtle-body traditions.

But before we explain them, a qualification.

**Abhinavagupta names the three channels here as another triad belonging to the symbolic structure of the Goddess.**

He does not pause at this point to give a complete practical manual of breathing exercises.

Nor does the sentence establish that these channels have been anatomically identified as physical nerves.

They're components of a traditional subtle-body and contemplative system.

Their theological significance is the immediate issue.

### Iḍā - इडा

The name of one subtle channel.

In many later yogic systems, Iḍā is associated with the lunar side.

But the precise correlations can vary across traditions.

The name here belongs to the triad.

Notice the consonant:

**ड - ḍa**

The retroflex stop.

And the long ending:

**आ - ā**.

**इडा - iḍā**.

### Piṅgalā - पिङ्गला

The second channel.

Now the Devanāgarī is particularly useful.

**पि - pi**

**ङ्ग - ṅga**

**ला - lā**

The consonant cluster:

**ङ्ग = ङ् + ग**

A velar nasal followed by a voiced velar stop.

The word is:

**पिङ्गला - piṅgalā**.

In many later yogic systems, Piṅgalā is associated with the solar side.

But again, the current passage doesn't provide an exhaustive mapping of every function.

### Suṣumṇā - सुषुम्णा

The central subtle channel.

Now look carefully at the consonants.

**सु - su**

**षु - ṣu**

**म्णा - mṇā**

The conjunct:

**म्ण = म् + ण**

A nasal cluster involving a retroflex nasal.

It's an awkward-looking written form, but perfectly identifiable once separated.

**सुषुम्णा - suṣumṇā**.

Now here's the important linguistic distinction.

The word is not simply *suṣumna* without retroflexion.

The retroflex consonants are part of the standard Sanskrit spelling.

And in the yogic context, the name designates the central channel.

### Why this triad belongs here

The earlier triangle expressed the relation among object, knowing and knower.

Now the same threefold generative symbolism is expressed through the channels of subtle physiology.

Iḍā.

Piṅgalā.

Suṣumṇā.

Three distinguishable channels.

One subtle-body system.

And the whole system is interpreted as belonging to Śakti's manifestation.

### The danger of forcing exact correspondences

Here's something to avoid.

You read:

Moon, Sun and Fire.

Then:

Iḍā, Piṅgalā and Suṣumṇā.

And immediately construct a universal equation:

Moon = Iḍā = creation = object.

Sun = Piṅgalā = maintenance = knowledge.

Fire = Suṣumṇā = withdrawal = subject.

But Abhinavagupta's sentence **lists several triads without explicitly deriving every pairwise identification**.

Some of those associations occur in particular Tantric traditions.

Others depend on ritual or contemplative context.

We shouldn't invent a complete correspondence table and call it an attested statement from this prose.

The securely established point is that these distinct threefold systems can express the same underlying structure of Śakti's manifestation.

And that is already philosophically substantial.

### The relation to experience

Now consider the broader implication.

The subject-knowing-object triad belongs to epistemology.

Moon-Sun-Fire belongs to cosmological and ritual symbolism.

Creation-maintenance-withdrawal belongs to the dynamics of manifestation.

Iḍā-Piṅgalā-Suṣumṇā belongs to subtle-body practice.

Different domains.

The same threefold organizing principle.

And, in Trika, one conscious ground.

**The Goddess is not divided into four independent universes merely because four different symbolic systems describe her.**

She is one power manifesting through distinguishable relations.

And now Abhinavagupta adds a final triad.

Dharma.

Adharma.

And their mixture.


## 7. धर्म अधर्म शबल - Even moral difference belongs to the triangle

The last named triad is:

**धर्म - dharma**

**अधर्म - adharma**

**शबल - śabala**

Now, this is interesting.

Because we've moved from cosmology and subtle physiology into the distinction between right and wrong, order and disorder, or meritorious and nonmeritorious action.

But we mustn't treat these as merely psychological moods.

The terms have technical meanings across Indian philosophical and religious traditions.

### Dharma - धर्म

From √**धृ - dhṛ**, to hold or sustain.

The noun:

**धर्म - dharma**

Can mean sustaining principle, duty, law, virtue, religious order or prescribed conduct.

Its precise meaning depends upon context.

### Adharma - अधर्म

The negative:

**अ - a-**

Plus:

**धर्म - dharma**.

Thus:

**अधर्म - adharma**

That which is contrary to Dharma.

Often translated as unrighteousness, nonvirtue or disorder.

### Śabala - शबल

Variegated, mottled, mixed.

Now this is a wonderfully appropriate word.

Because the triad includes two opposed categories and the mixed or differentiated condition.

**शबल - śabala**

Variegated.

Now the philosophical point.

The manifested universe contains differentiated conduct and conditions.

Dharma.

Adharma.

And their mixtures.

But the fact that the ultimate ground is one doesn't erase those differences at the practical level.

That would be a dangerous misunderstanding.

### Nonduality doesn't make ethics disappear

Imagine somebody saying:

"Everything is Śiva. Therefore Dharma and Adharma are indistinguishable."

That is not what Abhinavagupta's passage establishes.

In fact, the triad explicitly preserves their distinguishability within manifestation.

The nondual claim concerns their ultimate ground.

It doesn't make their practical consequences identical.

This is the same distinction we've been studying throughout the commentary.

A pot and a tree are not the same ordinary object.

But their ultimate reality is not independent of consciousness.

Similarly, Dharma and Adharma have different roles in the manifested field.

Their appearance within the divine power doesn't abolish those roles.

### The philosophical meaning of śabala

The term is especially useful because it suggests mixture and variety.

Manifestation isn't merely two perfectly separated categories.

It can contain complex combinations.

Different dispositions.

Different actions.

Different consequences.

And the same underlying conscious reality can manifest through these varied structures.

So the triangle does not simply represent three rigid metaphysical boxes.

It expresses the capacity for differentiation within one power.

Now we can gather the named triads.

| Domain | The threefold expression |
|--------|--------|
| Epistemology | Grāhya · Grahaṇa · Grāhaka |
| Cosmological symbolism | Soma · Sūrya · Agni |
| Manifestation | Sṛṣṭi · Sthiti · Saṃhṛti |
| Subtle-body tradition | Iḍā · Piṅgalā · Suṣumṇā |
| Ethical/qualitative distinction | Dharma · Adharma · Śabala |

Now the astonishing thing is that Abhinavagupta folds all of them into a single description of the Goddess.

The divine triangle is not merely a shape.

It's a general symbolic structure capable of expressing many different relations within manifestation.

And that leads to one of the most impressive names in the passage.

**Pārameśvarī Bhairavī Bhaṭṭārikā Mudrā.**


## 8. पारमेश्वरी भैरवी भट्टारिका मुद्रा - The entire triangle is the Goddess

Now the Sanskrit becomes magnificent.

Abhinavagupta calls this threefold power:

**पारमेश्वरी भैरवी भट्टारिका मुद्रा**

*Pārameśvarī bhairavī bhaṭṭārikā mudrā.*

The supreme divine seal or configuration of venerable Bhairavī.

And he says that she is designated *yoni* because she is the support of that generative form.

Let's learn the terms.

### Pārameśvarī - पारमेश्वरी

Pertaining to the Supreme Lord.

From:

**परमेश्वर - parameśvara**

Supreme Lord.

The feminine derivative:

**पारमेश्वरी - pārameśvarī**

The supreme divine Goddess, or what belongs to Parameśvara.

Notice the initial vowel strengthening.

**प - pa** becomes **पा - pā** in the derivative formation.

This is a legitimate Sanskrit morphological relationship, not an arbitrary mystical interpretation.

### Bhairavī - भैरवी

The feminine form associated with Bhairava.

**भैरव - bhairava**

Bhairava.

**भैरवी - bhairavī**

Bhairavī.

Here she is not an independent goddess who has somehow escaped the universal Śiva-Śakti relation.

She is the divine power through which the differentiated triangle becomes manifest.

### Bhaṭṭārikā - भट्टारिका

Venerable Lady, exalted Goddess, sovereign Mistress.

This is a title of reverence.

Abhinavagupta uses it frequently for the supreme divine powers.

And it matters because the theological register is not merely abstract.

He's not talking about an impersonal geometrical theorem.

He's discussing the living divine power as understood by his tradition.

### Mudrā - मुद्रा

Seal, sign, symbolic configuration, gesture.

Now this word is important.

In ordinary contexts, *mudrā* can mean a seal or mark.

In Tantric practice, it may designate a ritual gesture, a divine configuration, a partner in certain contexts, or a symbol of an underlying power.

Here the immediate sense involves the divine generative configuration.

**The triangle is a mudrā of Śakti.**

Not merely a drawing.

Not merely a hand gesture.

It is a sacred articulation of her triadic nature.

### Yonyādhāratā - योन्याधारता

The condition of being the support of the generative matrix.

Now:

**योनि - yoni**

Womb, matrix.

**आधार - ādhāra**

Support, foundation.

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract condition.

The compound expresses the supporting relation.

And this is the reason Abhinavagupta gives for the designation *yoni*.

The divine power is not merely *inside* a geometrical triangle as though she were an object enclosed by three lines.

The triangle expresses her generative structure.

And its support belongs to her own nature.

### The important geometrical qualification

Now, some explanatory translations interpret the union of Śiva's and Śakti's triadic forms as a **six-cornered configuration**, comparable to the intersection of two triangles.

That's a useful traditional extension.

But the Sanskrit at this exact point does not explicitly command us to draw two triangles or use the technical word **षट्कोण - ṣaṭkoṇa**.

It speaks of the three-cornered divine manifestation, its *mudrā*, and its generative support.

So we shouldn't silently put a complete hexagram construction into Abhinavagupta's sentence.

The six-cornered interpretation may illuminate the broader symbolism.

It isn't a substitute for the words actually present.

And that distinction matters, especially if you're studying the history of Śaiva diagrams.

### The incredible philosophical result

Now, think about what we've accomplished.

The first triangle described cognition.

Object.

Knowing.

Knower.

Then it became the basis for multiple threefold manifestations.

Moon, Sun, Fire.

Creation, maintenance, withdrawal.

The subtle channels.

Dharma and Adharma.

Now the whole structure is identified with the Goddess's sacred *mudrā*.

This is a remarkable union of epistemology, cosmology and ritual symbolism.

And it's not arbitrary within the system.

Because all the differentiated triads express modes of one conscious freedom.

**The Goddess is the power through which the threefold structure of manifestation becomes possible.**

That's the central claim.

And now Abhinavagupta provides scriptural support.

First from the *Kubjikāmata*.

Then from the *Trikatantrasāra*.

Both quotations are short.

But the philosophical implications are enormous.


## 9. The Kubjikāmata - Above Māyā, Mahāmāyā is triangular bliss

Abhinavagupta introduces the source:

**श्रीकुब्जिकामते खण्डचक्रविचारे**

*Śrīkubjikāmate khaṇḍacakravicāre.*

Within the *Khaṇḍacakravicāra* discussion of the venerable *Kubjikāmata*.

Now here's the quoted passage.

SCRIPTURAL FRAGMENT QUOTED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA · P. 184

**मायोपरि महामाया त्रिकोणानन्दरूपिणी।**

*Māyopari mahāmāyā trikoṇānandarūpiṇī.*

Above Māyā is Mahāmāyā, whose form is triangular bliss.

Now this is a beautiful line.

But there's an important source-critical issue: the GRETIL apparatus explicitly notes that this exact half-verse was not located in the consulted edition of the *Kubjikāmata*. Related wording is attested in other Śaiva sources. We can securely say **Abhinavagupta quotes and attributes the fragment to the Kubjikāmata**; we should not claim its location in a surviving published *Kubjikāmata* chapter has been independently confirmed. ; \]

Now let's unpack it.

### Māyopari - मायोपरि

**माया - māyā**

Māyā.

**उपरि - upari**

Above, over, beyond.

The joined form:

**मायोपरि - māyopari**.

Now notice the sandhi.

**ā + u → o**.

Thus:

*māyā + upari → māyopari*.

Excellent.

A real vowel combination.

And the meaning is *above Māyā*.

### Mahāmāyā - महामाया

**महा - mahā**

Great.

**माया - māyā**

Māyā.

Thus:

**महामाया - mahāmāyā**

Great Māyā.

Now in Śaiva cosmology, *Māyā* can be a technical principle of differentiation.

And *Mahāmāyā* can designate a higher divine domain or power, depending on the system.

But don't assume the two words mean simply:

"Māyā = fake."

"Mahāmāyā = extremely fake."

That would be a dreadful reading.

In this passage, Mahāmāyā is explicitly associated with the triangle and bliss.

### Trikoṇa - त्रिकोण

Three-cornered, triangular.

**त्रि - tri**

Three.

**कोण - koṇa**

Corner, angle.

Thus:

**त्रिकोण - trikoṇa**

Triangle.

And:

### Ānanda - आनन्द

Bliss.

### Rūpiṇī - रूपिणी

Having the form of, possessing the appearance or nature of.

A feminine adjective.

Thus:

**त्रिकोणानन्दरूपिणी - trikoṇānandarūpiṇī**

She whose form is triangular bliss.

Now the Sanskrit is compact enough to admit a little nuance in translation.

The compound can be read as connecting the triangular form with bliss, rather than merely saying Mahāmāyā is a geometric object.

And that's important.

The triangle represents the blissful generative power.

It isn't simply a physical diagram floating in the sky.

### Above Māyā - What does that mean?

Now the philosophical issue.

Māyā, within the standard Śaiva tattva scheme, is a principle through which differentiated limitation becomes possible.

The supreme Śakti is not simply another limited object within that domain.

So the fragment locates Mahāmāyā in a higher relation to the manifesting structure.

And describes her as the blissful triangular power.

That fits Abhinavagupta's argument.

The triadic matrix of cognition and manifestation is not merely a product of already existing limited objects.

It belongs to the deeper divine power through which differentiation is possible.

### The Goddess has not become a triangle-shaped prisoner

Imagine somebody drawing a triangle and announcing:

"Excellent. I have enclosed the Supreme Goddess."

No.

The symbol doesn't imprison the reality it represents.

Its function is to disclose a relation.

The three corners make visible the differentiated structure.

The blissful divine power is not exhausted by the drawn lines.

That's why *trikoṇānandarūpiṇī* is a theological statement rather than an assertion about the Goddess's measurable physical shape.

And Abhinavagupta uses it to confirm the connection between Mahāmāyā, the divine triangle and the generative power.

Now he brings us to the ritual conclusion.

**The union of Śiva and Śakti is to be worshipped as one's own Self.**

And that is where this passage becomes genuinely profound.


## 10. Śiva and Śakti become one compact mass - The union, not merely the picture

Here is Abhinavagupta's conclusion from the preceding argument:

**तथाविधबीजकुसुमैकघनभावशिवशक्तिसङ्घट्टः स्वयं स्वात्मनैव पूज्यः।**

*Tathāvidhabījakusumaikaghanabhāvaśivaśaktisaṅghaṭṭaḥ svayaṃ svātmanaiva pūjyaḥ.*

The union of Śiva and Śakti, whose seed-and-flower relation constitutes one compact reality, is to be worshipped by oneself, as one's own Self.

Now that's extraordinary.

Because it takes the explicit reproductive imagery and directs its significance toward recognition of the Self.

Let's dissect it.

### Bīja - बीज

Seed.

Here the Śiva-side of the generative symbolism.

### Kusuma - कुसुम

Flower, and in the Kaula context the female generative substance.

The Śakti-side.

### Ekaghana - एकघन

One compact mass.

**एक - eka**

One.

**घन - ghana**

Compact, dense, massed.

Thus:

**एकघन - ekaghana**

An undivided compact unity.

Now, here's the important thing.

The union isn't being described as two independently ultimate substances merely touching.

Their reality is one.

The imagery distinguishes the generative aspects.

The metaphysics denies their ultimate independence.

### Saṅghaṭṭa - सङ्घट्ट

Encounter, collision, conjunction, union.

Related to the verbal family of joining or striking together.

Now this is a particularly important term in Kaula and Trika symbolism.

It can describe a meeting of divine powers.

The context may be overtly erotic or reproductive.

But the metaphysical interpretation concerns the unity of consciousness and its expressive freedom.

And here Abhinavagupta explicitly turns that union toward self-worship.

### Svayam - स्वयम्

By oneself, personally, oneself.

An indeclinable.

### Svātmanā - स्वात्मना

By one's own Self.

Now:

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**आत्मन् - ātman**

Self.

The instrumental singular of *ātman* is:

**आत्मना - ātmanā**.

And:

**स्वात्मना - svātmanā**.

By one's own Self.

### Eva - एव

Precisely, indeed.

In sandhi:

**स्वात्मनैव - svātmanaiva**.

Because:

**ā + e → ai**.

Now:

### Pūjya - पूज्य

Worthy of worship, to be worshipped.

From √**पूज् - pūj**, to honour or worship.

The gerundive formation:

**पूज्य - pūjya**

To be worshipped.

Now the philosophical statement is astonishing.

The union of Śiva and Śakti is not merely a distant cosmic event.

Its supreme reality is to be recognized through one's own conscious Self.

**The worshipper, the act of worship and the divine reality worshipped are not ultimately independent.**

Now, this does not mean the historical ritual practices and bodily symbolism vanish from the tradition.

They are part of the textual context.

But the interpretation here directs them toward *svātman*, one's own Self.

The ritual form serves recognition.

It doesn't replace it.

### The difference between representing a union and recognizing it

Imagine looking at a sacred diagram.

Two triangles.

An arrangement of powers.

You memorize every name.

Every colour.

Every ritual correspondence.

Excellent.

But then you regard the entire system as a collection of foreign objects outside awareness.

The philosophical problem remains.

You have acquired information about a representation.

You haven't necessarily recognized the conscious ground the representation is intended to disclose.

Now Abhinavagupta's statement turns that around.

The unity of Śiva and Śakti is worshipped as the Self.

The ritual symbol is not the final metaphysical object.

The point is recognition of the nondual reality represented by the symbol.

And that's why this passage isn't simply a piece of sacred sexual symbolism.

It's a statement of Trika's nondual theology.

Now Abhinavagupta cites the *Trikatantrasāra* to confirm it.


## 11. The Trikatantrasāra - Worship the supreme Trika through Śiva-Śakti union

SCRIPTURAL VERSE QUOTED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA · P. 184

**शिवशक्तिसमापत्त्या शिवशक्तिघनात्मकः।**

**शिवशक्तिसमापत्तित्रिकं सम्पूजयेत्परम्॥**

*Śivaśaktisamāpattyā śivaśaktighanātmakaḥ.*

*Śivaśaktisamāpattitrikaṃ sampūjayet param.*

A close rendering:

Having become a compact unity of Śiva and Śakti through their conjunction, one should worship the supreme Trika characterized by that union.

Now the grammar is absolutely central.

And the parallel Sanskrit witnesses support **घनात्मकः - ghanātmakaḥ**, where the Kashmir Series transcription has the anomalous spelling **घृनात्मकः**.

The word *ghana* is exactly what we'd expect from the immediately preceding discussion of condensation and undivided compactness. ; \]

### Samāpatti - समापत्ति

Conjunction, attainment, complete coming together.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Together.

**आ - ā**

Toward, completely.

A formation of √**पद् - pad**, to go or arrive.

Thus:

**समापत्ति - samāpatti**

Coming together, attainment, conjunction.

Now the instrumental singular:

**समापत्त्या - samāpattyā**

Through conjunction.

And:

**शिवशक्तिसमापत्त्या - śivaśaktisamāpattyā**

Through the conjunction of Śiva and Śakti.

### Ghanātmakaḥ - घनात्मकः

**घन - ghana**

Compact, dense, undivided mass.

**आत्मक - ātmaka**

Having the nature of.

Masculine nominative singular:

**घनात्मकः - ghanātmakaḥ**.

Thus:

**शिवशक्तिघनात्मकः - śivaśaktighanātmakaḥ**

Having become a compact unity of Śiva and Śakti.

Now the implied subject is the practitioner.

The one who worships.

And here's the important thing.

The verse doesn't merely say:

"Look at Śiva and Śakti from a distance."

It connects the worshipper's own condition with the Śiva-Śakti union.

### Sampūjayet - सम्पूजयेत्

One should worship completely or properly.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Completely.

**√पूज् - pūj**

To worship, honour.

Now:

**पूजयेत् - pūjayet**

One should worship.

A third-person singular optative.

With the prefix:

**सम्पूजयेत् - sampūjayet**

One should worship fully.

Now remember the optative suffix:

**-एत् - -et**

It frequently expresses instruction, possibility or prescription.

Not simply a present-tense assertion that somebody is already doing something.

### Param - परम्

Supreme.

Neuter accusative singular, agreeing with:

**त्रिकम् - trikam**

The triad.

Thus:

**परं त्रिकम् - paraṃ trikam**

The supreme Trika.

Now the verse is a prescription.

Having become a compact unity of Śiva and Śakti, one should worship the supreme Trika.

### The great distinction

This is not merely two entities embracing and thereby manufacturing a third ultimate substance.

The unity is already fundamental.

The ritual and contemplative act discloses that unity.

And the triad expresses its differentiated manifestation.

### The worshipper is not excluded

Now consider an ordinary ritual situation.

You have a worshipper.

An object of worship.

An act of worship.

Three roles.

Exactly the same triadic structure we've been discussing.

Abhinavagupta's interpretation turns those roles toward their nondual ground.

The worshipper is not metaphysically independent of Śiva.

The sacred power isn't independent of Śakti.

And the act of worship is itself a manifestation of conscious freedom.

So the ritual can be understood as a mode of recognition.

**Śiva-Śakti worship is not simply the finite subject addressing a permanently separate divine object.**

It can become the recognition of the one conscious reality manifesting as worshipper, worship and worshipped.

Now that is the philosophical heart of the passage.

And it is why the triangle becomes such a powerful image.

It symbolizes the differentiated structure through which one consciousness knows, acts and recognizes itself.

But here's the extraordinary thing.

Abhinavagupta now turns away from ritual geometry.

Back to speech.

And he says that even though these powers become explicitly differentiated in **Vaikharī**, their principal reality remains in **Parā Vāk**.

The flower, triangle and divine union are therefore not the end of the argument.

They're part of an account of how speech itself manifests.

And this brings us back to the very beginning of the entire commentary.


## 12. वैखरी versus परावाक् - The Goddess doesn't begin existing when your mouth opens

Now Abhinavagupta makes the transition explicit.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · PP. 184-185

**एवं च घनीभावोऽपि वैखरीरूपे यद्यपि स्फुटीभवति तथापि सर्वसर्वात्मनि परावाग्वपुषि मुख्यतयावतिष्ठते।**

*Evaṃ ca ghanībhāvo 'pi vaikharīrūpe yady api sphuṭībhavati tathāpi sarvasarvātmani parāvāgvapuṣi mukhyatayāvatiṣṭhate.*

And thus, although condensation becomes explicit in the form of Vaikharī, it nevertheless principally abides within the nature of Parā Vāk, which encompasses everything.

This is an extraordinarily important sentence.

Because it prevents us from confusing outward physical speech with the ultimate ground of the phonemic powers.

Let's dissect it carefully.

### Ghanībhāva - घनीभाव

Condensation, becoming dense or explicit.

We've encountered:

**घन - ghana**

Dense.

**घनीभूत - ghanībhūta**

Become condensed.

Now:

**घनीभाव - ghanībhāva**

The condition or process of becoming concentrated into a more determinate form.

This describes the relation between the subtle vowel-powers and their more explicit consonantal manifestations.

### Vaikharī - वैखरी

Articulated external speech.

In the standard fourfold speech doctrine:

**परा - parā**

Supreme speech.

**पश्यन्ती - paśyantī**

The emerging visionary or unified mode.

**मध्यमा - madhyamā**

Intermediate, inner articulated speech.

**वैखरी - vaikharī**

Externally articulated speech.

Now the present passage concerns the relation between the most explicit form and its supreme ground.

### Sphuṭībhavati - स्फुटीभवति

Becomes explicit, clear or distinct.

From:

**स्फुट - sphuṭa**

Manifest, clear, distinct.

And:

**भवति - bhavati**

Becomes.

So:

**स्फुटीभवति - sphuṭībhavati**

Becomes fully manifest.

Now:

### Sarvasarvātman - सर्वसर्वात्मन्

The all-containing nature of everything.

A compact metaphysical expression involving:

**सर्व - sarva**

All.

**आत्मन् - ātman**

Self, nature.

Abhinavagupta repeatedly uses such constructions to express *sarvasarvātmakatva*, the condition of everything's ultimate implication in everything through the universal conscious ground.

It doesn't mean that every differentiated object is physically identical to every other object.

The point concerns their ultimate non-independence.

### Parāvāgvapus - परावाग्वपुस्

The nature or body of supreme speech.

**परा - parā**

Supreme.

**वाक् - vāk**

Speech.

**वपुस् - vapus**

Body, form, nature.

Thus:

**परावाग्वपुस् - parāvāgvapus**

The form of supreme speech.

The locative:

**परावाग्वपुषि - parāvāgvapuṣi**

In the nature of Parā Vāk.

### Mukhyatayā - मुख्यतया

Principally, primarily.

From:

**मुख्य - mukhya**

Principal, chief.

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract condition.

Instrumental:

**मुख्यतया - mukhyatayā**

In the principal manner.

### Avatiṣṭhate - अवतिष्ठते

Abides, remains established.

From:

**अव - ava**

Down, in.

**√स्था - sthā**

To stand, remain.

The middle-voice verb:

**अवतिष्ठते - avatiṣṭhate**

Remains established.

Now the philosophical meaning.

Even when the phonemic powers become outwardly articulated as particular sounds, their deeper reality isn't produced anew by the physical act of pronunciation.

They principally abide in Parā Vāk.

**The differentiated form is explicit in Vaikharī, but its ultimate conscious ground is Parā.**

That's the distinction.

### The cosmic microphone problem

Imagine somebody saying:

"Śakti exists only when somebody physically pronounces a Sanskrit syllable."

Then what happens in silence?

Has Śakti disappeared?

And what about before the person begins speaking?

Does the supreme conscious power need a human vocal tract to exist?

Abhinavagupta's answer is obviously no.

The physical articulation is one manifestation of speech.

The underlying expressive power is more comprehensive.

And that's why the commentary insists that the condensed phonemic form remains principally established in Parā.

The Goddess doesn't begin existing when you open your mouth.

Vaikharī is an expression.

Not the source of the ultimate reality.

Now he takes that argument into an astonishing direction.

Even the throat and lips, he says, have their principal reality within the all-containing nature of consciousness.

And this is where we must distinguish physical articulators from their subtle conscious powers.


## 13. कण्ठ and ओष्ठ - Even the throat and lips belong to consciousness

Abhinavagupta continues:

**तत्र परं कण्ठोष्ठस्थानकरणान्यपि सर्वसर्वात्मकमेव इति विशेषः।**

*Tatra paraṃ kaṇṭhoṣṭhasthānakaraṇāny api sarvasarvātmakam eva iti viśeṣaḥ.*

In that supreme domain, even the places and instruments of articulation-the throat, lips and so forth-participate in the all-containing nature of consciousness.

Now that's quite a claim.

Because he's taking something very physical-your throat and mouth-and relating it to Parā Vāk.

But the distinction is subtle.

He isn't saying that the physical throat, as an anatomical object, exists everywhere in the same way.

He's saying that the **power through which such articulation is manifest is not ultimately foreign to consciousness**.

### Kaṇṭha - कण्ठ

Throat.

Now:

**कण्ठ - kaṇṭha**

Contains the conjunct:

**ण्ठ = ण् + ठ**

A retroflex nasal followed by an aspirated retroflex stop.

This is worth recognizing in Devanāgarī because it looks quite different from ordinary dental clusters.

And:

### Oṣṭha - ओष्ठ

Lip.

Now look at the consonant cluster:

**ष्ठ = ष् + ठ**

Retroflex sibilant followed by an aspirated retroflex stop.

Together:

**कण्ठोष्ठ - kaṇṭhoṣṭha**

Throat and lip.

The vowel sandhi:

*kaṇṭha + oṣṭha → kaṇṭhoṣṭha.*

Because:

**a + o → au** in ordinary sandhi? Not here: the resulting form *kaṇṭhoṣṭha* preserves the phonetic sequence as a compound with the *o* vowel after *kaṇṭh-*; in the normalized compound, the first member is treated in the joined form. The important practical reading is **kaṇṭhoṣṭha**, not an invented *kaṇṭhauṣṭha*.

The compound refers to the articulatory regions.

### Sthāna - स्थान

Place, location, station.

In Sanskrit phonetics:

A **place of articulation**.

For example:

The throat-region.

The palate.

The retroflex region.

The teeth.

The lips.

### Karaṇa - करण

Instrument, means.

From √**कृ - kṛ**, to do or make, through its nominal formation.

In grammatical and phonetic discussions, *karaṇa* can refer to the articulatory instrument or organ involved in producing a sound.

Now the compound:

**स्थानकरण - sthānakaraṇa**

Places and instruments.

And the plural:

**स्थानकरणानि - sthānakaraṇāni**

Neuter nominative or accusative plural.

Thus:

**कण्ठोष्ठस्थानकरणानि - kaṇṭhoṣṭhasthānakaraṇāni**

The throat-and-lip articulatory places and instruments.

Now the metaphysical question.

If these are physical organs, how can they belong to Parā Vāk?

The answer requires distinguishing the **manifest organ** from the **conscious power of articulation**.

The gross organ is a differentiated manifestation.

The power through which it can express meaningful sound is not ultimately separate from consciousness.

And therefore the specific articulation can be understood as one manifestation of the universal expressive power.

### The human mouth isn't the source of the Absolute

Imagine a philosopher holding up a human tongue.

"This," he declares, "is the ultimate source of Sanskrit."

Well, the physical tongue participates in speech production.

But does that explain meaningful apprehension?

Does it explain how the listener recognizes an utterance?

Does it explain the conscious intention to speak?

Does it explain the internal capacity for speech before sound is produced?

Not by itself.

Those questions require additional explanation.

Abhinavagupta's answer is that the entire relation belongs to the power of consciousness.

The throat and lips express it in Vaikharī.

But the underlying ability to articulate and apprehend isn't produced from nothing at the instant of external pronunciation.

**The physical act is a manifestation of speech, not the whole reality of speech.**

And now he makes the point through ordinary experience.

We can speak inwardly.

We can see inwardly.

And these capacities are immediately recognizable.


## 14. अन्तः सञ्जल्पेत् पश्येत् - The inner voice and inner seeing

Here's the next phrase.

**तथाह्यन्तरपि सञ्जल्पेत् पश्येदिति स्फुट एवानुभवः।**

*Tathā hy antar api sañjalpet paśyed iti sphuṭa evānubhavaḥ.*

Indeed, the experience of speaking or seeing internally is quite clear.

Now this is a fascinating statement.

Because Abhinavagupta is explicitly appealing to ordinary introspective experience.

Not merely quoting scripture.

Not merely constructing a mathematical analogy.

He's asking you to examine what happens in your own awareness.

### Antar - अन्तर्

Within, inwardly.

An indeclinable or adverbial form in this use.

And:

**अपि - api**

Even, also.

So:

**अन्तरपि - antar api**

Even inwardly.

### Sañjalpet - सञ्जल्पेत्

One might speak, chatter or articulate.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Together.

**√जल्प् - jalp**

To speak, chatter, talk.

Now the optative:

**सञ्जल्पेत् - sañjalpet**

One might speak.

Notice the consonant cluster:

**ञ्ज = ञ् + ज**

A palatal nasal followed by a voiced palatal stop.

### Paśyet - पश्येत्

One might see.

From √**पश् - paś**, to see.

The optative form:

**पश्येत् - paśyet**.

Now compare:

**पश्यति - paśyati**

He sees.

**पश्येत् - paśyet**

He might see, should see or could see, according to context.

The difference lies in the verbal ending and mood.

### Sphuṭa - स्फुट

Clear, explicit.

And:

**अनुभव - anubhava**

Experience.

Thus:

**स्फुट एवानुभवः - sphuṭa evānubhavaḥ**

The experience is indeed clear.

Now here's the important philosophical point.

You can think in words without audibly speaking.

You can imagine or recall an object without having it physically present before your eyes.

The internal manifestation is distinguishable from external sensory presentation.

But it is not therefore meaningless.

It belongs to consciousness's expressive powers.

### The inner speech example

Imagine internally reciting:

**नमः शिवाय - namaḥ śivāya**.

You don't move your lips.

No audible sound necessarily emerges.

Yet you can apprehend the phrase.

You recognize its letters.

You may notice its rhythm.

And the meaning can be present.

Now, that doesn't imply that internal recitation and external articulation are physiologically identical.

They're distinguishable processes.

But the ability to apprehend meaningful speech isn't exhausted by physical sound waves.

And that is exactly the point Abhinavagupta is making.

### The inner seeing example

Now imagine a pot.

You can recall its shape.

Perhaps visualize it.

The pot isn't physically before your eyes.

Yet its form can be apprehended.

Again, the internal presentation isn't identical to ordinary visual perception.

But it shows that cognition isn't reducible to the immediate presence of external sensory stimulation.

And Abhinavagupta uses this kind of experience to explain the relation between the levels of speech and awareness.

### Why this matters for Parā Vāk

The inner articulation is still a differentiated manifestation.

It's not necessarily identical with the supreme Parā state.

But its existence shows that the power of meaningful expression extends beyond external phonation.

And therefore the physical articulators cannot be treated as the sole source of speech.

**The external sound is one expression of a more comprehensive conscious power.**

Now the text becomes even more interesting.

Because Abhinavagupta introduces a child learning language.

And he argues that even error and doubt involve the discriminating operation of speech.

This is one of the most intellectually interesting parts of the passage.


## 15. The child, the mistake, and the doubt - Speech operates even when cognition is wrong

Now Abhinavagupta says:

**किं बहुना बालोऽपि व्युत्पाद्यमानोऽन्तस्तथारूपतया विमृशति भावजातम्।**

*Kiṃ bahunā bālo 'pi vyutpādyamāno 'ntas tathārūpatayā vimṛśati bhāvajātam.*

What more need be said? Even a child undergoing instruction apprehends the multitude of things inwardly in that way.

Now this is extremely significant.

Because the child doesn't merely produce external sounds.

The child begins to develop meaningful distinctions.

And even when those distinctions are mistaken, the operation of understanding or reflective apprehension is present.

### Bālaḥ - बालः

Child.

Masculine nominative singular.

### Vyutpādyamānaḥ - व्युत्पाद्यमानः

Being instructed, trained, brought into understanding.

Related to the *vyutpatti* family we've already encountered.

**व्युत्पत्ति - vyutpatti**

Proficiency, understanding, linguistic acquisition.

The present passive participial form:

**व्युत्पाद्यमान - vyutpādyamāna**

Being taught or brought into proficiency.

And masculine nominative singular:

**व्युत्पाद्यमानः - vyutpādyamānaḥ**.

Now note the ending:

**-मानः - -mānaḥ**

A common participial pattern.

### Bhāvajāta - भावजात

The multitude or collection of entities.

**भाव - bhāva**

Being, entity, manifested condition.

**जात - jāta**

Born, arisen, produced; also a collection or class in compounds.

So:

**भावजात - bhāvajāta**

The multitude of manifested things.

And the accusative singular:

**भावजातम् - bhāvajātam**.

### Vimṛśati - विमृशति

Apprehends, examines, reflects upon.

From:

**वि - vi**

Distinctly, apart, analytically.

**√मृश् - mṛś**

To touch, examine or apprehend.

Now remember:

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive apprehension.

And:

**विमृशति - vimṛśati**

He or she apprehends or reflects.

Same verbal family.

### The child learning the world

Imagine a small child looking at a dog.

They hear somebody say "dog."

They connect the sound with the animal.

Later they see another animal.

Perhaps they identify a goat as a dog.

They're mistaken.

But the mistake involves a meaningful classification.

They're not simply making meaningless noises.

They are apprehending something under a concept.

Now here's the important point.

**Error still involves an act of cognition.**

The identification may be wrong.

But the occurrence of apprehension is real.

And that's why Abhinavagupta immediately introduces two technical terms.

*Viparyaya.*

And *Saṃśaya.*

Error.

And doubt.

### Viparyaya - विपर्यय

Misapprehension, inversion, incorrect cognition.

Related to:

**वि - vi**

Apart, differently.

**परि - pari**

Around.

And a verbal family involving reversal or turning.

The established noun:

**विपर्यय - viparyaya**

Error, misapprehension, reversal.

Now:

**विपर्ययेण - viparyayeṇa**

Through or by error.

Instrumental singular.

### Saṃśaya - संशय

Doubt, uncertainty.

The word is associated with a verbal formation involving being suspended or unsettled.

The instrumental:

**संशयेन - saṃśayena**

Through doubt.

Now the sentence says that the child may apprehend the world through error or through doubt.

But what remains present?

A differentiating cognitive activity.

And Abhinavagupta attributes that to:

**वाग्विमर्श - vāg-vimarśa**.

The reflexive or discriminating power of speech.

### The distinction between a wrong judgment and no cognition

Suppose you look at something in dim light.

You think:

"That's a snake."

Then discover it was a rope.

The first judgment was mistaken.

But it was still an occurrence of cognition.

It possessed a determinate content.

And your later correction depends upon recognizing the difference.

Similarly, doubt can take the form:

"Is that a snake or a rope?"

Again, the judgment is not settled.

But the alternatives are meaningful.

The capacity for differentiation is operating.

Now Abhinavagupta's point is that **the power of speech and reflective apprehension is involved even when the resulting judgment is not correct**.

And that's important because it distinguishes the existence of knowing from the truth of its particular content.

The error doesn't create consciousness.

The doubt doesn't abolish consciousness.

They are differentiated manifestations within awareness.

### A very important editorial distinction

The Kashmir Series inserts square-bracketed definitions of *viparyaya* and *saṃśaya* at this point.

Those definitions are useful.

But the main prose simply invokes the terms and continues.

We shouldn't silently treat the editor's explanatory definitions as uninterrupted words from Abhinavagupta.

The actual argument is already clear without that interpolation.

**Even mistaken or uncertain cognition manifests through the power of reflective differentiation.**

And now the commentary takes that argument back into phonetics.

Not simply the places of articulation.

But the subtle qualities of vocal effort.

Opening.

Closing.

Aspiration.

Voice.

And the difference between a lightly aspirated and strongly aspirated consonant.

The Goddess has now entered the actual mechanics of speech.

And, hilariously, the edition's editor responds with a detailed grammar lesson.

Which contains one particularly revealing historical clue.


## 16. Saṃvāra, Vivāra, Alpaprāṇa and Mahāprāṇa - The anatomy of sacred speech

Now Abhinavagupta lists several phonetic distinctions.

The main prose contains:

**संवारविवाराल्पप्राणमहाप्राणताश्वासनादानुप्रदानादियोगः**

*Saṃvāra-vivāra-alpaprāṇa-mahāprāṇatā-śvāsa-nāda-anupradānādi-yogaḥ.*

The presence or operation of closure, opening, lesser and greater aspiration, breath, resonance and related phonetic properties.

This is quite a list.

And it's important because Abhinavagupta argues that these distinctions must have a corresponding basis within the inner power of articulation.

Let's learn them properly.

### Saṃvāra - संवार

Closure, constriction, closing.

Related to √**वृ - vṛ**, to cover or enclose.

Now the point is phonetic.

Different Sanskrit consonants involve different articulatory closures and constrictions.

For example, the production of a stop consonant involves a temporary interruption or strong restriction of airflow.

### Vivāra - विवार

Opening, aperture.

Related to *vi-√vṛ*, to uncover or open.

Now compare:

**संवार - saṃvāra**

Closure.

**विवार - vivāra**

Opening.

These form a useful opposition.

And they describe relevant articulatory properties.

### Alpaprāṇa - अल्पप्राण

Lightly aspirated, with relatively little breath.

**अल्प - alpa**

Little, small.

**प्राण - prāṇa**

Breath, vital activity.

Thus:

**अल्पप्राण - alpaprāṇa**

Having little aspiration.

### Mahāprāṇa - महाप्राण

Strongly aspirated.

**महा - mahā**

Great.

**प्राण - prāṇa**

Breath.

Thus:

**महाप्राण - mahāprāṇa**

Having greater aspiration.

Now compare:

**क - ka**

And:

**ख - kha**.

The second is aspirated.

Likewise:

**ग - ga**

And:

**घ - gha**.

Again, the second is aspirated.

And:

**त - ta**

Versus:

**थ - tha**.

The distinction is phonemic in Sanskrit.

Not simply a matter of speaking more loudly.

That's important.

**Aspiration and volume are not the same thing.**

### Śvāsa - श्वास

Breath.

Related to √**श्वस् - śvas**, to breathe.

### Nāda - नाद

Sound, resonance.

Now, in ordinary phonetic description, *nāda* can concern voicing or resonance.

In Tantric metaphysics, *Nāda* can also name a subtle level of sacred sound.

Those uses must be distinguished by context.

Here the main argument is about sound-production characteristics.

### Anupradāna - अनुप्रदान

A further or secondary articulatory contribution, traditionally used in Sanskrit phonetic classification.

From:

**अनु - anu**

Following.

**प्रदान - pradāna**

Giving, contribution.

Now the term doesn't have one simple modern English equivalent covering every traditional phonetic context.

The edition's bracketed gloss explains it with reference to breath and resonance following the initial articulation.

That's useful, but it's an interpretive phonetic explanation.

The important thing is that Abhinavagupta includes the relevant distinctions among the properties that make articulated speech differentiated.

### The philosophical argument

Now, why is he discussing all this?

Because these physical differences appear in Vaikharī.

But he wants to explain how the capacity for meaningful differentiated articulation is grounded in the more comprehensive powers of consciousness.

The gross phonetic production is not ultimately separate from the inner expressive power.

And if the inner domain were completely devoid of differentiated capacities, it would be difficult to explain the coherent relation between internal apprehension and outwardly differentiated speech.

So he argues for corresponding inner powers.

Not necessarily literal, already-moving physical lips inside the mind.

But the conscious capacities by which those distinctions become meaningful and manifest.

### The spectacular editorial anachronism

Now here's something very important philologically.

The Kashmir Series places an exceptionally long square-bracketed explanation here, including a reference to the **Siddhāntakaumudī**.

That is decisive evidence that this explanatory passage is not Abhinavagupta's own eleventh-century prose.

Why?

Because the Siddhāntakaumudī was composed by Bhaṭṭoji Dīkṣita in the late sixteenth or early seventeenth century-centuries after Abhinavagupta.

A seventeenth-century grammar cannot have been quoted by Abhinavagupta himself. ; ; \]

Now that is exactly why distinguishing editorial brackets matters.

If we simply flatten the entire transcription into one continuous "Abhinavagupta said..." narrative, we introduce historical impossibilities.

The editor is supplying later grammatical explanation.

Abhinavagupta's own argument is earlier.

And the two must remain distinct.

This is not pedantry.

It's the difference between reading an historical source responsibly and accidentally making an eleventh-century philosopher cite a book that won't be written for another six hundred years.

My brother in Bhairava.

Even divine freedom does not justify making manuscripts time-travel without evidence.


## 17. The hidden instruments - Why an inner sentence isn't a silent physical recording

Now we move to Kashmir Series p. 186.

And Abhinavagupta concludes that the relevant instruments must also have an inner or concealed capacity.

The key expression is:

**अन्तर्हितकरणशक्तयः**

*Antarhitakaraṇaśaktayaḥ.*

The concealed or inwardly present powers of the instruments.

Now let's dismantle that.

### Antarhita - अन्तर्हित

Hidden, concealed, placed within.

Related to:

**अन्तर् - antar**

Inside.

And:

**हित - hita**

Placed, established, deposited.

From √**धा - dhā**, to place, in the relevant past-participial formation.

So:

**अन्तर्हित - antarhita**

Hidden within.

### Karaṇa - करण

Instrument, means of action or cognition.

We've already learned this.

### Śakti - शक्ति

Power.

Now the feminine nominative plural:

**शक्तयः - śaktayaḥ**.

Thus:

**अन्तर्हितकरणशक्तयः - antarhitakaraṇaśaktayaḥ**

The hidden or inward powers of the relevant instruments.

Now here's the point.

When you internally formulate a sentence, you don't need to produce every part of that sentence as an audible sound wave.

Yet its phonemic distinctions can be apprehended.

And when you recall or imagine something, the differentiated content can be present without the original external object being immediately available.

So Abhinavagupta argues that the underlying capacities for those distinctions exist within conscious manifestation.

But here's where we must be careful.

**This does not establish that inner speech is literally the same physical event as silent movement of the tongue, throat and lips.**

Nor does the text provide a modern account of motor imagery or neural speech planning.

It's a metaphysical argument about the relationship between the powers of articulation and the levels of speech.

The physical act is one manifestation.

The inner power is more comprehensive.

And the distinction between the two doesn't require their ultimate separation.

### The beautiful examples

Now Abhinavagupta offers a series of first-person expressions.

**शृणोमि - śṛṇomi**

I hear.

**अश्रौषम् - aśrauṣam**

I heard.

**पश्यामि - paśyāmi**

I see.

**अद्राक्षम् - adrākṣam**

I saw.

**सङ्कल्पयामि - saṅkalpayāmi**

I form an intention or conception.

**समकल्पयम् - samakalpayam**

I formed an intention or conception.

Now these are brilliant examples.

Because the same conscious subject can apprehend distinctions of tense, modality and content.

And those distinctions can be internally formulated.

Let's study the verbs.

### Śṛṇomi - शृणोमि

From √**श्रु - śru**, to hear.

The present first-person singular:

**शृणोमि - śṛṇomi**.

I hear.

Now the past form:

**अश्रौषम् - aśrauṣam**.

I heard.

The relation is not a simple addition of a universal past-tense suffix to the present stem.

Sanskrit verbal morphology can involve different stems and formations for different tenses.

So it's worth memorizing the attested pair as given.

### Paśyāmi - पश्यामि

From √**पश् - paś**, to see.

First-person singular present.

And:

**अद्राक्षम् - adrākṣam**.

I saw.

Again, a distinct past formation.

Now notice the internal consonant cluster:

**क्ष = क् + ष**.

It's everywhere.

The Sanskrit language cannot go three paragraphs without producing another *kṣa*.

### Saṅkalpayāmi - सङ्कल्पयामि

I conceive, intend, formulate.

From:

**सम् - sam**

Together.

**√कॢप् - kḷp**

To arrange, be suitable, form.

The causative-derived verbal formation:

**सङ्कल्पयति - saṅkalpayati**

He or she forms an intention.

First person:

**सङ्कल्पयामि - saṅkalpayāmi**.

Now the past:

**समकल्पयम् - samakalpayam**.

I formed an intention.

### The philosophical implication

Now imagine internally saying:

"I hear."

Then:

"I heard."

The external sound doesn't have to be presently occurring for you to apprehend the distinction.

The difference between present and past can be manifested through inner conceptual speech.

Likewise:

"I see."

"I saw."

And:

"I intend."

"I intended."

The differentiated grammatical structure belongs to the conscious apprehension of meaning.

Now Abhinavagupta's point is that the variety of such *saṅkalpas* would be inexplicable if the inward capacity for differentiated speech were wholly absent.

The experience of the distinction is meaningful.

And that meaningfulness belongs to the broader power of consciousness.

### The difference between a thought and a sound recording

An internal sentence is not simply an audio file being played inside your skull.

It can include meaning, intention, remembered sound-form and conceptual differentiation.

The outward acoustic form is one possible expression.

But meaningful inner speech isn't reducible to the immediate production of external sound waves.

And this is why Abhinavagupta insists on the relation between Parā Vāk and Vaikharī.

**The outer articulation is distinguishable, but its intelligible reality depends upon a more comprehensive conscious expressive power.**

Now we've reached the final instruction of this passage.

And it's a beautiful one.

Because after all that Sanskrit grammar, Tantric geometry and philosophical argument, Abhinavagupta tells the reader to examine consciousness more deeply.

Not merely to memorize the diagrams.

Not merely to classify the phonemes.

But to **enter inwardly, more and more**, and recognize the power through which all those distinctions are manifest.

This is the genuine philosophical climax of Part XVI.


## 18. अन्तर्धिकमधिकम् - Enter inward, further and further

Now comes Abhinavagupta's concluding instruction on p. 186.

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · KASHMIR SERIES P. 186

**तदनया युक्त्या निभालितयान्तरधिकमधिकमनुप्रविश्य परिशीलयतां संविदम्।**

*Tad anayā yuktyā nibhālitayāntar adhikam adhikam anupraviśya pariśīlayatāṃ saṃvidam.*

With this reasoning having been examined, enter further and further inward and thoroughly contemplate consciousness.

Now that's extraordinary.

Because Abhinavagupta has used an enormous amount of conceptual reasoning.

But he hasn't been doing it merely to manufacture more concepts.

He's using the analysis to direct attention toward the reality in which the distinctions arise.

Let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Yukti - युक्ति

Reasoning, rational argument, appropriate method.

From the √**युज् - yuj** family, to join or connect.

The instrumental singular:

**युक्त्या - yuktyā**

Through reasoning.

Now:

**अनया - anayā**

By this, through this.

Feminine instrumental singular of the demonstrative *idam*.

Thus:

**अनया युक्त्या - anayā yuktyā**

Through this reasoning.

Notice the agreement.

Both forms are feminine instrumental singular.

### Nibhālitā - निभालिता

Examined, observed, looked into.

The expression:

**निभालितया - nibhālitayā**

Through what has been examined.

The participial form is connected with an act of looking or examining.

Now:

### Antar - अन्तर्

Within, inward.

### Adhikam adhikam - अधिकमधिकम्

More and more.

From:

**अधिक - adhika**

Greater, additional, more.

Repeated adverbially:

**अधिकम् अधिकम् - adhikam adhikam**

Further and further.

Now this repetition matters.

Abhinavagupta isn't directing the reader toward a single fixed external object.

He's directing an increasingly subtle inquiry.

The grosser forms of expression.

The inner articulations.

The differentiated contents.

The reflexive power through which those contents are apprehended.

And ultimately the all-containing conscious ground.

### Anupraviśya - अनुप्रविश्य

Having entered, entering into.

From:

**अनु - anu**

Along, following.

**प्र - pra**

Forth.

**√विश् - viś**

To enter.

The absolutive:

**अनुप्रविश्य - anupraviśya**

Having entered.

Now the structure is important.

The absolutive establishes a relation to the following instruction.

Enter inwardly.

Then contemplate or cultivate the awareness being indicated.

### Pariśīlayatām - परिशीलयताम्

Let one thoroughly cultivate, examine or contemplate.

From:

**परि - pari**

Completely, all around.

**√शील् - śīl**

To practise, cultivate, become accustomed to.

The verbal form functions as an imperative instruction.

And:

### Saṃvidam - संविदम्

Consciousness.

From:

**संविद् - saṃvid**

Consciousness, awareness.

Accusative singular:

**संविदम् - saṃvidam**.

Now the full instruction makes sense.

Through the reasoning we've just examined, enter further inward and cultivate recognition of consciousness.

**The purpose of the analysis is direct understanding of the reality that makes the analysis possible.**

That's the culmination.

The grammar is serving philosophy.

The philosophy is serving recognition.

And the Tantric symbolism is serving the same end.


## 19. The great Mahāmantra - The letters themselves are divine powers

Now Abhinavagupta describes the reality into which one is to enter.

The Sanskrit contains the immense compound:

**बोधैकघनविमर्शात्मकस्वातन्त्र्यसारमहामन्त्ररूपवर्णभट्टारकनिवेशः**

*Bodhaikaghana-vimarśātmaka-svātantryasāra-mahāmantrarūpa-varṇabhaṭṭāraka-niveśaḥ.*

The establishment of the venerable phonemic power whose nature is the Great Mantra, whose essence is sovereign freedom constituted by the compact fullness of consciousness and reflexive awareness.

Now that is a spectacular compound.

But it is not impossible once separated.

### Bodha - बोध

Awareness, knowledge, cognition.

From √**बुध् - budh**, to awaken or know.

### Ekaghana - एकघन

One compact fullness.

**एक - eka**

One.

**घन - ghana**

Compact, dense, massed.

Together:

**एकघन - ekaghana**

One undivided fullness.

Now:

**बोधैकघन - bodhaikaghana**

One compact fullness of awareness.

### Vimarśātmaka - विमर्शात्मक

Having the nature of reflexive awareness.

**विमर्श - vimarśa**

Reflexive apprehension.

**आत्मक - ātmaka**

Having the nature of.

Thus:

**विमर्शात्मक - vimarśātmaka**.

### Svātantryasāra - स्वातन्त्र्यसार

Having sovereign freedom as its essence.

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Freedom.

**सार - sāra**

Essence.

### Mahāmantrarūpa - महामन्त्ररूप

Having the form of the Great Mantra.

**महा - mahā**

Great.

**मन्त्र - mantra**

Mantra.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form.

Now the essential point.

The Great Mantra isn't merely an arbitrary string of letters considered outside consciousness.

Its reality is the sovereign self-revealing power through which meaningful manifestation is possible.

And:

### Varṇabhaṭṭāraka - वर्णभट्टारक

The venerable or exalted phonemic Lord or power.

**वर्ण - varṇa**

Letter, phoneme.

**भट्टारक - bhaṭṭāraka**

Venerable Lord.

Now don't misread this as merely claiming the written glyph itself is a physically omnipotent object.

The phrase belongs to a metaphysical account of the **divine reality of phonemic manifestation**.

The sacred phonemes represent powers of consciousness.

Their forms can become differentiated in spoken language.

But their supreme nature remains grounded in the self-revealing freedom of Parā.

### The astonishing conclusion

The triangle is one representation.

The vowel-consonant system another.

The thirty-six-tattva scheme another.

The levels of speech another.

But the conscious ground they're intended to disclose remains one.

And that's why Abhinavagupta's instruction is not simply:

"Memorize fifty letters and congratulations, you're liberated."

It's:

**Examine the conscious reality through which the differentiated forms are apprehended.**

That is the underlying direction of the commentary.

And now we can understand why the triangle matters so much.

It reveals the structure of ordinary experience.

Object.

Apprehension.

Subject.

But the three are not independent ultimate realities.

Their manifestation belongs to one consciousness.

And recognition of that ground is the philosophical heart of the teaching.

## 22. Where we stop in the original commentary

We've reached the end of the inward instruction on Kashmir Series p. 186.

The next movement begins on p. 187 with a serious philosophical objection.

If consciousness is one homogeneous fullness, how can there be distinguishable places of articulation, different instruments and particular phonemes?

The Sanskrit question begins:

**इदं स्थानमिदं करणमयं वर्ण इति कथङ्कारं विभागः?**

*Idaṃ sthānam idaṃ karaṇam ayaṃ varṇa iti kathaṅkāraṃ vibhāgaḥ?*

"This is a place, this is an instrument, this is a phoneme"-how is such differentiation possible?

That is the exact next issue.

And it's a wonderful question.

Because Abhinavagupta has just explained that the gross articulation of speech remains grounded in Parā Vāk.

But if Parā is one undivided consciousness, how can it manifest all these different letters?

How can the one become many?

And how can the many remain one?

That is the central philosophical problem of the entire work.

Part XVII will examine his response through the freedom of manifestation, the differentiation of knowledge, the relation of the object to awareness and the great variety of speech.


## 23. The final insight - The triangle is already present whenever anything is known

Think about something ordinary.

You look at a pot.

There is a pot.

There is the seeing.

And there is the one who sees.

Three distinguishable aspects.

Now Abhinavagupta asks:

What is the reality in which this entire relation becomes manifest?

The object is known.

The act of knowing is manifest.

And the subject is aware.

But their ultimate ground is not three independent substances somehow welded together after the fact.

It's consciousness.

And the triangle expresses the differentiation of that one reality.

Now the extraordinary thing is that he uses exactly the same structure to understand divine manifestation.

The seed.

The matrix.

The generative union.

The Moon, Sun and Fire.

Creation, maintenance and withdrawal.

The three subtle channels.

All are distinguishable structures of manifestation.

But none establishes an independent second Absolute.

And the ritual conclusion is that the Śiva-Śakti union is worshipped through one's own Self.

Not merely as an external image.

Not merely as a symbol to be memorized.

But as a means of recognizing the conscious ground underlying every differentiated relation.

**The Goddess isn't simply one corner of the triangle. She is the power through which the entire triangle becomes manifest.**

And that is why the threefold structure doesn't imprison her.

It reveals her expressive freedom.

The whole passage has been leading us toward one question:

**What is the consciousness through which the knower, knowing and known are all manifest?**

And now Abhinavagupta tells us to investigate that directly.

Not to stop at the diagram.

Not to stop at the terminology.

Not to stop at the intellectual classification.

But to enter inwardly and recognize the conscious reality through which the entire structure appears.

That's the heart of Part XVI.

And now, having explained why the one consciousness manifests as three, he's about to explain why it can manifest as fifty letters, thirty-six tattvas and the entire differentiated universe without ceasing to be one.

The triangle has opened.

And the Goddess is still not finished.
