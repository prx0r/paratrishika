# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART VIII - Every Tattva Contains the Universe

*Abhinavagupta discovers that the Earth contains all thirty-six principles of reality, makes a tree testify in a metaphysical court case, argues that the future is already present in consciousness, and finally explains why the universe can look completely backwards in a mirror without Śiva losing his bloody mind.*

Continuing the commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series edition, pp. 139-151


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

We've been studying one of the greatest philosophical problems in the entire Trika tradition.

The Sanskrit alphabet is supposed to express the powers of consciousness.

The *tattvas* are supposed to explain the order of manifestation.

And different Śaiva scriptures assign the letters to different tattvas.

One scripture says Earth corresponds to **क - ka**.

Another says **क्ष - kṣa**.

Mālinī turns up and says **फ - pha**.

The poor Earth tattva has been relocated three times and is considering filing a complaint with the metaphysical housing authority.

But Abhinavagupta has refused two easy answers.

First: "The correspondences are completely arbitrary."

Second: "Śiva is free, mate. Stop asking questions."

Neither is sufficient.

Instead, he develops a much deeper account.

**Every principle of manifestation depends upon the entire reality of which it is an expression.**

The Earth isn't ultimately independent of Water, the subtle elements, the senses, the limited subject, Māyā, Śakti or Śiva.

And yet the Earth remains distinguishable from Water within manifestation.

So how do difference and non-difference coexist?

How can something possess a particular nature while depending upon something more comprehensive?

And why should the answer explain the apparently reversed alphabetic arrangements?

That's where we resume.

We're at Kashmir Series p. 139, where Abhinavagupta invokes the *Spanda* scripture, proceeds through a remarkable example involving trees, and develops a causal argument that leads to a surprisingly radical account of time, reflection and sacred language.

I've checked the Devanāgarī transcription against the parallel *Spandakārikā* text. One important distinction: the online Kashmir Series transcription includes bracketed explanatory annotations, which are not always Abhinavagupta's own words. I'll keep the principal commentary separate from those notes, and quote only short, securely attested Sanskrit passages. ; \]

Right.

Let's find out why a lump of Earth apparently contains the entire universe.


## 1. The entire cosmos is present in each tattva

Abhinavagupta has just argued that the Earth principle, though apparently the final and grossest member of the familiar tattva sequence, cannot exist independently of the conditions constituting its nature.

Now he invokes the scriptural teaching that **each individual tattva possesses the nature of all thirty-six tattvas**.

Let's unpack that before we turn it into mystical nonsense.

### The Sanskrit: Sarvasarvātmakatva

**सर्वसर्वात्मकत्व - sarvasarvātmakatva**

An absolutely magnificent philosophical compound.

Break it into its meaningful elements:

- **सर्व - sarva:** all, everything.
- **सर्व - sarva:** repeated to express the comprehensive relation.
- **आत्मक - ātmaka:** having the nature of, constituted by.
- **त्व - tva:** the condition of being something.

Thus *sarvasarvātmakatva* expresses the doctrine of **everything having the nature of everything**, understood through the universal ground of manifestation.

Now here's where we must be precise.

Abhinavagupta isn't claiming that an ordinary pebble literally contains a miniature elephant, a miniature Himalayan mountain, six galaxies and an extremely small man operating a teashop.

Nor is he establishing a modern physical theory that every atom contains the whole universe.

He is making a claim about **ontological dependence and the inseparability of manifested principles**.

Earth cannot manifest independently of the higher principles upon which its existence depends.

And those higher principles ultimately depend upon universal consciousness.

Thus an individual tattva, fully understood, implicates the whole order.

### The grammar of ātmaka

**आत्मक - ātmaka**

Having the nature of.

You'll encounter this everywhere.

**शिवात्मक - śivātmaka**

Having the nature of Śiva.

**चिदात्मक - cidātmaka**

Having the nature of consciousness.

**सर्वात्मक - sarvātmaka**

Having the nature of all.

Notice the sandhi:

**sarva + ātmaka → sarvātmaka**

Because **a + ā → ā**.

And when the abstract suffix *-tva* is added:

**सर्वात्मकत्व - sarvātmakatva**

The condition of having an all-inclusive nature.

This suffix *-tva* is becoming one of our great Sanskrit friends.

You've seen it in:

**शिवत्व - śivatva**

The condition of being Śiva.

**कर्तृत्व - kartṛtva**

Agency, doership.

And now:

**सर्वसर्वात्मकत्व - sarvasarvātmakatva**

The doctrine that the entire manifestation is implicated in each principle.

### Why this isn't just saying “everything is one”

Imagine a world made of thirty-six completely independent objects.

Each could exist without any relation to any other.

Earth would be Earth all by itself.

Water would be Water all by itself.

Mind would be Mind all by itself.

And consciousness would be another isolated item in the collection.

But that's not the Trika ontology.

The tattvas form an ordered account of manifestation.

They stand in relations of dependence.

They express different functions.

Their differentiation is real within the manifestation, but not metaphysically independent.

And now Abhinavagupta produces a Spanda verse to make the point.

## 2. The Spanda teaching - See everything, understand it for yourself

SPANDAKĀRIKĀ 3.11, QUOTED BY ABHINAVAGUPTA

## दिदृक्षयेव सर्वार्थान्यदा व्याप्यावतिष्ठते।

## तदा किं बहुनोक्तेन स्वयमेवावभोत्स्यते॥

*Didṛkṣayeva sarvārthān yadā vyāpyāvatiṣṭhate.*

*Tadā kiṃ bahunoktena svayam evāvabhotsyate.*

A close translation:

When one remains established, pervading all objects as though wishing to see them, what need is there to say much? One will understand directly for oneself.

This is a verse of the *Spandakārikā*, cited here by Abhinavagupta. Its numbering is 3.11 in the sectioned arrangement and 11 in some continuous presentations of that chapter. ; \]

Now that is an extraordinary instruction.

But don't immediately reduce it to:

"Just look at everything, bro."

The important issue is the nature of the pervading awareness.

### Didṛkṣā - दिदृक्षा

Let's start with the first word.

**दिदृक्षा - didṛkṣā**

The wish or desire to see.

From √**दृश् - dṛś**, to see, through a desiderative formation.

A desiderative expresses wanting to perform the action of the root.

Compare the idea:

*Dṛś* - to see.

*Didṛkṣā* - the wish to see.

The Sanskrit root undergoes morphological changes, including reduplication and the desiderative element.

Now look at the original:

**दिदृक्षयेव - didṛkṣayeva**

This conceals a lovely sandhi.

**didṛkṣayā + iva → didṛkṣayeva**

The final long *ā* combines with the following *i*:

**ā + i → e**

Thus:

*didṛkṣayeva.*

Notice the Devanāgarī conjunct:

**क्ष = क् + ष**

You've encountered this repeatedly.

And now it appears inside the desiderative formation of a verb meaning "to see."

The philosophy and the actual Sanskrit writing are finally getting to know one another.

### Sarvārthān - सर्वार्थान्

**सर्व - sarva**

All.

**अर्थ - artha**

Object, meaning, purpose, thing, referent.

The compound:

**सर्वार्थ - sarvārtha**

All objects, all things or all meanings, depending on context.

Here the plural accusative:

**सर्वार्थान् - sarvārthān**

All objects.

Now why is the ending *-ān*?

Because we're dealing with a masculine *-a* stem in the accusative plural.

Compare:

**अर्थः - arthaḥ**

An object.

**अर्थम् - artham**

An object, accusative singular.

**अर्थान् - arthān**

Objects, accusative plural.

And in the compound:

*sarvārthān.*

The grammar tells us the range of what is pervaded or encompassed.

### Vyāpya - व्याप्य

**व्याप्य - vyāpya**

Having pervaded, after encompassing.

It is a verbal absolutive form related to *vi-* and √*āp*, to reach, obtain or pervade.

The absolutive indicates an action associated with the main verb.

Now:

**अवतिष्ठते - avatiṣṭhate**

Remains established, abides.

From *ava-* and √*sthā*, to stand or remain.

Third-person singular, present middle voice.

And the original continuous form:

**व्याप्यावतिष्ठते - vyāpyāvatiṣṭhate**

Comes from:

*vyāpya + avatiṣṭhate*.

Again:

**a + a → ā**

So:

*vyāpyāvatiṣṭhate.*

Now notice what the sentence is doing.

One is established while comprehending or pervading the entirety of the object-field.

The specific relation between seeing and the whole field of manifestation is what matters.

Not merely the physical widening of the eyes.

### Kiṃ bahunoktena

And this is my favourite phrase.

**किं बहुनोक्तेन - kiṃ bahunoktena**

What use is much being said?

Break it down.

**किम् - kim**

What?

**बहु - bahu**

Much, many.

**उक्त - ukta**

Said, spoken.

From √*vac*, to speak, in a past participial form with the familiar change to *ukta*.

Now the instrumental singular:

**उक्तेन - uktena**

By what has been said.

And:

**बहुना उक्तेन → बहुनोक्तेन**

*bahunā uktena → bahunoktena*

Because:

**ā + u → o**

So the phrase means:

What need is there for much explanation?

And the verse ends:

**स्वयमेवावभोत्स्यते - svayam evāvabhotsyate**

One will understand for oneself.

The verb *avabhotsyate* is a future middle form connected with *ava-* and √*budh*, to understand or awaken.

And now we have a very beautiful philosophical movement.

**The teaching points toward direct recognition of the whole, rather than endlessly accumulating separate descriptions of its parts.**

Imagine reading a seven-hundred-page philosophical explanation of what a mango tastes like.

You understand the vocabulary.

The chemistry.

The botanical classification.

The culinary applications.

You can identify its Sanskrit names.

But you've never eaten one.

At some point, somebody hands you a mango.

You taste it.

And suddenly the long explanation is related to a direct experience.

The analogy isn't exact: the Spanda verse concerns the nature of conscious manifestation rather than sensory knowledge of fruit.

But it captures the contrast between discursive explanation and immediate apprehension.

Abhinavagupta isn't dismissing his own commentary.

He's showing what the commentary is ultimately directed toward.

And naturally, having quoted a verse asking what need there is for many words, he proceeds to explain everything at considerable length.

Magnificent.


## 3. The tree takes the witness stand - Śiṃśapā versus Vṛkṣa

Now comes an argument so philosophically useful that you should remember it permanently.

Abhinavagupta asks:

How can one thing be described as the nature of another?

Doesn't that violate their distinct identities?

He answers using a tree.

Specifically:

**शिंशपा - śiṃśapā**

A particular kind of tree, traditionally identified with the North Indian *śiśam* or Indian rosewood.

And:

**वृक्ष - vṛkṣa**

Tree.

Now the distinction is beautifully simple.

Every *śiṃśapā* is a tree.

But not every tree is a *śiṃśapā*.

A banyan is a tree.

A mango tree is a tree.

A *śiṃśapā* is a tree.

But the generic property of being a tree does not require possessing the particular identity of *śiṃśapā*.

This is the entire logical structure of the example.

NECESSARY RELATION

**Śiṃśapā → Tree**

The specific kind implies the more general kind.

NOT NECESSARILY REVERSIBLE

**Tree ↛ Śiṃśapā**

Something can be a tree without being that particular kind.

Now, I know what you're thinking.

We were investigating supreme consciousness, and Abhinavagupta has decided to explain basic category inclusion.

Yes.

And that's precisely why the argument is so powerful.

He's using an ordinary logical asymmetry to explain how one principle can depend upon another without their being identical in every respect.

### The Sanskrit: Śiṃśapātva

**शिंशपात्व - śiṃśapātva**

The condition of being a *śiṃśapā*.

Break it down:

*śiṃśapā + tva*.

The abstract suffix *-tva* expresses the quality or state.

And:

**वृक्षत्व - vṛkṣatva**

The condition of being a tree.

Now the relation:

*Śiṃśapātva* implies *vṛkṣatva*.

But *vṛkṣatva* does not imply *śiṃśapātva*.

Abhinavagupta uses this to illustrate a relation of dependence.

The specific cannot exist without the more general condition that makes it what it is.

But the more general condition need not depend on that one specific manifestation.

### The crucial distinction: “Without which it cannot exist”

He introduces the idea that something without which another cannot exist belongs to the latter's nature.

Now, this is not a universal permission to identify every necessary circumstance with the essence of something.

The argument is operating within a particular account of ontological dependence.

And we must distinguish the level of analysis.

A *śiṃśapā* requires being a tree.

That is part of what it is.

But being a tree does not require being a *śiṃśapā*.

The relation is asymmetric.

And this becomes extremely important for the tattvas.

Because Earth depends upon higher principles.

But those higher principles are not necessarily dependent upon Earth in the same manner.

### The grammar of vṛkṣa

**वृक्ष - vṛkṣa**

Tree.

Look at the syllable:

**वृ = व + ृ**

The vocalic ṛ sign is attached to व.

Then:

**क्ष = क् + ष**

So the full written word is:

**वृ + क्ष**

*vṛkṣa.*

Now compare:

**वृक्षत्वम् - vṛkṣatvam**

Treehood.

And:

**शिंशपात्वम् - śiṃśapātvam**

Being a śiṃśapā.

These are two abstract nouns with the same *-tva* formation.

The Sanskrit is doing exactly what the philosophy needs.

It distinguishes a specific nature from a more comprehensive one.

And Abhinavagupta now applies the same logic to the entire cosmology.

**If Earth requires the higher principles to be what it is, then Earth's reality cannot be understood independently of them.**

Which means the Earth tattva has a much larger metaphysical family than you first suspected.

And the family reunion is about to become completely unreasonable.




## 4. Earth cannot exist without Water - The causal argument begins

Now Abhinavagupta applies the tree example to the tattva sequence.

He argues that Earth does not exist independently of Water.

And Water, in turn, does not exist independently of the principles preceding it in the ontological explanation.

Now before someone throws a geology textbook at Abhinavagupta, let's understand what sort of claim he's making.

He is **not** giving a modern physical account of why terrestrial solids exist.

He's reasoning within the traditional Śaiva system of elements and subtle principles.

The important term is:

**पृथिवी - pṛthivī**

Earth.

And:

**अप् - ap**

Water.

The common inflected forms of the water noun include **आपः - āpaḥ**, waters.

The Earth and Water tattvas are philosophical categories whose significance is broader than ordinary chemical substances.

Within that system, the grossest principle has characteristics dependent upon subtler forms of manifestation.

### Why does he mention hardness?

The passage discusses:

**काठिन्य - kāṭhinya**

Hardness, rigidity, solidity.

From:

**कठिन - kaṭhina**

Hard, rigid, difficult.

With an abstract nominal formation.

Now look at the consonants:

**ठ - ṭha**

Retroflex aspirate.

**थ - tha**

Dental aspirate.

They are not interchangeable.

In *kāṭhinya*, the consonant is **ठ - ṭha**.

The word has the long initial ā:

**काठिन्य - kāṭhinya**

And the ending expresses the state of being hard.

Abhinavagupta relates the firmness and cohesion of Earth to the water principle within his inherited elemental framework.

The deeper reasoning is that a gross manifestation depends upon the subtler conditions allowing it to possess its particular character.

Now here's the crucial asymmetry.

Earth requires certain prior principles.

But the prior principles needn't be reducible to Earth.

Exactly like the tree example.

A śiṃśapā requires treehood.

Treehood doesn't require being a śiṃśapā.

Likewise, the manifested Earth principle requires its ontological conditions.

But the more comprehensive principles aren't simply identical to the gross Earth element.

### The Sanskrit: Upādāna-kāraṇa

**उपादानकारण - upādānakāraṇa**

Material cause.

Let's dismantle this properly.

**उपादान - upādāna**

Taking up, appropriation, material constituent or material cause in certain philosophical contexts.

**कारण - kāraṇa**

Cause, instrument, explanatory condition.

Together:

*upādāna + kāraṇa → upādānakāraṇa*

The material cause.

A traditional example is clay and a clay pot.

The pot depends upon clay as the material from which it is formed.

But a potter's wheel is an instrument.

And the potter's activity functions as another kind of cause.

This is why Indian philosophical systems distinguish different causal relationships.

The Sanskrit:

**निमित्तकारण - nimittakāraṇa**

An instrumental or efficient cause.

*Nimitta* means cause, occasion, instrument or sign, depending on context.

Now, here's what Abhinavagupta is arguing.

A produced effect cannot be understood as completely independent of the material reality necessary for its existence.

The effect is not an ontologically isolated object that can simply discard its causal basis while retaining the same nature.

In this context, he is emphasizing the continuity between effect and underlying cause.

### The philosopher's pot factory

Imagine a potter.

He takes clay.

He makes a pot.

Then somebody announces:

"The pot is now a completely independent reality, and clay has absolutely nothing to do with it."

Well, that's rather silly.

The pot's particular shape is new.

Its function as a container may be new.

But its material constitution hasn't become magically unrelated to clay.

Similarly, Abhinavagupta argues that the differentiated tattvas do not cease depending upon their underlying principles merely because they acquire distinct characteristics.

But again, the metaphysical system is more elaborate than a straightforward physical-material example.

Because the causal chain eventually reaches **consciousness itself**.

And consciousness is not being treated as one more ordinary material ingredient.

That's the decisive point.

### A warning about what this proves

Abhinavagupta is not demonstrating through laboratory chemistry that water is the physical ingredient responsible for every instance of hardness.

His argument is internal to a Śaiva metaphysical account.

Its philosophical force concerns the relation between dependent manifestation and underlying reality.

Within that account, the gross principle cannot be isolated from the subtler principles that constitute its possibility.

And now he begins climbing through the entire tattva structure.

Not as a yogin physically travelling up his spine.

But as a philosopher tracing what must already be implicated in an apparently simple object.


## 5. A lump of Earth drags the whole universe into court

Now the argument expands.

And this is one of the most satisfying movements in the commentary.

Abhinavagupta essentially asks:

What must be present for an apparently independent Earth-object to manifest?

Then he follows the chain.

**भूत - Bhūta**

The gross elements, including Earth, depend upon the subtler principles that allow their sensory qualities to manifest.

**तन्मात्र - Tanmātra**

The subtle sensory qualities depend upon the faculties and structures through which differentiated experience occurs.

**इन्द्रिय - Indriya**

The senses depend upon their coordinating and determining cognitive principles.

**बुद्धि / अहङ्कार / मनस्**

Intellect, ego-making function and mind belong to a broader manifestation grounded in Prakṛti.

**प्रकृति / पुरुष**

The object-field presupposes the limited experiencer and its relation to what is experienced.

**माया / कञ्चुक**

Limited experience depends upon the divine power through which universal consciousness manifests contraction.

**शिव / शक्ति**

All differentiated principles ultimately depend upon the inseparable reality of consciousness and its freedom.

This is a condensed dependency diagram of the argument, not an independently quoted verse or a claim that Abhinavagupta lists these exact boxes.

Now the important thing is that Abhinavagupta doesn't simply enumerate the categories.

He argues for their mutual implication.

### Tanmātra - तन्मात्र

Start with:

**तत् - tat**

That.

**मात्र - mātra**

Mere, only, measure.

Thus:

**तन्मात्र - tanmātra**

Literally, something like "that alone" or "mere that," used technically for a subtle sensory principle.

Now look at the consonant sequence.

**तन् = त + न्**

And then:

**मात्र - mātra**

The compound joins through the nasal consonant.

This isn't the same as *tatmātra*, because the form reflects the traditional combination and the noun's established shape.

Now the five tanmātras:

**शब्द - śabda**: sound.

**स्पर्श - sparśa**: touch.

**रूप - rūpa**: form or colour.

**रस - rasa**: taste.

**गन्ध - gandha**: smell.

These are subtler principles underlying the gross elements.

But Abhinavagupta's argument goes further.

Even these subtle categories are not independent of cognition.

### Indriya - इन्द्रिय

A sense faculty or organ.

The word is traditionally related to *Indra* and the notion of power, though its established philosophical meaning is what matters here.

The senses don't simply float outside the subject-object structure.

Their functioning belongs to the larger apparatus of experience.

The sense of smell implies a capacity to apprehend smell.

Visual form implies the conditions through which visual cognition becomes manifest.

Now, don't confuse the epistemological structure with a claim that a physical object can only exist if one particular person happens to look at it.

Trika's universal consciousness is not identical with any one empirical observer.

Rather, the manifested object-world and the structures of knowing ultimately depend upon universal consciousness.

### Adhyavasāya - अध्यवसाय

Now here's a beautiful technical word.

**अध्यवसाय - adhyavasāya**

Determination, ascertainment, decisive cognition.

Abhinavagupta says that the senses depend upon the relevant determinative cognition.

In Sāṃkhya and related systems, *buddhi* is especially associated with determination.

Now imagine seeing something in poor light.

There's a shape.

Maybe a tree.

Maybe a person.

Then you look more closely.

You determine:

"That's a tree."

The cognitive operation involves moving from uncertain presentation toward definite ascertainment.

But Abhinavagupta's metaphysical point is not simply that the brain classifies visual inputs.

It is that the structures enabling determinate experience belong to the larger tattva-order.

### Prakṛti - प्रकृति

Now:

**प्रकृति - prakṛti**

Primordial nature, underlying productive principle.

The word is formed from *pra-* and √*kṛ*, to make or do, with the relevant nominal formation.

In Sāṃkhya-influenced cosmology, Prakṛti is the source of the differentiated mental and material principles.

But Abhinavagupta now asks:

How can Prakṛti be understood as the experienced object-field without a corresponding experiencer?

The Sanskrit distinction is:

**भोक्तृ - bhoktṛ**

Experiencer, enjoyer.

And:

**भोग्य - bhogya**

What is to be experienced or enjoyed.

Now learn these together.

They come from √**bhuj**, to enjoy, partake, consume or experience.

*Bhoktṛ* names the experiencer.

*Bhogya* names what is available to experience.

The ending **-tṛ** often forms agent nouns.

The ending **-ya** can form a gerundive, something to be acted upon.

So:

**भोक्तृ - bhoktṛ**

The one who experiences.

**भोग्य - bhogya**

What is experienced.

Now here's the philosophical move.

A field of experience presupposes the relational structure of experiencer and experienced.

And the finite experiencer is **Puruṣa**.

But even Puruṣa is limited.

So we must investigate the powers responsible for that limitation.

### The malas and the bonds

Abhinavagupta now invokes the structures of contraction.

**सङ्कोच - saṃkoca**

Contraction, restriction.

From *saṃ-* and √*kuc*, to contract or shrink.

We've encountered this word repeatedly.

And:

**पाश - pāśa**

Bond, fetter, noose.

The limited subject experiences itself through a network of restrictions.

Its knowledge is restricted.

Its agency is restricted.

Its experience is temporally situated.

Its capacities are not universal.

The commentary specifically mentions the limiting powers associated with **Kāla** and **Kalā**, among others.

Don't confuse them.

**काल - kāla**

Time.

**कला - kalā**

Portion, aspect, or limited capacity.

They look similar in Roman letters without diacritics, but they're distinct words with different technical functions.

Now the important thing.

The limited subject doesn't arise from an independently existing force capable of overpowering Śiva.

The contraction itself must be explained through the freedom of consciousness.

Which means we now reach:

**माया - māyā**

The power associated with differentiation.

And finally:

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

The universal freedom through which even limitation can become manifest.

The conclusion is astonishing.

**Earth depends upon the entire ladder, and the ladder ultimately depends upon the freedom of consciousness.**

So a complete understanding of Earth implicates every higher principle.

Abhinavagupta has, quite literally, followed a lump of dirt all the way back to Bhairava.

And the poor lump of dirt has turned out to be a rather serious metaphysical object.


## 6. But couldn't the universe be arranged backwards?

Now Abhinavagupta anticipates the objection.

If Water and the higher principles are present as conditions of Earth, why not say Earth is present in Water in the same way?

Wouldn't that reverse the order?

And if so, doesn't the entire hierarchy become unstable?

Now here's where the argument becomes more subtle than a simple chain of causes.

Abhinavagupta allows that the principles can be understood through their mutual inclusion within the universal reality.

But this does not necessarily undermine the distinction between their manifested functions.

The *tattvas* remain distinguishable within the ordered account.

Yet their complete reality is not exhausted by those distinctions.

### Two levels of analysis

At the level of a particular *prakriyā*, a systematic exposition, the order may proceed from gross to subtle.

Earth.

Water.

Fire.

And so forth.

At the level of supreme consciousness, the entire manifestation is present within a single undivided reality.

The differentiated order does not impose an external restriction upon that reality.

So when the apparent order is reversed, the result isn't automatically a contradiction of nonduality.

It can be another mode of articulating the same inclusive reality.

But that doesn't mean the different tattvas are casually interchangeable in ordinary experience.

A hand isn't an eye.

Water isn't fire.

Buddhi isn't the sense of smell.

Their differentiated functions remain meaningful.

What changes is the metaphysical assumption that each one possesses an independent ultimate existence.

### The Sanskrit: Pārāmeśvara-svātantrya

**पारमेश्वरस्वातन्त्र्य - pārameśvarasvātantrya**

The sovereign freedom of Parameśvara.

Break it into:

**पारमेश्वर - pārameśvara**

Pertaining to the Supreme Lord.

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Freedom, independence.

Now this is a particularly important compound because it explains why the manifested order needn't be an absolutely compulsory sequence.

The higher and lower principles are related through divine manifestation.

But the supreme freedom is not itself a passive product of the hierarchy.

**The hierarchy expresses freedom. Freedom is not manufactured by the hierarchy.**

That's the key distinction.

And Abhinavagupta is about to test it through an argument about something even more difficult than cause and effect.

Time.

The past.

The future.

And a figure whose future appearance is already narrated in scripture.

Which raises a rather delightful problem for anyone who thinks the future is completely absent.


## 7. The future is already present - Abhinavagupta's argument about time

We've now arrived at Kashmir Series pp. 141-143.

And suddenly Abhinavagupta introduces a remarkably interesting problem.

How can something described as future already be known?

He uses an example involving **Kalkī**, the future avatāra described in Purāṇic literature.

The example concerns the scriptural description of events that, from the standpoint of ordinary temporal experience, have not yet occurred.

Now, Abhinavagupta isn't presenting historical evidence that those future events have actually taken place.

He's investigating the relationship between **temporal succession and the manifestation of knowledge**.

Suppose a scripture describes a future event.

That event is apprehended in the present through the scriptural statement.

But its temporal position is future.

So what is the relationship between the content already manifest in knowledge and the event said to occur later?

### The Sanskrit: Bhaviṣyat

**भविष्यत् - bhaviṣyat**

Future, what will be.

From √**भू - bhū**, to be or become.

Compare:

**भूत - bhūta**

Having been, become, past.

**भविष्यत् - bhaviṣyat**

What will be.

And:

**वर्तमान - vartamāna**

Present, occurring.

Now here's a lovely grammatical distinction.

The future participial formation expresses a temporal orientation.

But the cognition of the future event is occurring now.

You can think about tomorrow today.

That's not a contradiction.

The temporal content of the thought and the time at which the thought occurs are different.

Abhinavagupta develops a much stronger metaphysical thesis from this distinction.

### The future is manifest within consciousness

If the supreme consciousness is the ground of all temporal manifestation, it cannot itself be constituted by the succession of past, present and future.

Its freedom includes the manifestation of that succession.

Thus the apparently future state is not outside the scope of universal consciousness.

But be careful.

This does not establish that every ordinary prediction is true, or that a person's imagination of tomorrow guarantees tomorrow will happen exactly that way.

The argument concerns the metaphysical relation between universal awareness and temporal determination.

Not the empirical reliability of predicting events.

### What if the event is already past?

Abhinavagupta considers the possibility that a supposed future event might be described as having already occurred in some other cosmic cycle.

But then the same difficulty returns.

Are we speaking about the identical event?

Or another event?

If it's different, the original problem remains.

If it's identical, the relation between temporal position and identity must be explained.

And if we say it exists beyond time, we're already approaching the account Abhinavagupta wants.

The ultimate reality isn't bounded by the temporal distinctions it manifests.

### The Sanskrit: Akālakalita

**अकालकलित - akālakalita**

Not determined or delimited by time.

Let's break it down.

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**काल - kāla**

Time.

**कलित - kalita**

Measured, reckoned, formed or determined, depending on context.

Thus:

**akālakalita**

Not measured out or delimited by time.

This is an important expression in Trika.

Bhairava's nature isn't one event occurring before all other events.

Nor is it an event occurring after them.

It is the ultimate reality within which temporal differentiation becomes possible.

Now the philosophical connection becomes clear.

The tattvas can be arranged as a sequence.

But the complete reality of consciousness does not come into existence only after the sequence has finished.

And the same principle applies to the relation between past and future.

**Temporal succession is manifest within consciousness; consciousness isn't exhausted by temporal succession.**

### The Cosmic Calendar Department

Imagine Śiva sitting at a desk.

A clerk arrives.

"Lord, the universe is scheduled to manifest at 9:30."

Śiva asks:

"What is 9:30?"

"Time, Lord."

"And where did time come from?"

"Your divine manifestation, Lord."

"Then why are you telling me I have to wait for it?"

The clerk quietly resigns.

That's the problem.

You cannot impose the conditions of a manifested order upon the ultimate reality that, according to the system, makes the order possible.

And Abhinavagupta is now ready to explain the whole system through one of his teachers' most important techniques.


## 8. सम्पुटीकरण - Sampuṭīkaraṇa: The entire universe enclosed within the Supreme

Now we reach Kashmir Series p. 143.

Abhinavagupta invokes a teaching transmitted through his gurus.

And the key word is magnificent:

**सम्पुटीकरण - sampuṭīkaraṇa**

Enclosing, encompassing, forming a container around, folding together.

Let's dismantle it.

**सम् - sam-**

Together, completely.

**पुट - puṭa**

A fold, container, enclosure, covering.

**सम्पुट - sampuṭa**

A closed container, joined enclosure, or pair of enclosing surfaces.

**करण - karaṇa**

Making, doing, producing.

So:

**sampuṭīkaraṇa**

The act or condition of enclosing or encompassing something.

Now the metaphysical application.

The supreme consciousness encompasses both the individual tattvas and their entire collective order.

It's not merely an additional principle outside them.

It is their ultimate ground.

### The Sanskrit: Tattvaugha

**तत्त्वौघ - tattvaugha**

The mass or totality of principles.

Break it down:

**तत्त्व - tattva**

Principle of reality.

**ओघ - ogha**

Mass, flood, collection, multitude.

Their combination:

**tattva + ogha → tattvaugha**

Here **a + o → au**.

That's a useful vowel-sandhi pattern.

So:

**तत्त्वौघ - tattvaugha**

The entire collection of tattvas.

Abhinavagupta's point is that both each particular principle and the whole collection are encompassed within the supreme reality.

Now the distinction is subtle.

A particular tattva is not simply identical with the entire manifest universe in every functional respect.

But its complete reality is inseparable from the consciousness that is the ground of all the tattvas.

### The guru's method

The *sampuṭa* teaching involves enclosing the manifested order within pure supreme existence.

And because the entire universe is so encompassed, its apparently separate stages do not establish independent realities.

Now consider what we've been studying.

Earth.

Water.

The tanmātras.

The senses.

Buddhi.

Puruṣa.

Māyā.

Śakti.

All these principles can be considered individually.

But their ultimate reality is present within the same supreme consciousness.

The *sampuṭa* interpretation makes this relation explicit.

Imagine a vast book.

Every chapter has its own contents.

The chapters have a sequence.

And you can read them one by one.

But the whole work isn't created each time you turn to a new chapter.

The sequence of reading and the complete structure of the book are different.

Now, this is merely an analogy.

But it helps explain how a sequence can be meaningful without being the ultimate cause of the unity within which the sequence appears.

And Abhinavagupta is about to develop this through **reflection**.

Because the Parātrīśikā isn't simply explaining a linear series.

It's also explaining why different sequences can be reversed.

And that requires a mirror.


## 9. Parā, Parāparā and Paśyantī - The universe enters the mirror

Now we reach pp. 144-145.

Abhinavagupta returns to the relation between the supreme Goddess **Parā** and the more differentiated mode of manifestation associated with **Parāparā** and **Paśyantī**.

This is one of the most technically important parts of the commentary.

Let's recall the levels.

**परा - Parā**

Supreme speech, the undivided power of consciousness.

**पश्यन्ती - Paśyantī**

The visionary level in which distinctions are beginning to emerge without full outward articulation.

**मध्यमा - Madhyamā**

Internal speech, with more differentiated relations between expression and meaning.

**वैखरी - Vaikharī**

Articulated external speech.

But the text doesn't simply treat these as four separate containers.

They are modes of manifestation.

And the same supreme power is present throughout.

### The mirror analogy

Abhinavagupta describes Paśyantī through the image of a clear mirror in which Parā's nature becomes reflected.

Now picture a mirror.

A face appears within it.

The reflection resembles the original.

Yet the reflection can involve a different spatial orientation.

The image is related to the original, but the relation between them is not simply that of two identical physical faces.

And now Abhinavagupta asks us to apply this to the tattvas.

In the supreme condition, the principles are not articulated as independent objects.

But in the reflective manifestation, distinctions emerge.

And their relative ordering can appear reversed.

### Why reflection requires a distinction

Here's a subtle but important point.

If no distinction whatsoever can be made between an original and its apparent image, then speaking of a reflection doesn't explain anything.

There must be a relation between what is reflected and the mode in which it appears.

But that relation need not establish two independent ultimate substances.

The same reality can manifest as the original and the reflection.

And Abhinavagupta connects this with the difference between Parā and Parāparā.

In Parā, the ultimate unity of conscious manifestation predominates.

In Parāparā, differentiation begins to become intelligible without establishing complete separation.

Thus the mirror analogy becomes appropriate.

### The Sanskrit: Pratibimba

**प्रतिबिम्ब - pratibimba**

Reflection, reflected image.

Break it down:

**प्रति - prati**

Towards, back, in correspondence with.

**बिम्ब - bimba**

Image, disc, original form.

So:

**pratibimba**

A corresponding reflected image.

Now the wonderful distinction.

**बिम्ब - bimba**

The original.

**प्रतिबिम्ब - pratibimba**

The reflection.

Abhinavagupta is using the relation to explain how the one conscious reality can appear through different modes of manifestation.

Not because the Absolute has literally become a mirror made of glass.

But because the reflection provides an analogy for differentiation without complete independence.

### The original isn't destroyed

Now imagine looking at your face in a mirror.

The reflection appears.

But your original face isn't thereby annihilated.

Nor does the reflection become a wholly independent person living behind the glass.

It depends upon the original and the reflecting conditions.

In the Tantric analogy, the differentiated powers depend upon the supreme conscious ground.

And the mode of their presentation can differ from the mode in which they are present in undivided awareness.

That's why the apparent reversal of the tattva order need not establish a contradiction.

**The reflected ordering is not an independent creation outside the reality it reflects.**

And now Abhinavagupta makes the argument explicit.


## 10. Why Earth becomes Śakti in the mirror

This is the payoff.

The passage at Kashmir Series p. 146 explains the apparent reversal of the tattva order through the mirror-like manifestation of Parā within Parāparā.

Abhinavagupta states that what is the Śakti principle in supreme consciousness becomes the Earth principle in the Parāparā mode, and conversely what is Earth there corresponds to Śakti in the reversed ordering.

Now remember the dispute.

In the Parātrīśikā's initial arrangement:

**क - ka** corresponds to Earth.

And **क्ष - kṣa** to Śakti.

But another system assigns Earth to **क्ष - kṣa**.

How?

Abhinavagupta's answer is that the ordering is reflected or inverted through the differentiated manifestation.

### Ūrdhva and Adhara

Two exceptionally useful words.

**ऊर्ध्व - ūrdhva**

Upper, above, higher.

**अधर - adhara**

Lower, below, inferior.

Now:

**ऊर्ध्वाधर - ūrdhvādhara**

Upper and lower.

And:

**विपर्यास - viparyāsa**

Reversal, inversion, error or contrary arrangement, depending on context.

Here the sense of inversion is central.

Thus the compound:

**ऊर्ध्वाधरविपर्यास - ūrdhvādharaviparyāsa**

Inversion of upper and lower.

Now look at the ordinary sandhi.

*ūrdhva + adhara → ūrdhvādhara*.

Because **a + a → ā**.

The word is not just some gigantic philosophical monstrosity.

It's made of perfectly recognizable pieces.

And now the meaning.

The principle appearing uppermost in one arrangement may appear lower in the reflected arrangement.

But that does not prove that the ultimate reality has changed its nature.

The manifested order has changed.

### The cosmic mirror

Imagine writing the word **ŚIVA** on a piece of paper and holding it in front of a mirror.

The reflected orientation changes.

Yet the written word itself hasn't become another independently existing word with an entirely unrelated source.

The relation between original and reflected appearance explains the difference.

Now apply that very cautiously to the tattva arrangement.

What appears at one end of the supreme ordered exposition can appear at the opposite end of the reflected manifestation.

The hierarchy is inverted through the mode of reflection.

And this is how Abhinavagupta begins reconciling the apparently conflicting phoneme-to-tattva arrangements.

### An absolutely essential qualification

He is not saying that the physical Earth element literally transforms into the divine Śakti principle in ordinary empirical experience.

Nor that the two have identical practical functions.

He is discussing **the assignment and relation of tattvas in different modes of sacred manifestation**.

The reflection concerns their position within an ontological and phonemic order.

Within that order, the inversion becomes intelligible.

And the supreme consciousness remains unaffected.

### Bhairava never gets inverted

Now comes the crucial point.

Abhinavagupta emphasizes that supreme Bhairava is never fundamentally reversed.

Why?

Because the divine consciousness is not one object standing opposite another object within the reflected arrangement.

It is the ground of the whole manifestation.

The reflection belongs to differentiated expression.

The source remains the supreme reality.

This is a genuinely elegant answer to the earlier problem.

The *kṣa*-beginning arrangement isn't necessarily a contradiction of the *a*-beginning arrangement.

The two can be understood through different levels of manifestation and reflection.

But the reflection isn't arbitrary.

It expresses the relation between Parā and Parāparā.

Now, this is why the Trika triad matters so much.

Without understanding the relation between the supreme and the differentiated powers, the alphabetic dispute looks incoherent.

With the triadic structure, Abhinavagupta can explain why the orders differ.


## 11. The three Goddesses aren't three independently competing universes

Now we're at p. 147.

Abhinavagupta reminds the reader that the supreme consciousness is associated with the three divine powers.

**परा - Parā**

**परापरा - Parāparā**

**अपरा - Aparā**

Now, you know these well.

But in this passage, the distinction is being applied to the levels of speech and phonemic manifestation.

The powers are not simply three different female deities competing for control of the same cosmos.

They're distinct modalities of the one supreme consciousness.

And the relative manifestation of unity and difference varies between them.

### Parā - Undivided manifestation

At the supreme level, the tattvas do not stand forth as independently differentiated objects.

Their reality is grounded in undivided conscious power.

So no one phonemic ordering can be imposed upon the supreme consciousness as an externally fixed necessity.

### Parāparā - Difference within non-difference

In the intermediate mode, the distinctions become more articulated.

But their relation to the original supreme consciousness remains evident.

This makes the mirror analogy appropriate.

The image differs from the original.

Yet its reality is not independent of the original.

The higher and lower orderings can therefore appear in different relations.

### Aparā - Fully differentiated manifestation

At the more differentiated level, the distinct forms of speech and cognition become increasingly explicit.

The signifier.

The signified.

The uttered sound.

The object designated.

All are distinguishable.

And now the linguistic system begins to function in the manner familiar from ordinary communication.

But the relation among the levels isn't one of complete separation.

The differentiated modes remain grounded in the supreme.

And that brings us to the next step.

**How can the signifier and signified be different while remaining within one consciousness?**

Abhinavagupta's answer will involve a particularly beautiful Tantric symbolism.

Seed and womb.

The vowels and consonants.

Śiva and Śakti.

And now the alphabet is about to become a family drama again.


## 12. वाचक and वाच्य - The difference between a word and what it means

At Kashmir Series p. 148, Abhinavagupta turns to the internal level of speech, **Madhyamā**.

This is the field in which signifier and signified can be differentiated without yet requiring fully articulated external speech.

Now we need two crucial Sanskrit words.

### Vācaka - वाचक

**वाचक - vācaka**

The denoter, signifier, what expresses or names something.

Related to √**vac**, to speak.

This is the word or expression considered in its signifying function.

For example:

**वृक्ष - vṛkṣa**

The word "tree."

That's the signifier in the example.

### Vācya - वाच्य

**वाच्य - vācya**

What is to be said, signified or expressed.

Also related to √*vac*, but through a different formation.

This is the object or meaning expressed by the word.

Thus:

| Sanskrit | Function |
|--------|--------|
| **वाचक - vācaka** | The signifying word |
| **वाच्य - vācya** | What is signified |

And the difference is extremely important.

The spoken word "tree" is not itself a physical tree.

You cannot plant the phoneme *vṛkṣa* in the garden and expect it to grow branches.

But the word can designate a tree.

Ordinary language involves that relationship.

Now Abhinavagupta asks:

If the signifier and signified are really two entirely independent substances, what makes their relation intelligible?

And if they share one underlying conscious ground, how can differentiation occur?

This is the same question we've been investigating all along.

### The Sanskrit: Adhyāsa

**अध्यास - adhyāsa**

Superimposition, placing something upon something else.

Now be careful.

This isn't always identical with the particular Advaita Vedānta theory of error associated with Śaṅkara.

The term has wider philosophical uses.

In this passage, Abhinavagupta is examining how signifier and signified can be related through a form of mutual presentation or superimposition.

The word and the meaning appear connected.

Yet distinguishable.

And that connection is established within consciousness.

### The wonderful cloth example

Abhinavagupta uses a wonderfully concrete comparison.

A cloth cannot cover another cloth if it is too small to encompass it.

Now that sounds like somebody discussing household laundry.

But the point is metaphysical.

To explain the superimposition of a signifier upon the signified, the two must possess a basis for their mutual relation.

If the signifier were completely unrelated to the world it expresses, what would enable its meaningful application?

Abhinavagupta's solution is that **both are manifestations of one consciousness**.

The word and its object are differentiated.

Yet both are present within the same universal expressive power.

So the one can function as the expression of the other.

### The universal signifier and the universal signified

Now imagine a word that can refer to the entire universe.

And the universe as that which is expressed.

At the highest level, both are grounded in the same consciousness.

The differentiation belongs to the manifestation.

But the ultimate ground is not divided.

And this allows Abhinavagupta to connect the linguistic relationship with Śiva and Śakti.

The signifier expresses the conscious subjectivity.

The signified expresses the manifesting power through which the object becomes present.

Now the symbolism is ready.

**Vowels are the seed. Consonants are the womb.**

And the whole alphabet is a manifestation of their inseparable relation.


## 13. बीज and योनि - The seed and womb of language

Now we're at the point where Abhinavagupta invokes the Mālinī scripture's doctrine of the relation between vowels and consonants.

This is one of the most important symbolic classifications in the whole passage.

The Mālinīvijayottaratantra explicitly identifies the seed principle with Śiva and the generative matrix with Śakti.

### Bīja - बीज

**बीज - bīja**

Seed.

Ordinarily, something from which a plant develops.

In Tantric phonemic symbolism, the *bīja* can represent concentrated generative power.

Here the vowels are associated with the seed principle.

Now the philosophical significance.

A seed contains generative potential.

But it doesn't manifest as a fully developed plant independently of all conditions.

The seed must participate in the appropriate generative relationship.

And in the symbolic system, that relationship is represented through the union of Śiva and Śakti.

### Yoni - योनि

**योनि - yoni**

Womb, source, generative matrix.

The consonants are associated with this feminine generative power.

Now remember the ordinary Sanskrit alphabet.

The vowels can be spoken independently.

Consonants, in actual phonetic articulation, require a context for their complete syllabic expression, and Devanāgarī normally represents consonant letters with an inherent vowel.

But don't conflate that orthographic feature with the entire metaphysical doctrine.

The Tantric distinction between *bīja* and *yoni* is not simply a description of how to write syllables.

It is a symbolic account of creative consciousness.

### The seed and the womb

In the traditional mapping:

**Śiva - the seed, bīja.**

**Śakti - the womb, yoni.**

Their conjunction manifests the differentiated world.

And the distinction between vowels and consonants expresses this symbolically.

Now the key philosophical move.

The seed is not ultimately independent of the womb.

The womb is not ultimately independent of the seed.

The divine manifestation depends upon their inseparable relationship.

But their functions can be distinguished.

### The Sanskrit: Vyajana

**व्यञ्जन - vyañjana**

Consonant, but also expression, manifestation or indication in other contexts.

Related to *vi-* and √*añj*, to manifest, mark or adorn.

Now compare:

**स्वर - svara**

Vowel, tone.

**व्यञ्जन - vyañjana**

Consonant.

The ordinary linguistic distinction is genuine.

But Abhinavagupta uses it to express the theological relationship between Śiva and Śakti.

And the difference between conventional phonology and esoteric symbolism must remain clear.

**The vowels are not biologically male. The consonants are not literally anatomical wombs.**

The symbolic system assigns them complementary generative functions.

Those functions are then interpreted through the metaphysics of consciousness.

### Why Mālinī matters here

Remember the earlier dispute?

The Mātṛkā alphabet preserves the familiar ordering.

Mālinī arranges the phonemes differently.

Now the distinction becomes intelligible in a richer way.

The phonemes are not simply isolated units in a rigid sequence.

They participate in the relationship between seed and matrix, signifier and signified, consciousness and manifestation.

The ordering can change while the underlying generative principles remain.

And because Śiva and Śakti are inseparable, their differentiated phonemic arrangements need not establish separate ultimate realities.

This is why the earlier alphabetic dispute has led to the doctrine of *bīja* and *yoni*.

The whole sequence is coherent.

Abhinavagupta isn't randomly inserting sexual symbolism into a linguistics lesson.

He's using the generative relation of Śiva and Śakti to explain the manifestation of language.

Though, admittedly, the entire alphabet has now become a metaphysical fertility clinic.


## 14. Even the distinction between seed and womb is not ultimate

Now Abhinavagupta pushes the symbolism further.

If the vowels represent Śiva and the consonants represent Śakti, we might be tempted to treat them as two completely separate categories.

But that would violate the nondual doctrine.

So the distinction must be understood as functional rather than ultimately substantial.

The vowels participate in manifestation.

The consonants participate in expression.

The signifier and signified are related.

And both depend upon the same universal consciousness.

### The Sanskrit: Avicchinna

**अविच्छिन्न - avicchinna**

Unbroken, uninterrupted, inseparable.

From:

**a-** - negative prefix.

**vicchinna** - cut off, separated, interrupted.

The underlying verbal family involves √*chid*, to cut or divide.

Now compare:

**छिन्न - chinna**

Cut, severed.

**विच्छिन्न - vicchinna**

Separated, interrupted.

**अविच्छिन्न - avicchinna**

Unseparated, continuous.

And this is precisely the condition Abhinavagupta wants to establish.

The apparent distinction between signifier and signified does not sever them from their universal conscious ground.

### The problem with insisting on an absolute division

Suppose somebody says:

"Vowels are Śiva. Consonants are Śakti. Therefore the two must be fundamentally separate."

Abhinavagupta's metaphysics would immediately reject the conclusion.

The distinction is part of manifestation.

It doesn't establish an independent dualism.

And the same principle applies to the knower and known.

They can be differentiated in experience.

But their ultimate ground remains one.

Now this matters for the practical interpretation of mantra.

The sacred sound is not a completely autonomous magical object.

Its power is understood through consciousness.

The sound, meaning and act of realization are related manifestations of the same reality.

### The connection with liberation and enjoyment

Abhinavagupta also relates the conjunction of the seed and womb principles to the traditional pair:

**भोग - bhoga**

Enjoyment, experience.

**अपवर्ग - apavarga**

Liberation, release.

The generative manifestation includes the field of experience.

But its recognition can also be associated with liberation.

Again, this doesn't mean every ordinary sensual experience automatically liberates.

Nor that the mere pronunciation of any syllable guarantees realization.

The relation between experience and release must be understood through the appropriate recognition of consciousness.

And this is exactly where the philosophical doctrine and the ritual tradition intersect.

The mantras are not merely labels for the tattvas.

They are interpreted as participating in the powers through which manifestation and recognition occur.

And now Abhinavagupta returns explicitly to Mālinī.


## 15. Mālinī is established in Madhyamā - Not merely a scrambled alphabet

At Kashmir Series pp. 149-151, Abhinavagupta returns to the distinctive Mālinī arrangement.

And here's the important distinction.

**Mālinī isn't merely the ordinary alphabet with the letters shuffled for entertainment.**

Her arrangement belongs to the expressive domain associated with Śakti and the more differentiated level of speech.

Abhinavagupta explicitly connects her with the principal reality of Madhyamā.

### The Sanskrit: Madhyamā

**मध्यमा - madhyamā**

The middle one, the intermediate one.

From:

**मध्य - madhya**

Middle.

The feminine adjective:

**madhyamā**

Intermediate.

In the speech doctrine, Madhyamā is the internal level where word and meaning can be distinguished without yet appearing as fully articulated external sounds.

Now consider what Mālinī does.

The phonemes are arranged in an alternative order.

But the order is not merely an arbitrary sequence of visible characters.

It is a manifestation of the inner expressive power.

And because the relation between signifier and signified is grounded in consciousness, the arrangement can be interpreted as a valid mode of divine speech.

The philosophical explanation therefore precedes the ritual application.

### The famous Mālinī verse

Abhinavagupta cites the *Śrīpūrva* scripture, identified with the Mālinīvijayottara tradition, instructing the use of Mālinī as a differentiated generative matrix for the manifestation of the Śākta body.

Its key phrase is:

**भिन्नयोनिं तु मालिनीम्**

*Bhinnayoniṃ tu mālinīm.*

Mālinī as the differentiated matrix.

Now look at the grammar.

**भिन्नयोनि - bhinnayoni**

Having a differentiated source or womb.

**मालिनी - mālinī**

The Goddess Mālinī.

Both appear here in the feminine accusative singular.

Why accusative?

Because they are the object of the instruction involving *nyāsa*, ritual placement or installation.

The verse is discussing a traditional ritual application, not merely offering a dictionary definition.

### Nyāsa - न्यास

**न्यास - nyāsa**

Placing, depositing, assigning, installation.

Related to *ni-* and √*as*, to place or throw.

In Tantric ritual, *nyāsa* can refer to the placement of mantra syllables upon or within the ritualized body.

Now, we're studying the historical and philosophical meaning here, not prescribing an initiation ritual.

The important point is that **the phonemes constitute an ordered manifestation of divine power**.

Their placement has theological significance within the ritual system.

And Mālinī's differentiated arrangement expresses the generative Śakti.

### The contrast with Mātṛkā

Remember:

**मातृका - Mātṛkā**

The alphabetic Mother-power.

And:

**मालिनी - Mālinī**

The differentiated arrangement of the phonemes.

Abhinavagupta has already established that these orders can differ without requiring different ultimate realities.

Now he explains that the relation between seed and womb, Śiva and Śakti, and signifier and signified provides the underlying metaphysical structure.

Thus the two systems are not merely contradictory lists.

They represent different modalities of expression.

**Mātṛkā and Mālinī can be distinguished through their arrangement while remaining inseparable from the supreme conscious power.**

Now that's the core of the resolution.

And notice how much work Abhinavagupta has done to get here.

He didn't just say:

"Different alphabets, same Śiva."

He developed the dependence of every tattva upon the whole.

He analysed the relationship between specific and general properties.

He traced the causal order.

He examined the status of past and future.

He invoked the supreme enclosing consciousness.

He explained reflection and inversion.

He distinguished the levels of speech.

And he finally connected the signifier-signified relation with Śiva-Śakti.

That's why the conclusion matters.

It has been earned through the argument.


## 16. Why mantra belongs particularly to Śakti

Now Abhinavagupta invokes another traditional distinction.

A mantra may be associated with the limited *puruṣa* principle.

Or with the supreme Śiva principle.

Or with Śakti.

But the ritual efficacy attributed to mantra is particularly connected with **Śakti as operative power**.

Why?

Because the mantra is understood as a manifestation of divine activity.

And activity is the domain of Śakti.

This isn't saying Śiva is fundamentally an unconscious corpse.

Nor that Śakti is a second independent deity who performs tasks on his behalf.

The distinction is theological and functional.

### Niṣkriya - निष्क्रिय

**निष्क्रिय - niṣkriya**

Without activity, inactive.

The negative prefix *niṣ-* is joined to:

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action.

Now the important distinction.

In a particular theological classification, the supreme Śiva principle may be described as beyond determinate activity.

But the universal consciousness cannot be treated as absolutely devoid of the free power of manifestation.

Otherwise Abhinavagupta's entire doctrine of *svātantrya* would collapse.

So when the ritual text distinguishes the supreme principle from operative Śakti, it is marking different functions within the doctrine.

Not asserting that Śiva is a dead substance and Śakti is an independent machine.

### Jaḍatva - जडत्व

**जडत्व - jaḍatva**

Inertness, insentience.

From:

**जड - jaḍa**

Inert, dull, insentient.

With the abstract suffix:

**-त्व - -tva**

Now the limited *puruṣa*, within certain ritual classifications, lacks the universal freedom of action.

The supreme principle, considered beyond differentiation, is not an operative ritual instrument in the same sense.

Śakti is the expressive power through which mantra can function.

This is why the ritual text places special emphasis on Śakti.

### Bhoga and Apavarga again

The traditional goal-pair returns.

**भोग - bhoga**

Experience, enjoyment.

**अपवर्ग - apavarga**

Liberation.

The operative power of mantra is said to concern both.

But the distinction must be interpreted within the theological system.

Śakti manifests the field of experience.

And through her self-revealing nature, the recognition of its ground becomes possible.

That's the metaphysical relation.

The ultimate reality isn't divided into a god of worldly enjoyment and a god of liberation.

Both belong to the one divine freedom.

And this is where Abhinavagupta closes the present movement.

Because now that the nature of Mālinī as a differentiated generative power has been established, he can begin the incredibly detailed exposition of her individual phonemic correspondences.

That is the next textual boundary.

The commentary is about to become a dense enumeration of how the sounds unfold through different sensory, cognitive and divine structures.

And rather than silently leap across that extraordinary material, we'll take it as the beginning of Part IX.


## 17. The great synthesis - Why everything can be everything without becoming identical in every respect

Now gather the entire argument.

And I mean genuinely gather it.

Not merely repeat the familiar sentence:

"Everything is Śiva."

We have a much more precise understanding now.

### The first claim: Particulars depend upon their grounds

A *śiṃśapā* is a tree.

But not every tree is a *śiṃśapā*.

The specific depends upon the general.

The dependence is asymmetric.

### The second claim: The gross manifestation depends upon subtler principles

Earth cannot be understood independently of the higher tattvas.

The gross elements imply the subtle qualities.

The qualities belong to the structures of cognition.

The cognitive structures belong to the relation of experiencer and experienced.

And the finite subject depends upon the powers of contraction and the universal conscious ground.

### The third claim: The full order exists within consciousness

The tattvas don't manufacture consciousness as their final product.

Their reality is grounded in consciousness.

And that consciousness is not itself bound to the temporal order through which the tattvas are explained.

### The fourth claim: Differentiated arrangements can be reflected or reversed

The higher and lower positions may be differently organized in Parā, Parāparā and the subsequent expressive levels.

The reflected order can differ from the original.

But the supreme conscious reality remains the ground of both.

### The fifth claim: Language expresses the same structure

The signifier and signified are distinguishable.

The vowels and consonants are distinguishable.

The seed and womb are distinguishable.

Śiva and Śakti can be distinguished for the purposes of explanation.

Yet none of those distinctions establishes two completely independent ultimate realities.

### The sixth claim: The alphabetic systems can differ without annihilating the doctrine

Mātṛkā follows one ordering.

Mālinī follows another.

The ritual systems can assign different phonemes to different tattvas.

But their fundamental significance depends upon the relation between consciousness and its expressive powers.

And that relationship is not confined to a single externally imposed sequence.

Now, here's what I find so remarkable about Abhinavagupta's argument.

He's not trying to eliminate differences.

He's trying to explain their existence without surrendering nonduality.

That's a much harder philosophical task.

It's easy to declare that everything is one.

It's also easy to declare that everything is different.

The difficult problem is explaining **how the one can genuinely manifest difference without becoming ultimately divided**.

And that's exactly the problem Trika is attempting to solve.


## 18. The deeper implication for recognition

Now compare all of this with the original question of the Parātrīśikā.

The Goddess asked:

How does Anuttara grant Kaulika perfection through mere recognition?

We've spent all these chapters discovering what that could mean.

The finite subject experiences itself as one particular entity within a vast universe.

It has limited knowledge.

Limited agency.

A body.

A mind.

A particular location.

A particular history.

And it seems to stand opposite a world of independently existing things.

But Abhinavagupta argues that the whole structure of subject and object ultimately depends upon universal consciousness.

The individual is not simply an independent substance that happens to possess consciousness as one of its properties.

Rather, its reality is grounded in the self-manifesting consciousness of Śiva.

Recognition concerns that ground.

Now notice how the *sarvasarvātmakatva* doctrine changes the inquiry.

You needn't imagine consciousness as a remote cosmic object inaccessible from ordinary experience.

The ordinary experience itself is not independent of consciousness.

The tree.

The sound.

The sense of hearing.

The thought of the tree.

The cognition that says "I am seeing."

All are manifestations whose reality depends upon the same ground.

But this doesn't mean merely asserting:

"I am the tree."

Nor does it mean ordinary intellectual acquaintance with the doctrine is equivalent to liberation.

The recognition concerns the fundamental nature of the experiencer, rather than the ego appropriating objects as its possessions.

### The mistake of the metaphysical collector

Imagine someone studying every tattva.

They learn the order.

Memorize the thirty-six principles.

Study the phonemic correspondences.

Draw elaborate diagrams.

Collect every scriptural variant.

And eventually announce:

"I have mastered the entire universe."

Now Abhinavagupta asks:

Have you recognized the consciousness through which the entire collection is manifest?

Or have you merely accumulated another elaborate system of conceptual objects?

The distinction is devastating.

You can study every map without recognizing the ground upon which maps are possible.

But equally, simply rejecting the maps isn't necessarily realization.

The maps can be genuine instruments of understanding.

**The proper relationship is to use the differentiated teaching without mistaking the teaching's particular structure for an independent ultimate reality.**

And that is why Abhinavagupta's philosophical reasoning is not an unnecessary diversion from the spiritual goal.

It is part of the work of removing misrecognition.

### The philosophical conclusion

What began as an argument about conflicting alphabetic charts has revealed a much deeper issue.

The world isn't a collection of independent objects that later acquire a relationship with consciousness.

For Abhinavagupta, the entire manifestation is grounded in consciousness from the beginning.

Its distinctions are genuine as manifestations.

But their ultimate reality is not divided.

And that is why every tattva, considered in its complete nature, implicates the whole.

Not because a pebble literally contains a miniature universe.

But because **no manifested principle is ultimately independent of the consciousness and powers through which the entire universe is present**.

The teaching is astonishingly ambitious.

And the Sanskrit is getting more interesting with every page.
