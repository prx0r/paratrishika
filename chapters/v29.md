# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XXIX - The Secret Meaning of a Single Letter

*Kashmir Series pp. 238-241 · Abhinavagupta's commentary on the Heart of Bhairava*

Previously, Utpaladeva had delivered a rather devastating blow to the idea that liberation requires the permanent disappearance of every thought:

**विश्वात्मनो विकल्पानां प्रसरेऽपि महेशता।**

*Viśvātmano vikalpānāṃ prasare 'pi maheśatā.*

For one whose Self is the universe, Great Lordship remains possible even while conceptual determinations expand.

Abhinavagupta now changes the subject.

Or rather, he performs that particularly infuriating philosophical manoeuvre in which the subject appears to change completely, only for you to discover that he's been explaining the same thing all along.

We're going from **universal consciousness** to **the letter स - sa**.

And the argument is magnificent:

What if a phoneme isn't merely a meaningless acoustic brick?

What if the capacity for meaning is already present in the conscious power through which language is manifested?

What if the difference between a word, its object and the cognition of that object is itself an expression of the freedom of consciousness?

And, because this is Abhinavagupta, we're going to investigate all this through four-armed deities, intoxicating beverages, sacred lettering, sexual physiology, animal names, grammatical particles and an argument with nearly the entire philosophy of language.

Then Patañjali and Bhartṛhari turn up.

The entire Sanskrit grammatical department is about to discover that it accidentally enrolled in a Kaula Tantra seminar.

## 1. The textual boundary - What belongs to whom?

We resume immediately after the quotation from Utpaladeva's *Īśvarapratyabhijñā* 4.12.

The Kashmir Series contains a long **square-bracketed explanatory passage** after that verse. It is not part of Abhinavagupta's continuous prose here.

His actual prose resumes with:

**यथा चाकृतिमध्य एव चतुर्भुजत्रिनयनपूर्णकृशाद्या आकृतयः।**

*Yathā cākṛtimadhya eva caturbhujatrinayanapūrṇakṛśādyā ākṛtayaḥ.*

"Just as, among forms themselves, there are forms such as four-armed, three-eyed, full-bodied and emaciated ones."

This is the genuine beginning of our installment.

The next major transition comes after Abhinavagupta has interpreted **sa**, quoted Patañjali's *Yogasūtra* 3.17, explained single-phoneme grammatical expressions, invoked Bhartṛhari's *Vākyapadīya* 2.2, and qualified the limits of phonemic etymology.

He then concludes:

**तदेवं सकार ईदृशः। औकारविसर्गावपि व्याख्यातौ।**

*Tad evaṃ sakāra īdṛśaḥ. Aukāravisargāv api vyākhyātau.*

"Thus the letter *sa* is of this nature. The vowel *au* and Visarga have also been explained."

Immediately afterward he introduces a quotation from the *Pūrvaśāstra*, identified independently as the *Mālinīvijayottaratantra* 4.25.

**That will be our stopping point:** the conclusion of this letter-and-meaning argument, before the fresh scriptural citation.

One particularly important discovery from comparing the Sanskrit sources: the Kashmir Series quotes only the **first half** of *Vākyapadīya* 2.2, and its reading *sākāṅkṣam* differs from the independent GRETIL text's *sāpekṣam*. We must not invent a different Bhartṛhari verse merely because the subject is phonemes. ; ; ; \]


## 2. यथा चाकृतिमध्य एव - Some forms stand out among forms

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · P. 238

**यथा चाकृतिमध्य एव।**

*Yathā cākṛtimadhya eva.*

"Just as, even within the domain of forms..."

This is an analogy.

Not a new root-Tantra verse.

Not an independently quoted hymn.

Abhinavagupta is preparing to explain why one particular phoneme may have a distinctive or superior role among phonemes.

### Yathā - यथा

Just as, in the manner that.

An indeclinable introducing a comparison.

### Ca - च

And.

The connected form:

*yathā + ca → yathā ca.*

### Ākṛti - आकृति

Form, configuration, shape.

The components are:

**आ - ā**, toward or fully.

**√कृ - kṛ**, to make.

The nominal form **ākṛti** describes a particular configuration or appearance.

### Madhya - मध्य

Middle, midst, domain.

Now:

**आकृतिमध्य - ākṛtimadhya**

The midst or class of forms.

The locative:

**आकृतिमध्ये - ākṛtimadhye**

Among forms.

### Eva - एव

Precisely, even, indeed.

So the opening says:

"Just as among forms themselves..."

And then come four examples.

Now these are wonderfully chosen because they concern **distinctive configurations within a shared category**.

Not different substances.

Different forms.


## 3. चतुर्भुज - The four-armed form

**चतुर्भुज - caturbhuja**

Four-armed.

Let's dissect it.

**चतुर् - catur**

Four.

**भुज - bhuja**

Arm.

The compound can mean "having four arms."

In Sanskrit compounds, the form describes a characteristic of something or someone beyond the words explicitly appearing in the compound. It can function as a *bahuvrīhi* adjective.

### Bahuvrīhi - बहुव्रीहि

A possessive compound.

The classic explanatory example is something like:

"Much rice" → one who possesses much rice.

Likewise:

**चतुर्भुज - caturbhuja**

One having four arms.

Now in Hindu iconography, multiple arms frequently designate divine figures.

But this is not an invitation to identify one particular god from the compound alone.

A four-armed form could belong to various deities.

The immediate philosophical point concerns the form's exceptional configuration.

### The divine anatomy department

Imagine ordinary embodied beings standing in a queue.

Two arms.

Two arms.

Two arms.

Then a deity with four arms appears.

The clerk looks up.

"That's unusual."

"Precisely."

"Does having four arms establish an entirely separate universe of ultimate being?"

"No. It is a distinctive form within the domain of forms."

That's the point.

A form may stand out without ceasing to belong to the broader category of form.

And Abhinavagupta applies precisely this reasoning to phonemes.

But first, three more examples.


## 4. त्रिनयन - Three eyes, and the metaphysics of distinctive appearance

**त्रिनयन - trinayana**

Three-eyed.

Let's dissect it.

**त्रि - tri**

Three.

**नयन - nayana**

Eye.

Related to √**नी - nī**, to lead, through its nominal formation.

The word *nayana* can mean the eye, the organ by which visual apprehension occurs.

Thus:

**त्रिनयन - trinayana**

Three-eyed.

Now here the Śaiva resonance is immediate.

Śiva is famously represented with a third eye.

But Abhinavagupta's immediate argument isn't that every form with an exceptional characteristic must be Śiva in a literal iconographic sense.

He is offering a **class analogy**.

A three-eyed configuration stands out among forms.

And a particular phoneme can stand out among phonemes.

### The difference between distinction and ultimate separation

Here's the philosophical point.

Something can possess a distinctive manifestation.

Without becoming independently ultimate.

The third eye can signify an extraordinary divine capacity within iconography.

But the manifestation remains within the wider field of consciousness.

And the same distinction applies to the letter *sa*.

It can receive a privileged theological interpretation.

Without becoming a second Absolute independent of Śiva.


## 5. पूर्णकृशाद्याः - Fullness and emaciation

Now the other pair:

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa**

Full, complete, filled out.

**कृश - kṛśa**

Thin, emaciated, lean.

And:

**आदि - ādi**

And so forth.

The transmitted compound is:

**पूर्णकृशाद्याः - pūrṇakṛśādyāḥ**.

"Those beginning with full and lean forms, and the like."

### The philosophical value of the contrast

Now this is really interesting.

Four arms and three eyes are extraordinary morphological features.

But fullness and emaciation can be familiar bodily conditions.

So the analogy encompasses both:

Exceptional configurations.

And ordinary differences within form.

The point is the **differentiation of features within one general field**.

### A morphology lesson

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa** is a past participial formation associated with √**पॄ - pṝ**, to fill.

**कृश - kṛśa** is an adjective meaning thin or lean.

And:

**-आद्याः - -ādyāḥ** marks a plural series beginning with the named examples.

The noun:

**आकृतयः - ākṛtayaḥ**

Forms.

Feminine nominative plural.

It agrees with the feminine plural adjective:

**चतुर्भुजत्रिनयनपूर्णकृशाद्याः**.

That agreement is why the entire phrase belongs to the comparison of forms.

### The cosmic fashion show

Imagine a divine fashion show.

Four arms.

Three eyes.

Full-bodied.

Lean.

Every kind of configuration.

The announcer says:

"Each displays a distinctive form."

Then Abhinavagupta points at the alphabet.

"Exactly."

The Sanskrit letters become nervous.

Because we're about to start judging which one possesses the most profound intrinsic significance.

And **स - sa** is feeling extremely confident.


## 6. द्रव्यमध्ये च सुरासवाद्याः - Even substances have exceptional forms

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · KASHMIR SERIES PP. 238-239

After the bodily forms, Abhinavagupta adds another analogy.

**द्रव्यमध्ये च सुरासवाद्याः**

*Dravyamadhye ca surāsavādyāḥ.*

"And among substances, alcoholic preparations such as surā and āsava."

Now we have an unexpectedly Kaula example.

Why would he introduce alcohol in a discussion of the letter *sa*?

Not because the Sanskrit letter needs a drink.

Though after twenty-eight installments, the poor thing could probably use one.

The argument is that **particular forms and substances can acquire an exceptional significance within a larger class**.

### Dravya - द्रव्य

Substance, material, thing.

The locative compound:

**द्रव्यमadhye - dravyamadhye**

Among substances.

Here *madhya* is a neuter *-a* stem, so:

**मध्ये - madhye**

Is the locative singular.

### Surā - सुरा

An intoxicating beverage.

### Āsava - आसव

A fermented or infused alcoholic preparation.

The compound:

**सुरासव - surāsava**

Surā and āsava.

### Ādi - आदि

And so forth.

Thus:

**सुरासवाद्याः - surāsavādyāḥ**

Those beginning with surā and āsava.

### The comparison

We have two sets:

| Domain | Exceptional examples |
|--------|--------|
| Forms (*ākṛti*\) | Four-armed, three-eyed, full and emaciated forms |
| Substances (*dravya*\) | Surā, āsava and other ritually distinctive materials |

The point is **utkarṣa**, distinctive eminence or standing out.

This shouldn't be converted into the claim that alcohol is inherently superior to all other substances in every practical context.

The text is operating within a particular Tantric symbolic framework.

Certain materials can assume a privileged ritual significance.

And certain phonemes can assume a privileged metaphysical significance.

### The important boundary

We have encountered Kaula ritual substances already in Part XXVII.

But the present passage is not prescribing their preparation or consumption.

It uses them as comparative examples.

Abhinavagupta is arguing from exceptional members within a class to the exceptional significance of a phoneme.

That's why the sequence matters.

**Forms → substances → written characters → phonemes.**

And that third step is especially interesting.

Because now we have to distinguish a phoneme from its written representation.

This is where the philosophical alphabet enters the world of actual handwriting.


## 7. लिप्यक्षराणि - A written character is not identical with a phoneme

THE FIRST DIFFICULT TRANSMISSION · P. 239

Abhinavagupta's next comparison uses:

**लिप्यक्षराणि - lipyakṣarāṇi**

Written letters, characters of a script.

Now the morphological structure is beautifully clear.

### Lipi - लिपि

Writing, script, inscription.

Related to the Sanskrit verbal family of coating or writing, especially √**लिप् - lip**.

### Akṣara - अक्षर

Syllable, letter, phonemic unit; also imperishable in another famous semantic development.

The compound:

**लिप्यक्षर - lipyakṣara**

A written character.

Neuter plural:

**लिप्यक्षराणि - lipyakṣarāṇi**.

Now notice the **y**.

*lipi + akṣara → lipyakṣara.*

The final **i** becomes **y** before the following vowel.

A classic instance of *yaṇ*-sandhi.

### The important distinction

**वर्ण - varṇa** can denote a phoneme.

**लिप्यक्षर - lipyakṣara** denotes a written character.

These are not automatically identical.

For example, the same Sanskrit sound can be represented using Devanāgarī, Grantha, Śāradā or Latin transliteration.

Consider the sound represented in IAST as:

**sa**

In Devanāgarī:

**स**

The written glyph isn't the sound wave.

Nor is the acoustic event identical with the conscious recognition of the phoneme.

And none of those levels, by itself, exhausts the supreme phonemic power described in the Tantric doctrine.

### An important manuscript disagreement

The Kashmir Series has an awkwardly transmitted phrase involving:

**सती अवर्णभूमिः**

*Satī avarṇabhūmiḥ.*

GRETIL instead reads:

**satyavarṇabhūmim**.

These are not trivially interchangeable.

The GRETIL reading points toward a relationship with the real or genuine domain of *varṇa*.

The Kashmir Series wording instead suggests a different grammatical construction involving *sati* and an *avarṇa*-ground.

Both require care; neither warrants an elaborate confident translation of the whole sentence without a critical edition.

What remains secure is the surrounding argument: exceptional forms, ritual substances and graphic representations furnish analogies for the distinguished status of a phoneme. ; \]

### The metaphysical handwriting examination

Imagine someone writing:

**स**

On a piece of paper.

Then announcing:

"I have captured the supreme phonemic power!"

Abhinavagupta asks:

"Do you mean you've written its conventional graphic representation?"

"Yes."

"And is the graphic form identical with the conscious power through which writing and reading become possible?"

The student looks at the paper.

Apparently the letter has a much deeper biography than expected.

That's the point.

A script can represent a phoneme.

A phoneme can participate in linguistic meaning.

And both can become objects of conscious apprehension.

But the Tantric argument concerns the underlying power by which they become manifest.

### The distinction from ordinary linguistics

This doesn't mean modern phonology should abandon the distinction between grapheme and phoneme.

Quite the opposite.

A rigorous reading of Abhinavagupta benefits from making those distinctions clear.

The claim is metaphysical:

**A phonemic power may be understood as a manifestation of consciousness, while its written and audible forms remain distinguishable expressions.**

Now, after this sequence of analogies, Abhinavagupta finally identifies the privileged letter.

The entire argument has been preparing us for it.

**स - sa.**

And the reason is astonishing.

It is associated with the nectar of supreme bliss.


## 8. उत्कर्षभागित्वम् - Why one letter may enjoy pre-eminence

The prose draws the general conclusion through:

**उत्कर्षभागित्वम्**

*Utkarṣabhāgitvam.*

"The condition of possessing eminence or distinction."

Let's dismantle this monster.

### Utkarṣa - उत्कर्ष

Excellence, elevation, pre-eminence.

Related to *ud-√kṛṣ*, to draw upward.

The formation suggests raising or elevating.

### Bhāgin - भागिन्

Sharing in, possessing, participating in.

Related to:

**भाग - bhāga**

Portion, share.

### -Tva - त्व

Abstract condition.

Thus:

**उत्कर्षभागित्व - utkarṣabhāgitva**

The state of possessing distinction.

### Why the abstract suffix matters

Abhinavagupta isn't merely saying:

"This letter is interesting."

He is describing the **principle of exceptional standing**.

Some forms are distinguished among forms.

Some substances among substances.

Some graphic or linguistic forms among others.

And now:

**Among all phonemes, this phoneme stands out.**

### Does that make the other phonemes worthless?

Absolutely not.

Earlier installments made it clear that the entire sacred phonemic network expresses Śakti.

The letter *sa* doesn't become a second god competing against the remaining alphabet.

Rather, it receives a special designation within the particular mantric construction of the Heart.

Think about the difference between:

**An element having a privileged function.**

And:

**An element existing independently of the whole system.**

Those are completely different claims.

### The cosmic alphabet awards ceremony

All the Sanskrit letters have gathered.

The vowels sit at the front.

The gutturals look extremely serious.

The retroflexes have formed their own committee.

Visarga is lurking by the exit.

Then the host announces:

"Special recognition for the letter **sa**!"

The consonants protest.

"Why him?"

"Because of his connection with supreme bliss and the expressive power of the entire phonemic network."

The letter *sa* steps forward.

A small hiss fills the hall.

Apparently this is the metaphysical equivalent of a standing ovation.

But the argument is going to become much deeper.

Because *sa* doesn't merely represent one isolated experience of pleasure.

Abhinavagupta associates it with the **nectar of supreme bliss**.

And that means we need to distinguish ordinary enjoyment, bodily expression and the nondual fullness of consciousness.


## 9. सकारस्तावत् परमानन्दामृतस्वभावः - Sa, the nectar of supreme bliss

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · P. 239

**सकारस्तावत् परमानन्दामृतस्वभावः।**

*Sakāras tāvat paramānandāmṛtasvabhāvaḥ.*

"To begin with, the phoneme *sa* has the nature of the nectar of supreme bliss."

Now we've reached the heart of the argument.

Let's dismantle every element.

### Sakāra - सकार

The phoneme *sa*.

From:

**स - sa**

And:

**कार - kāra**

A suffix used when naming phonemes.

Thus:

**सकार - sakāra**.

Masculine nominative singular:

**सकारः - sakāraḥ**.

Before *tāvat* it appears with the appropriate external sandhi.

### Tāvat - तावत्

First of all, to begin with, for the present.

An indeclinable.

### Parama - परम

Supreme, highest.

### Ānanda - आनन्द

Bliss.

The sandhi:

*parama + ānanda → paramānanda.*

### Amṛta - अमृत

Deathless nectar.

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**मृत - mṛta**

Dead.

Thus:

**अमृत - amṛta**

Deathless, immortal.

In Tantric symbolism, nectar or immortal essence.

### Svabhāva - स्वभाव

Own nature.

**स्व - sva**

Own.

**भाव - bhāva**

Being, condition, nature.

Thus:

**स्वभाव - svabhāva**.

Now the compound:

**परमानन्दामृतस्वभाव - paramānandāmṛtasvabhāva**

Having the nature of the nectar of supreme bliss.

### The metaphysical significance

Now here's where we must avoid a simplistic reading.

If *sa* has the nature of supreme bliss, does that mean an ordinary spoken **sa** always causes someone to experience unlimited ecstasy?

No such empirical conclusion follows.

The text is interpreting the phoneme through a sacred metaphysical correspondence.

Its significance concerns the supreme conscious power represented by the phoneme.

Not merely the measurable properties of a sound wave.

### Bliss versus pleasure

Now let's distinguish two terms.

**सुख - sukha**

Pleasure, ease, happiness.

**आनन्द - ānanda**

Bliss, delight, fullness.

These can overlap in ordinary Sanskrit.

But in the philosophical setting, *paramānanda* points beyond a merely fleeting sensory pleasure.

It concerns the supreme conscious fullness.

That distinction becomes particularly important when the text immediately turns to bodily signs of pleasure.

Because Abhinavagupta does not deny their significance.

He interprets them as manifestations through which the deeper bliss-power may be indicated.

### The divine bliss department

Imagine the ordinary person announcing:

"I have experienced pleasure."

The philosopher replies:

"Very good."

"Therefore I know the supreme nature of bliss."

"Not necessarily."

"But the pleasure was intense!"

"Intensity alone doesn't establish metaphysical realization."

The person is visibly disappointed.

Abhinavagupta's point is not that pleasure is evil.

It is that the ultimate nature of bliss isn't exhausted by one limited bodily event.

And that's why the next phrase matters.

The letter *sa* surges forth.

And gathers the entire alphabet into its manifestation.


## 10. समस्तं वर्णजालमाक्षिप्य - Sa draws in the entire network of phonemes

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 239

The text says that *sa*, in its upsurging manifestation, draws in:

**समस्तं वर्णजालम्**

*Samastaṃ varṇajālam.*

"The entire network of phonemes."

And the action is expressed by:

**आक्षिप्य - ākṣipya**

"Having drawn in, gathered or brought into its scope."

Let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Samasta - समस्त

Entire, all, complete.

### Varṇa - वर्ण

Phoneme.

### Jāla - जाल

Net, network, interconnected collection.

Thus:

**वर्णजाल - varṇajāla**

Network of phonemes.

Accusative singular:

**वर्णजालम् - varṇajālam**.

### Ākṣipya - आक्षिप्य

Having drawn in, brought into scope or attracted.

From:

**आ - ā**

Toward.

**√क्षिप् - kṣip**

To throw, cast or direct.

The absolutive:

**आक्षिप्य - ākṣipya**.

Now here's the important nuance.

The verb can mean throwing or casting, but here the context supports the sense of gathering or encompassing the alphabet.

### Ullasan - उल्लसन्

Shining forth, surging, bursting into manifestation.

From the *ud-√las* family.

The participial form describes the dynamic emergence of the phonemic power.

### Why the entire alphabet?

Because *sa* is not being treated as a mere isolated unit.

Within the *sauḥ* construction, it represents a power whose expansion is related to the entire phonemic network.

The individual phoneme is understood through the whole.

And the whole is manifest through differentiated elements.

### The connection with Spanda

Now remember the previous installments.

**उच्छलत्ता - ucchalattā**

Upsurging.

**स्पन्द - spanda**

Pulsation.

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission.

The same imagery has returned.

Manifestation emerges through the intrinsic power of consciousness.

It doesn't require an independent external material outside the supreme ground.

The phonemic network is one way of articulating this relationship.

### The cosmic alphabet vacuum cleaner

Imagine the letter *sa* suddenly expanding.

The vowels are pulled toward it.

The consonants begin sliding across the floor.

Anusvāra rolls under a table.

Visarga's two dots are nearly lost.

The entire Sanskrit alphabet gets swept into one enormous phonemic event.

The grammarian screams:

"That's not how ordinary pronunciation works!"

Correct.

Abhinavagupta isn't describing a literal acoustic vacuum cleaner.

He is describing the all-inclusive expressive capacity of the divine phonemic power.

The distinction matters.

And now comes the really remarkable claim.

Truth.

Happiness.

Prosperity.

Being.

All are related to that same underlying blissful power.

Yet the evidence he introduces is strangely bodily.

He describes sounds of pleasure, trembling and contraction and expansion of the sexual organs.

The Sanskrit is explicit.

And we shouldn't sanitize it.


## 11. सत्य-सुख-सम्पत्-सत्ता - Truth, happiness, prosperity and being

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S PROSE · P. 239

Now Abhinavagupta names a remarkable series of words.

**सत्य - satya**

Truth.

**सुख - sukha**

Happiness, pleasure, ease.

**सम्पत् - sampat**

Prosperity, attainment, flourishing.

**सत्ता - sattā**

Existence, being.

The words appear together in the expression:

**सत्यसुखसम्पत्सत्तादीनाम्**

*Satyasukhasaṃpatsattādīnām.*

"Of truth, happiness, prosperity, existence and the rest."

Now notice that each word has a familiar, conventionally distinct meaning.

Truth is not the same dictionary entry as pleasure.

Prosperity is not the same concept as existence.

But Abhinavagupta is interested in what he calls their **pāramārthika vapuḥ**: their ultimate form or nature.

### Satya - सत्य

True, real, truth.

Related to **sat**, being or existing, with the adjectival formation **-ya**.

The word can describe factual truth or genuine reality according to context.

### Sukha - सुख

Ease, pleasure, happiness.

The familiar explanation often connects *su-* with good or easy, and *kha* with an opening or space.

But the history of a word shouldn't be confused with every later philosophical interpretation attached to it.

Here the relevant meaning is happiness or pleasure.

### Sampat - सम्पत्

Prosperity, attainment, flourishing.

Related to *sam-√pad*, to arrive at, succeed or attain.

The compound undergoes regular changes when joined with following elements.

### Sattā - सत्ता

Being, existence.

From:

**सत् - sat**

Existing.

With the abstract formation:

**-ता - -tā**.

Thus:

**सत्ता - sattā**

The condition of existing.

### Ādi - आदि

Beginning with, and so forth.

The plural genitive at the end:

**-आदीनाम् - -ādīnām**

Shows that this is an open group of examples.

### The ultimate form

Now comes the key conceptual phrase:

**पारमार्थिकं वपुः**

*Pāramārthikaṃ vapuḥ.*

"The ultimate nature or form."

### Pāramārthika - पारमार्थिक

Pertaining to ultimate reality.

From:

**परमार्थ - paramārtha**

Ultimate reality, highest meaning.

The adjective:

**पारमार्थिक - pāramārthika**.

### Vapus - वपुस्

Body, form, nature.

Neuter noun.

The form:

**वपुः - vapuḥ**.

Now Abhinavagupta is not merely making a conventional dictionary argument.

He's not saying:

*Tatpravibhāgasaṃyamāt sarvabhūtarutajñānam.*

A close translation:

"Because word, meaning and cognition are superimposed upon one another, confusion results. Through concentrated discernment of their distinctions comes knowledge of the sounds of all beings."

This is genuinely **Yogasūtra 3.17**. It is independently transmitted in the Pātañjalayogaśāstra and its traditional commentary. ](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutras-with-commentaries/d/doc1216724.html); \]

Now we're going to dissect it properly.

Because this is an absolute grammatical monster.

And the philosophical point depends upon understanding the three terms being distinguished.

### Śabda - शब्द

Word, sound, linguistic expression.

Now the context is important.

*Śabda* can denote articulated speech, a meaningful verbal unit or sound more broadly.

The relevant distinction here concerns linguistic expression.

### Artha - अर्थ

Meaning, referent, object.

Now this word is wonderfully polysemous.

It can mean:

Meaning.

Purpose.

Object.

Value.

Thing intended.

In the present sūtra, it designates what the word is taken to signify.

### Pratyaya - प्रत्यय

Cognition, mental presentation, determination.

From the *prati-√i* family of arriving at or apprehending, within its established philosophical usage.

Now this is also a technical grammatical term for an affix.

But **in this sūtra**, *pratyaya* is a cognitive presentation, not a grammatical suffix.

That distinction matters enormously.

### The threefold relation

| Term | Sanskrit | What it designates here |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Word | **शब्द - śabda** | Linguistic or acoustic expression |
| Referent | **अर्थ - artha** | Object or meaning expressed |
| Cognition | **प्रत्यय - pratyaya** | Mental apprehension of that meaning |

Now here's the problem.

In ordinary experience, these can become conflated.

You hear the word:

**गौः - gauḥ**.

You think of a cow.

And the word, animal and mental presentation seem to form one familiar unit.

But they're distinguishable.

The sound isn't a biological cow.

The animal isn't the sound.

And your cognition of the cow isn't identical with the animal's material body.

Patañjali's sūtra examines precisely that conflation.

### Itaretara - इतरेतर

Mutual, one another.

The repetition of forms related to:

**इतर - itara**

Other.

Thus:

**इतरेतर - itaretara**

Mutual, reciprocal.

### Adhyāsa - अध्यास

Superimposition, placing one upon another.

From:

**अधि - adhi**

Upon.

**√अस् - as**

To throw or place, in this formation.

The ablative singular:

**अध्यासात् - adhyāsāt**.

Because of superimposition.

Thus:

**इतरेतराध्यासात् - itaretarādhyāsāt**

Because of mutual superimposition.

### Saṃkara - सङ्कर

Mixture, confusion, blending.

Masculine nominative singular:

**सङ्करः - saṃkaraḥ**.

Now don't confuse:

**सङ्कर - saṃkara**

Mixture, confusion.

With:

**शङ्कर - Śaṅkara**

An epithet of Śiva.

The consonants differ.

**स - sa**

Versus:

**श - śa**.

A tiny difference in writing.

A very significant difference in meaning.

### The first half

Now the sense is clear.

Word, object and cognition become confused through mutual superimposition.

The ordinary linguistic event contains distinguishable factors.

Yet we often experience them as a unified whole.

### Pravibhāga - प्रविभाग

Careful separation, discrimination.

**प्र - pra**

Thoroughly, forth.

**वि - vi**

Apart.

**भाग - bhāga**

Division.

Thus:

**प्रविभाग - pravibhāga**

Discrimination into distinct components.

### Saṃyama - संयम

Concentrated restraint or integration.

Now here is the specifically Yogic technical meaning.

In Patañjali's system, *saṃyama* refers to the combined application of:

**धारणा - dhāraṇā**

Concentration.

**ध्यान - dhyāna**

Meditation.

**समाधि - samādhi**

Absorption.

The combination is defined earlier in the third book of the *Yogasūtra*.

So it should not be translated merely as "thinking carefully for a few moments."

### Ablative singular

**संयमात् - saṃyamāt**

Through or because of concentrated discipline.

Now the combined compound:

**तत्प्रविभागसंयमात् - tatpravibhāgasaṃyamāt**

Through saṃyama upon the distinction among those elements.

### Sarva - सर्व

All.

### Bhūta - भूत

Being, creature.

### Ruta - रुत

Sound, call, cry, vocal expression.

Related to √**रु - ru**, to sound or cry out.

Thus:

**सर्वभूतरुत - sarvabhūtaruta**

The sounds of all beings.

### Jñāna - ज्ञान

Knowledge.

Thus:

**सर्वभूतरुतज्ञान - sarvabhūtarutajñāna**

Knowledge of the sounds of all beings.

### The traditional claim

Patañjali says that through *saṃyama* upon the distinctions among word, object and cognition, the yogin obtains knowledge of the sounds made by living beings.

This is a traditional extraordinary-knowledge claim.

Its presence in the text is certain.

Its empirical reliability is a separate question.

The passage is not independently verified evidence that a practitioner can understand every animal's communication.

### The cosmic linguistics laboratory

Imagine a yogin hearing a bird.

"Chirp."

An ordinary listener says:

"A bird made a sound."

The yogin attempts to discern:

The sound itself.

Its referent.

And the cognition through which it is understood.

Patañjali attributes extraordinary knowledge to perfected *saṃyama* on that distinction.

Now Abhinavagupta has borrowed the sūtra within a different metaphysical framework.

The distinction among word, meaning and cognition matters for both.

But the **philosophical systems are not identical**.

That's essential.


## 21. Why does Abhinavagupta quote a dualist Yoga text?

Now this is a genuinely excellent question.

Patañjali's Yoga is generally interpreted within a metaphysical structure involving the distinction between *puruṣa* and *prakṛti*.

The Recognition school presents a different account of ultimate reality, centred upon the self-revealing and sovereign nature of consciousness.

So why quote Patañjali?

Because the sūtra supplies a relevant distinction.

**Word, meaning and cognition can be distinguished despite their ordinary conflation.**

Abhinavagupta can use that distinction without adopting the entire metaphysics of classical Yoga.

### Different claims, same analytical point

Patañjali:

The distinction among sound, object and cognition is a legitimate subject for contemplative discrimination.

Abhinavagupta:

The phoneme participates in genuine expressive power, and the ultimate ground of meaningful expression belongs to consciousness.

These aren't identical theses.

But they intersect in the claim that linguistic experience has distinguishable components.

### A crucial nuance

Patañjali's sūtra doesn't say:

"Every isolated sound has one independently complete conventional meaning."

Nor does it prove that the letter **ga** uniquely denotes *gagana*, *gavaya* and *go*.

It concerns the discrimination of word, meaning and cognition.

Abhinavagupta invokes it to support his larger analysis of expressive power.

That's a legitimate historical use of a philosophical authority.

But we shouldn't retroactively turn Patañjali into a fully developed Trika metaphysician.

### The philosophical guest lecturer

Imagine Patañjali entering the classroom.

"I've come to discuss word, object and cognition."

Abhinavagupta says:

"Excellent."

"Are you adopting my entire metaphysics?"

"No."

"Then why am I here?"

"Because your analysis is relevant to my argument."

Patañjali takes a seat.

The two systems remain distinct.

But their insights can be brought into conversation.

That is how sophisticated Indian philosophical writing frequently operates.

**A commentator can appropriate an analytical distinction without accepting every premise of the source tradition.**

Now the next step is brilliantly practical.

Abhinavagupta turns to grammatical particles and suffixes.

Some of them are extremely short.

Some are represented by one written phonemic unit.

And yet they can contribute real meaning.

So perhaps the idea that meaningful expression always requires a long sequence of sounds isn't quite right.

Time for the smallest members of Sanskrit grammar to have their moment.


## 22. अकार-चकार - The tiniest grammatical expressions enter the argument

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · P. 240

Now the commentary turns to examples beginning with:

**अकार - akāra**

The vowel *a*.

And:

**चकार - cakāra**

The letter *ca*.

The relevant expression describes grammatical elements:

**एकवर्णात्मानः - ekavarṇātmānaḥ**

"Having the nature of a single *varṇa*."

This is the next stage in the argument.

An individual phonemic element need not be meaningless merely because it is short.

### Akāra - अकार

**अ - a**

The vowel.

**कार - kāra**

Phoneme-designating element.

Thus:

**अकार - akāra**.

### Cakāra - चकार

**च - ca**

The syllabic letter.

**कार - kāra**

Thus:

**चकार - cakāra**.

Now here's an important phonetic distinction.

The Sanskrit grammatical tradition's use of *varṇa* doesn't map perfectly onto modern claims about the number of IPA segments.

The written akṣara **च** represents a consonant with its inherent vowel.

The traditional expression *cakāra* names that phonemic letter-unit.

So don't automatically turn **ekavarṇātman** into the modern proposition that every example consists of precisely one independently segmented acoustic phone.

That would be anachronistic.

### Eka - एक

One.

### Varṇa - वर्ण

Phoneme or letter.

### Ātman - आत्मन्

Nature.

Now:

**एकवर्णात्मन् - ekavarṇātman**

Having the nature of a single phonemic unit.

Plural:

**एकवर्णात्मानः - ekavarṇātmānaḥ**.

The morphology is important.

The form ends with **-ānaḥ** because it belongs to the *-ātman* stem in the masculine plural.

### The obvious example: ca

Consider:

**रामश्च कृष्णश्च।**

*Rāmaś ca Kṛṣṇaś ca.*

"Rāma and Kṛṣṇa."

The particle **च - ca** is short.

It doesn't name another person standing beside Rāma and Kṛṣṇa.

Its function is conjunctive.

It joins the terms.

Now that is meaningful.

But its meaning isn't an independent third object.

That's precisely why the passage is important.

### The negative a-

Consider:

**अमृत - amṛta**

Deathless.

The initial **अ - a-** functions as a privative prefix.

It negates or reverses the relevant lexical property.

But here's a grammatical qualification.

A negative prefix in a compound is not necessarily an independent indeclinable word in ordinary sentence syntax.

So *a-* and *ca* shouldn't be treated as structurally identical merely because both are short.

Abhinavagupta's broader category includes different kinds of compact expressions.

### Meaning without an independently existing object

Now imagine saying:

"Not a pot."

The negation does not require a separately existing object called "not-a-pot" floating in the universe.

It modifies or excludes the relevant determination.

Similarly:

"Pot and cloth."

The conjunction doesn't name a third physical substance.

It connects two expressions.

Yet the negative and conjunction are meaningful.

That's the precise issue.

**Meaningful linguistic contribution need not always consist in naming an independently existing object.**

And that brings us to one of the most sophisticated semantic distinctions in the passage.

These grammatical operators have what Abhinavagupta calls an **asattvabhūta** character.

A non-substantive or non-object-like function.

That is not the same as saying they are completely meaningless.


## 23. निपात-विभक्ति - Particles and grammatical endings

The text now explicitly includes:

**निपात - nipāta**

Particles, indeclinables.

And:

**विभक्ति - vibhakti**

Grammatical inflection or case endings.

Now these are two genuinely important classes in Sanskrit grammar.

Let's learn them.

### Nipāta - निपात

Particle, indeclinable expression.

The traditional grammatical class includes items whose functions are not simply the naming of an object.

Examples include:

**च - ca**

And.

**वा - vā**

Or.

**हि - hi**

Indeed, because.

**अपि - api**

Also, even.

But these don't all consist of one phonemic unit.

Abhinavagupta's phrase says such elements **often** have short phonemic forms, with examples such as *a* and *ca*.

We shouldn't convert his statement into the false claim that every particle is one letter.

### Vibhakti - विभक्ति

Division, distinction, grammatical inflection.

In Sanskrit grammar, *vibhakti* can designate the sets of case and personal endings.

In this immediate argument, the point concerns their role as meaningful grammatical markers.

Let's use an example.

**रामः - rāmaḥ**

Rāma as nominative singular.

**रामम् - rāmam**

Rāma as accusative singular.

**रामेण - rāmeṇa**

By Rāma.

Now compare the forms.

The lexical stem remains recognizably related to *rāma-*.

But the endings contribute information about the word's grammatical role.

### The Paninian subtlety

A Sanskrit grammatical ending isn't always identical with the exact visible string in the final surface form.

For example, the nominative singular is conventionally associated with the grammatical affix **su**, whose phonological outcome undergoes further operations.

The final **ḥ** of *rāmaḥ* should not be treated as the entire original underlying affix without qualification.

That's why Sanskrit grammar distinguishes:

Underlying affix.

Morphophonological operation.

Surface form.

### Why this matters for the argument

A grammatical ending can contribute meaning or grammatical function without independently naming an object.

For example, an instrumental ending helps express a relation of agency, instrumentality or other case functions.

It doesn't necessarily denote an entirely separate physical entity.

### The difference between word meaning and grammatical function

Let's compare:

**घट - ghaṭa**

Pot.

This can name a conventional object-kind.

**च - ca**

And.

This joins expressions.

**न - na**

Not.

This negates.

A grammatical case ending helps establish a syntactic and semantic relation.

All can contribute to meaningful communication.

But they don't do so by the same mechanism.

### The cosmic grammar factory

Imagine a Sanskrit sentence factory.

Nouns arrive.

Verbs arrive.

Particles arrive.

Case endings arrive.

The manager asks:

"What does everyone contribute?"

The noun says:

"An object or entity-type."

The verb says:

"An action or state."

The conjunction says:

"A relation of combination."

The negative particle says:

"Exclusion or denial."

The instrumental ending says:

"A grammatical relation."

The manager looks impressed.

Then a philosopher says:

"So the meaningfulness of language isn't reducible to independently naming objects?"

Exactly.

And that is why Abhinavagupta's next phrase is so significant.

He describes the relevant grammatical expressions as turning away from *idantā*-objectified this-ness-and remaining closely related to the apprehending subject.

This is the Pratyabhijñā contribution to the argument.


## 24. इदन्तापराङ्मुखम् - Meaning that doesn't point toward a separate “this”

ABHINAVAGUPTA'S OWN PROSE · PP. 240-241

The relevant expression is:

**इदन्तापराङ्मुखम्**

*Idantāparāṅmukham.*

"Turned away from objectified this-ness."

This is a beautiful piece of Pratyabhijñā vocabulary.

Let's dissect it.

### Idam - इदम्

This.

The demonstrative pronoun.

### Idantā - इदन्ता

This-ness, objectified presentation.

The abstract condition of appearing as **idam**, "this."

Within Pratyabhijñā, *idantā* is often contrasted with:

**अहन्ता - ahantā**

I-ness.

Now this is not the same as saying every ordinary use of "this" is false.

The distinction concerns the mode in which something appears as an object over against a subject.

### Parāṅmukha - पराङ्मुख

Turned away from, facing away from.

**पराङ् - parāṅ**

Away, in another direction.

**मुख - mukha**

Face.

Thus:

**पराङ्मुख - parāṅmukha**

Facing away.

### The whole compound

**इदन्तापराङ्मुख - idantāparāṅmukha**

Not directed toward the objectified "this" as an independently standing entity.

Now the immediate context concerns grammatical particles and endings.

Such expressions can contribute meanings that aren't simply a separately objectified *this*.

### The philosophical significance

Consider the particle:

**च - ca**

And.

Its function is meaningful.

But it doesn't independently name a pot, person, tree or mountain.

The meaning is relational.

And Abhinavagupta describes such expressions through their dependence upon the meaningful cognitive act.

### Pramātṛpada - प्रमातृपद

The level or domain of the knowing subject.

**प्रमातृ - pramātṛ**

Knower.

**पद - pada**

State, position, level.

Thus:

**प्रमातृपद - pramātṛpada**

The level of the apprehending subject.

Now the prose says these short meaningful expressions are:

**प्रमातृपदलीनम् - pramātṛpadalīnam**

Absorbed or situated within the domain of the knower.

### Līna - लीन

Merged, absorbed, dissolved.

From √**ली - lī**, to dissolve or merge.

Now the point.

The meanings expressed by certain grammatical forms are not independently standing objects in the same way as the objects designated by ordinary nouns.

Their operation is closely connected with the subject's meaningful determination.

### Asattvabhūta - असत्त्वभूत

Non-substantive in nature.

Let's learn this carefully.

**अ - a-**

Not.

**सत्त्व - sattva**

Being, entity-status, substantial existence in the relevant grammatical-semantic usage.

**भूत - bhūta**

Become, having the condition of.

Thus:

**असत्त्वभूत - asattvabhūta**

Having a non-substantive character.

Now here's a crucial warning.

**Asattvabhūta does not automatically mean utterly nonexistent or meaningless.**

In the present context, it's more precise to describe the relevant expressions as lacking the same independent object-denoting character as substantive nouns.

They still contribute to meaningful language.

### Negation and conjunction

Abhinavagupta invokes:

**निषेध - niṣedha**

Negation, exclusion.

And:

**समुच्चय - samuccaya**

Combination, conjunction, aggregation.

These are exactly the sorts of functions we're discussing.

### Niṣedha - निषेध

From *ni-√ṣidh*, to prohibit or deny.

### Samuccaya - समुच्चय

From *sam-ud-√ci*, to gather together.

Now the contrast:

Negation excludes.

Conjunction combines.

But neither has to name an independently existing substance.

### The grammatical operator that demands its own throne

Imagine the conjunction **ca** declaring:

"I am a thing!"

The noun says:

"What sort of thing?"

"A combination."

"Of what?"

"Whatever expressions I'm joining."

"So your role depends upon a relation?"

"Yes."

"Then you're not functioning exactly like an ordinary object-naming noun?"

"No."

The conjunction looks devastated.

It had been hoping for a throne.

But it possesses something arguably more interesting.

**A meaningful function within the structure of conscious linguistic apprehension.**

And now Abhinavagupta says that Bhartṛhari explains the same underlying issue.

That is the next major quotation.

But the exact content is subtle.

Because Bhartṛhari isn't simply saying "one letter means everything."

He is cataloguing competing philosophical accounts of what constitutes a sentence.

And only the first half of his verse is quoted here.


## 25. भर्तृहरि - Bhartṛhari enters the argument

INDEPENDENT QUOTATION · VĀKYAPADĪYA 2.2 · KASHMIR SERIES P. 241

Abhinavagupta introduces the authority with:

**तत्रभवतो भर्तृहरेः।**

*Tatrabhavato bhartṛhareḥ.*

"Of the venerable Bhartṛhari."

Now this is a respectful reference.

And the next words introduce a verse from Vākyapadīya.

The quoted half-verse in the Kashmir Series is:

**पदमाद्यं पृथक्सर्वं पदं साकाङ्क्षमित्यपि।**

*Padam ādyaṃ pṛthak sarvaṃ padaṃ sākāṅkṣam ity api.*

A close rendering:

"The initial word $$may be regarded as the sentence$$, or each word separately, or a word requiring completion."

But this needs immediate qualification.

The line is part of a list of competing theories about **what constitutes a sentence**.

It does not by itself state Bhartṛhari's final preferred doctrine.

### The crucial independent reading

The independently transmitted *Vākyapadīya* 2.2 reads:

**पदमाद्यं पृथक्सर्वं पदं सापेक्षमित्यपि।**

*Padam ādyaṃ pṛthak sarvaṃ padaṃ sāpekṣam ity api.*

And the second half is:

**वाक्यं प्रति मतिर्भिन्ना बहुधा न्यायदर्शिनाम्।**

*Vākyaṃ prati matir bhinnā bahudhā nyāyadarśinām.*

"Philosophers hold many different views concerning the sentence."

The independent text therefore has **sāpekṣam**, while the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* witnesses give **sākāṅkṣam**.

Both are meaningful readings, but they are not the same form. ; ; \]

Let's dismantle the entire argument.

### Pada - पद

Word.

But *pada* can also mean a position, foot, quarter of a verse or state.

Here the context is grammatical.

**पदम् - padam**

A word.

### Ādya - आद्य

Initial, first.

Thus:

**पदमाद्यम् - padam ādyam**

The first word.

### Pṛthak - पृथक्

Separately, individually.

### Sarvam - सर्वम्

All, every.

### Sāpekṣa - सापेक्ष

Dependent upon something else, requiring another element.

**स - sa-**

With.

**अपेक्षा - apekṣā**

Expectation, dependence.

Thus:

**सापेक्ष - sāpekṣa**

Dependent, requiring completion.

Neuter singular:

**सापेक्षम् - sāpekṣam**.

### Sākāṅkṣa - साकाङ्क्ष

Having expectation or syntactic expectancy.

**आकाङ्क्षा - ākāṅkṣā**

Expectation, desire, need for completion.

Thus:

**साकाङ्क्ष - sākāṅkṣa**

Expectant, requiring something more.

Neuter singular:

**साकाङ्क्षम् - sākāṅkṣam**.

Now here's the interesting difference.

*Sāpekṣa* emphasizes dependence.

*Sākāṅkṣa* emphasizes expectancy.

They are semantically close in discussions of sentence structure.

But the variation is still real.

### The second half's crucial word

**वाक्य - vākya**

Sentence.

### Mati - मति

View, opinion, understanding.

From √**मन् - man**, to think.

### Bhinna - भिन्न

Different, divided.

From √**भिद् - bhid**, to split.

### Bahudhā - बहुधा

In many ways.

### Nyāyadarśin - न्यायदर्शिन्

One who examines through reasoning or philosophical method.

**न्याय - nyāya**

Reasoning, method, logical principle.

**दर्शिन् - darśin**

Seeing, examining.

Genitive plural:

**न्यायदर्शिनाम् - nyāyadarśinām**.

Thus the second half says that philosophical authorities disagree about the sentence's nature.

### The eight theories

Bhartṛhari's opening two verses of the second book present several competing understandings of the sentence.

These include theories identifying it with:

A verb.

A collection of words.

A universal residing in the collection.

An indivisible linguistic unity.

A sequence.

A cognitive synthesis.

The initial word.

Or individual words requiring completion.

The exact classification is discussed in scholarship on *Vākyapadīya* II.1-2.

### Why Abhinavagupta quotes it

Now we can understand the connection.

Abhinavagupta has been discussing the meaningfulness of individual phonemic expressions and grammatical operators.

Bhartṛhari's treatment establishes that the meaningful unit of language is philosophically contested.

A sentence need not be understood simply as a mechanical accumulation of independently complete words.

Nor does everyone agree on exactly how linguistic parts relate to the whole.

The quotation supports Abhinavagupta's exploration of the relation between linguistic units, expectation and expressive meaning.

### A crucial correction to a tempting interpretation

It would be inaccurate to say:

"Bhartṛhari, Vākyapadīya 2.2, proves that every single phoneme contains every meaning."

That is **not** what the verse says.

It catalogues views of the sentence.

Its relevance is to the debate over linguistic parts and the meaningful whole.

The broader Vākyapadīya contains discussions directly relevant to the relation between phonemes, words and cognition, but this particular citation must be explained according to its actual wording.

### The greatest philosophical grammar seminar

Imagine Bhartṛhari entering the room.

Abhinavagupta says:

"Can a meaningful whole be understood from parts?"

Bhartṛhari replies:

"First, we should identify the competing theories of the sentence."

A grammarian produces eight possibilities.

Another grammarian objects.

A third begins discussing the indivisible *sphoṭa*.

The original question has somehow become a three-day philosophical conference.

And Abhinavagupta is delighted.

Because the entire dispute illustrates why language cannot simply be reduced to a collection of dead acoustic pieces.

But here's the important subtlety.

**Bhartṛhari's argument and Abhinavagupta's nondual Śaiva explanation should be compared, not automatically identified.**

Their accounts of linguistic unity and ultimate reality have points of contact.

But the historical texts retain their own conceptual contexts.


## 26. Metre laboratory - Bhartṛhari's verse is anuṣṭubh

Now let's scan the authentic independently transmitted verse.

**पदमाद्यं पृथक्सर्वम्**

*pa-dam-ā-dyaṃ-pṛ-thak-sar-vam*

8 syllables

**पदं सापेक्षमित्यपि**

*pa-daṃ-sā-pek-ṣam-it-ya-pi*

Because nonduality is not the assertion that everything is indistinguishable mush.

It is the recognition that differentiation itself is a power of the undivided.

An ocean can generate different waves without surrendering its oceanhood. More precisely, in Pratyabhijñā, consciousness can manifest an indefinitely differentiated world without acquiring a second substance outside itself.

But Abhinavagupta wants more than a wave analogy.

He wants to explain why certain differentiations have particular *capacities*.

### First, the grammar

Break open the beginning.

**yathā** - “just as,” introducing an analogy. It promises a corresponding *evam*, “so,” later in the sentence.

**ca** - “and,” connecting this argument to the preceding demonstration of universal divine sovereignty.

**ākṛti** - form, figure, configuration, appearance; conventionally analysed through the prefix *ā-* and √*kṛ*, “make, produce.”

**madhya** - middle, midst, interior.

**ākṛtimadhya eva** - precisely among forms, within the domain of forms themselves.

Notice **eva**. The comparison is not between an extraordinary sacred form and some wholly separate supernatural universe. The extraordinary form arises within the same domain as ordinary forms.

Then we encounter the compound:

- catur-bhuja - four-armed. *Catur* means four; *bhuja* means arm, and is also connected with √*bhuj*, to bend or enjoy.
- tri-nayana - three-eyed. *Tri* is three; *nayana*, eye, is related to √*nī*, to lead, with an instrumental sense of what guides perception.
- pūrṇa - full, filled, complete.
- kṛśa - thin, lean, emaciated.
- ādi - beginning with, and so forth.

The whole sequence qualifies **ākṛtayaḥ**, feminine nominative plural of *ākṛti*: “forms.”

There is a striking juxtaposition here.

A four-armed figure and a three-eyed figure are recognisably superordinary iconographic forms.

But *pūrṇa* and *kṛśa* concern bodily condition, fullness and thinness.

The point is therefore wider than identifying conventional deity-images.

**A form can stand out by possessing an exceptional configuration or by expressing a marked condition.**

Now notice the writing.

In **चतुर्भुज**, the sequence र्भ represents the consonant *r* followed by *bh*; the repha marks the preceding *r*. In **त्रिनयन**, त्र combines त् + र. In **पूर्ण**, र्ण represents र् + ण, with the vowel ū on प.

And in **कृश**, the little vocalic ṛ-sign below क is not simply an ordinary *r* plus an *i* sound. It represents the independent Sanskrit vowel ऋ in its dependent form.

These distinctions matter because, later in the passage, Abhinavagupta will move from visible letter-shapes to the more fundamental status of phonemes.

For now he is speaking about differentiated forms.

### Then the intoxicants arrive

He continues with a second comparison: among substances there are things such as **surā** and **āsava**.

Both belong to the vocabulary of intoxicating or fermented preparations, though their precise preparation and ritual classifications depend on context.

Why introduce them?

Because certain Kaula ritual substances possess marked significance within a particular religious discipline.

In the preceding pages Abhinavagupta has challenged rigid ideas of purity and impurity, arguing that these are conditioned by convention, ritual orientation, and the degree of recognition.

A substance may be classified as prohibited in one ritual order and sacramentally significant in another.

This doesn't mean that intoxication automatically produces liberation. Nor is the passage a general instruction to drink alcohol.

Rather, its philosophical function is to deny that a worldly object's inherited classification exhausts its possible relation to consciousness.

The same object can participate in different systems of meaning and practice.

And those systems are themselves manifestations of divine freedom.

**Abhinavagupta's trick is that he refuses to let the absolute become a bland denial of relative distinctions.**

He doesn't say, “All objects are Śiva, therefore every ritual difference is pointless.”

He says, in effect, that differences can be meaningful precisely because they manifest Śiva's powers.

Your spiritual philosophy has somehow become more sophisticated by allowing an alcoholic beverage into an ontological argument.

Marvelous.


## II. The Written Letter and the Real Phoneme: The First Serious Textual Problem

Abhinavagupta now draws a comparison with written characters and the more fundamental ground of letters.

This is where we must be extremely careful.

The 1918 Kashmir Series main text has the difficult sequence:

**लिप्यक्षराणीव सती अवर्णभूमिः**

*lipyakṣarāṇīva satī avarṇabhūmiḥ*

Vasudeva's GRETIL transcription instead presents:

**लिप्यक्षराणीव सत्यवर्णभूमिम्**

*lipyakṣarāṇīva satyavarṇabhūmim*

These are not equivalent Sanskrit sentences.

The 1918 apparatus additionally reports manuscript readings corresponding to *paravarṇabhūmim* and *satyavarṇabhūmim*. The latter corresponds closely to what appears in GRETIL. The 1918 printed reading is syntactically difficult, so we cannot simply declare it the unquestionable original and build an entire philosophical interpretation around its grammar. ; ; \]

The common philosophical point is nevertheless recoverable from the comparison and its continuation.

A written character is not identical with the full reality of the phoneme it represents.

Think about the letter स.

You can write it in Devanāgarī.

You can represent it as *s* in IAST.

You can engrave it in stone.

You can write it in sand, or imagine a luminous form of it in the body.

Those are different graphic or imaginal presentations of something recognisable as a Sanskrit sound-unit.

But Abhinavagupta wants to investigate the status of *varṇa* at a still more fundamental level: its relation to consciousness, expressive power, and the unfolding of manifestation.

Here it is important not to collapse three distinct notions:

A **lipyakṣara** is a character in a writing system.

A **varṇa** is a phonemic unit in Sanskrit phonological analysis, although *varṇa* also has other uses, including colour and class.

An **akṣara** can mean a syllabic unit or letter, and in other Sanskrit contexts can also mean the imperishable.

These terms have overlapping histories, but they are not always interchangeable.

And Abhinavagupta is emphatically not proving that Sanskrit's particular written shapes are the metaphysical foundation of reality.

Devanāgarī is a script.

Śāradā is a script.

A phoneme is not simply either script.

The metaphysical interpretation concerns the expressive structure of consciousness, not the proposition that Śiva personally designed the Unicode character set.

Although, given the number of conjuncts in Sanskrit, you might be forgiven for thinking the gods outsourced typography to a particularly vindictive demon.

### Why the comparison matters

Let us reconstruct the analogy.

Within the domain of forms, certain configurations are extraordinary.

Within the domain of substances, certain materials are ritually extraordinary.

Within the domain of phonemes, certain sounds have extraordinary expressive or mantric significance.

Thus Abhinavagupta can say that some phenomena possess an exceptional *utkarṣa*, a kind of prominence or superiority, without abandoning his universal nonduality.

His position is not:

“Only the sacred objects are actually divine.”

It is:

“Everything participates in the divine, but particular manifestations may disclose or activate that reality in distinctive ways.”

This is a crucial distinction for understanding mantra.

If every phoneme is a manifestation of consciousness, mantra is not a magical exception to the universe.

It is a specialised mode of engaging powers already implicit in manifestation.

And now the principal defendant enters the courtroom.

One consonant.

One fucking consonant.

**स.**


## III. Sakāra: The Letter That Apparently Contains an Entire Ocean of Bliss

### Kashmir Series p. 239 - Abhinavagupta's prose

His claim is direct:

**सकारस्तावत् परमानन्दामृतस्वभावः**

*sakāras tāvat paramānandāmṛtasvabhāvaḥ*

“The phoneme *sa*, to begin with, has the essential nature of the nectar of supreme bliss.”

There it is.

One minute you are learning the alphabet.

The next minute your teacher says the dental sibilant is an ocean of immortal ecstasy.

The average Sanskrit instructor is trying to explain why a compound is masculine rather than neuter.

Abhinavagupta is conducting an ontological investigation into a hissing sound that apparently involves the very essence of supreme bliss.

And, infuriatingly, he does have an argument.

### First, the phonetics of स

We need to distinguish the three Sanskrit sibilants.

# स

sa

**Dental sibilant**

Formed with the tongue near the upper teeth or dental ridge. The unvoiced airflow creates audible friction. This is the phoneme central to the present passage.


# श

śa

**Palatal sibilant**

A distinct sound, traditionally associated with a more palatal tongue position.


# ष

ṣa

**Retroflex sibilant**

Produced with the tongue curled or retracted toward the postalveolar region.

These are separate Sanskrit phonemes.

**Sakāra is स, not श and not ष.**

The distinction is absolutely essential to this passage.

You cannot simply replace them because the mystical theory associates phonemes with consciousness. Sanskrit phonology does not dissolve into mystical enthusiasm.

Indeed, the fact that they differ is philosophically significant: each specific phonemic form is one articulation within the expressive totality.

Now the deeper point.

The consonant *s* is sustained by an outward flow of breath passing through a narrowed articulation.

You can prolong the hissing component.

The physical sound is a vibration of air.

But in Abhinavagupta's system, phonetic production is the final audible manifestation of processes whose more fundamental levels are cognitive and expressive.

We previously examined the levels of speech:

**parā → paśyantī → madhyamā → vaikharī**

Not four independent languages, but differentiated ways in which expressive consciousness becomes manifest.

At the *vaikharī* level, the phoneme is an articulated sound.

At the level of *madhyamā*, there is an internal differentiation of language and meaning without requiring audible speech.

At *paśyantī*, differentiation is more deeply unified.

At *parā*, expression belongs inseparably to consciousness's primordial self-awareness.

Therefore, the phoneme's ultimate nature is not exhausted by the movement of tongue, teeth, breath, and vocal apparatus.

This is the conceptual background for calling *sa* a manifestation of supreme bliss.

It is not an experimental finding that pronouncing /s/ causes supernatural physiology.

It is an interpretation of a phoneme's place in the Trika account of manifestation.

### Now dismantle paramānandāmṛtasvabhāva

This is an exceptionally beautiful compound.

**parama** - supreme, highest, unsurpassed.

**ānanda** - bliss, delight, fullness of enjoyment.

**amṛta** - nectar of immortality; literally “not dead,” from privative *a-* \+ *mṛta*, the past participle of √*mṛ*, to die.

**svabhāva** - own nature, intrinsic condition, characteristic being.

The compound can be understood as describing something whose own nature is the nectar of supreme bliss.

Why not merely *ānanda*?

Because, in this system, the bliss under discussion is not simply a pleasant feeling accompanying some temporary desirable object.

The central issue is the supreme subject's self-presence.

Ordinary pleasure involves some combination of sensation, desire, fulfilment, expectation, interpretation, and memory.

But Abhinavagupta wants us to understand the very possibility of experience's self-disclosing immediacy.

Consciousness does not require an additional light to make it manifest.

It does not have to be observed by another consciousness in order to be experienced.

Its self-manifestation is already the basis of the appearance of objects.

And the bliss of this self-manifestation is inseparable from its freedom.

This is why *ānanda* and *svātantrya* are so intimately related throughout our readings.

A limited subject can enjoy something.

The supreme subject manifests the very distinction between enjoyer and enjoyed.

And can recognise both as its own powers.

The pleasure of eating a mango is not identical to the intrinsic fullness of Śiva-consciousness.

But the very fact that mango-pleasure appears, is savoured, and is known already presupposes that conscious luminosity.

In Abhinavagupta's vocabulary, the deeper *ānanda* is not dependent on possessing the mango.

Which is exceptionally convenient if your mango has been eaten by somebody else.

### The Sanskrit sandhi

*parama + ānanda* gives **paramānanda**.

The final short *a* and the following long *ā* coalesce into long *ā*.

Then:

*paramānanda + amṛta* gives **paramānandāmṛta**.

Again, adjacent *a* vowels combine into long *ā*.

There is no mystical exemption from Sanskrit sandhi.

Even the supreme bliss of consciousness must comply with the vowel-combination rules.

And **svabhāva** is built from *sva*, one's own, and *bhāva*, being or becoming, connected with √*bhū*, to be or become.

This is a metaphysically loaded choice.

Abhinavagupta is not saying that a teacher arbitrarily assigns bliss to the phoneme.

He presents this as its inherent character within the framework of the mantra being explained.

A major difference.


## IV. The Entire Alphabet Comes Rushing Out Behind It

Abhinavagupta continues with the participle and finite verb **ullasan ... ullasati**, together with **samastaṃ varṇajālam ākṣipya**.

The image is extraordinary.

The *sa* sound shines forth, drawing in or implicating the entire network of phonemes, and shines forth in that fullness.

Let's unpack it.

**ullasan** is a masculine nominative singular present participle from *ud-* \+ √*las*, shining forth, flashing, becoming manifest.

Its finite counterpart **ullasati** is present indicative, third-person singular: “it shines forth,” “it becomes radiant,” “it springs into manifestation.”

The repetition is emphatic.

**samasta** - entire, altogether assembled.

**varṇa** - phoneme or sound-unit.

**jāla** - net, network, interconnection.

**varṇajāla** - the network or totality of phonemes.

**ākṣipya** - an absolutive from *ā-* \+ √*kṣip*, whose meanings include throwing, drawing toward, bringing into a situation, or implying.

An absolutive expresses a subordinate action completed relative to the principal action: “having drawn in,” “having implicated.”

The argument is not simply that the letter S makes a nice noise.

Its particular manifestation is said to presuppose or implicate the broader system of manifestation.

### The key distinction: part and totality

Normally, when we look at a phoneme, we see a component.

The sound *s* is part of a word.

That word is part of a sentence.

That sentence belongs to a language.

That language belongs to human communication.

We start with a little piece and reconstruct an increasingly large totality.

Abhinavagupta's fundamental direction is different.

Consciousness is not first assembled from isolated perceptual and linguistic components.

Rather, its expressive capacities are differentiated into particular manifestations.

The phoneme is therefore not an ontologically independent fragment.

It is a particular articulation of the same conscious power that makes the entire phonemic network possible.

The distinction is subtle.

He is **not** asserting that every audible *s* literally contains every other acoustic frequency.

Nor is he claiming that someone can reconstruct every dictionary entry by recording one consonant.

The inclusion is metaphysical and expressive, not a claim about a physical audio spectrum.

This is where *sarvasarvātmakatva*, the principle that each is in some sense constituted through all, becomes relevant.

A particular manifestation is not severed from its encompassing ground.

Everything is not literally the same object.

Everything participates in the same ultimately undivided consciousness.

Consider an image in your awareness.

You see a tree.

The tree is one specific visual form.

But its appearing is inseparable from the conscious field in which it appears.

You do not have to append consciousness to the tree afterwards, like installing an optional plugin.

The tree's appearing is already a conscious event.

For Abhinavagupta the same principle applies to the emergence of a phoneme.

The sound is particular.

Its manifestness is not separate from the one illuminating consciousness.

Now comes the important question.

Why **this** particular phoneme?

Why *sa*?

Why not *ka*, *pa*, or the desperately unfortunate conjunct *kṣa*?

Because we are working within a specific mantra-analysis.

The *sa* is being interpreted as the opening component of the supreme heart-mantra **सौः - sauḥ**.

The complete mantra includes **sa**, **au**, and **visarga**.

But they do not perform identical conceptual roles.

The passage develops *sa* first.

It will then make the connection to *au* and visarga explicit.

And, crucially, Abhinavagupta does not abandon the earlier argument about the totality of phonemes simply because this particular mantra has privileged status.

All phonemes belong to consciousness.

Some are specially configured as the constituents of this mantra.

Now we move from the consonant to the most unexpectedly physical part of the entire discussion.


## V. The Hiss, the Trembling, and the Mystical Physiology of Enjoyment

### Kashmir Series pp. 239-240

Abhinavagupta now makes a connection that practically guarantees you will never read a standard Sanskrit grammar textbook quite the same way again.

The passage refers to:

**सीत्कारसमुल्लासशेपकम्पवराङ्गसङ्कोचविकासोपलक्ष्यम्**

*sītkārasamullāsaśepakampavarāṅgasaṃkocavikāsopalakṣyam*

This is the 1918 edition's *śepa* reading. GRETIL gives *śepha*. Both are legitimate Sanskrit lexical forms for the male sexual organ; the variation does not by itself demonstrate a different philosophical interpretation. ; ; \]

What is being described?

Audible expressions of pleasure, trembling of the sexual organ, and contraction and expansion of bodily parts.

Yes.

Abhinavagupta is discussing erotic physiology in an argument about the Sanskrit alphabet.

Because apparently the distance between phonology and sex is about three lines in a Kashmir Śaiva commentary.

But the argument is more interesting than the joke.

### Begin with sītkāra

**सीत्कार - sītkāra**

A hissing or drawn-in exclamation, often associated with excitement, pleasure, surprise, pain, or other intense bodily responses, depending on context.

It is a sound-word.

Its precise pronunciation is therefore important.

The long **ī** is not the short vowel *i*.

The compound includes the sound-imitating element *sīt* and **kāra**, an act of making or producing, connected with √*kṛ*, to do or make.

What fascinates Abhinavagupta is the relation between intensely felt experience and spontaneous expression.

A person experiences overwhelming delight.

There may be an involuntary hiss, a sudden intake of breath, a bodily tremor, an alteration in muscular contraction.

The experience has not necessarily been preceded by an articulated conceptual judgement.

The body is already participating in the expression of felt significance.

This matters because the Trika theory of speech does not reduce expression to deliberately formed sentences.

Expression begins deeper than propositional language.

Think of laughter.

You hear a joke.

Sometimes the laugh occurs before you could explain why it is funny.

You could subsequently analyse the joke's conceptual incongruity, syntax, social assumptions, and relationship to the semantic expectations of the audience.

But the laugh has already happened.

Your body has issued its philosophical commentary.

And, frankly, it is considerably shorter than Abhinavagupta's.

### Why bodily trembling?

**kampa**, from √*kamp*, means trembling or shaking.

The relevant compound joins *śepa* or *śepha* with *kampa*.

In its immediate context, it is a specifically sexual physiological reference.

We shouldn't euphemise the Sanskrit out of embarrassment.

Nor should we inflate it into a detailed yogic practice manual that Abhinavagupta has not provided at this point.

His examples are signs of bodily arousal and pleasure.

The wider compound includes **saṃkoca** and **vikāsa**.

This is particularly remarkable given the philosophy we've been examining.

**saṃkoca** - contraction, constriction, drawing together.

**vikāsa** - expansion, opening, unfolding.

The former is related to the root √*kuc* with the prefix *sam-*; the latter to √*kas* with *vi-*.

In Trika metaphysics, contraction and expansion are not merely mechanical processes.

*Saṃkoca* can describe consciousness's self-limitation.

*Vikāsa* can describe its opening or manifestation.

But these words also have perfectly ordinary physical meanings.

That allows Abhinavagupta's argument to move between levels without asserting they are identical in every respect.

A muscular contraction is not automatically the metaphysical process of universal manifestation.

A moment of sexual pleasure is not automatically liberation.

Rather, bodily expressions of delight can furnish examples through which the relation between felt experience, spontaneous expression, and the underlying conscious power becomes intelligible.

### What exactly is being identified?

This is the crucial philosophical problem.

Suppose a pleasurable sensation arises.

One explanation is physiological: particular neural, autonomic, hormonal, muscular, and sensory processes contribute to the episode.

Another is phenomenological: there is an experienced quality of delight.

Another is conceptual: the person recognises or interprets the event as pleasurable.

Abhinavagupta's metaphysics addresses a more fundamental question.

**What makes the entire episode manifest as something experienced?**

He would not regard consciousness as merely another object appearing alongside the bodily processes.

Consciousness is their condition of appearing at all.

His account then asks whether the delight associated with the experience can reveal something about consciousness's intrinsic nature.

That does not establish his metaphysical interpretation as a modern physiological fact.

But it explains why the body matters to his philosophy.

The body is not an embarrassing accident that the spiritual person must somehow escape.

Its sensations can become occasions for recognising the subject whose freedom is expressed through them.

### And what about the letter S?

The sibilant in *sītkāra* brings the argument back to phonetic expression.

The sound is intimately associated here with the spontaneous articulation of intense experience.

Abhinavagupta connects that expressive phenomenon with the *sakāra* of the mantra.

Yet we must distinguish ordinary linguistic evidence from tantric interpretation.

Linguistically, the fact that *sītkāra* begins with a sibilant does not prove that *s* inherently means bliss.

A Sanskrit word's initial letter is not ordinarily a complete lexical definition.

Abhinavagupta is claiming something stronger and more specialised about phonemic expressive power within his metaphysical framework.

His language is therefore neither a modern acoustic theory nor ordinary dictionary etymology.

It is an attempt to uncover the metaphysical significance of expression itself.

And now we reach a particularly provocative claim.


## VI. Satya, Sukha, Sampat, Sattā: The Words Are Not Merely Illusory

Abhinavagupta invokes a cluster of words:

**सत्य · सुख · सम्पत् · सत्ता**

*satya · sukha · sampat · sattā*

Truth. Pleasure or happiness. Prosperity or attainment. Being or existence.

His point concerns their **pāramārthika** reality-their ultimately real nature-and their *amāyīya* character.

This is easily misunderstood.

It does **not** mean that any ordinary sentence containing the word *satya* is automatically an absolute truth.

Nor does it mean that the mere pronunciation of *sukha* produces unlimited happiness.

He is distinguishing the underlying reality of these experiences and meanings from their restricted appearance within conditioned understanding.

### Satya - सत्य

From **sat**, “being” or “real,” connected with √*as*, “to be,” with the adjectival formation *-ya*.

The word signifies truth, reality, or truthfulness, depending on its grammatical and philosophical setting.

In writing, notice the conjunct **त्य**.

The त् has no independent following *a*. It joins य to form *tya*.

So:

**सत्य - satya**, not *sataya*.

The difference between writing and phonology should already be becoming clearer.

The visible character sequence represents a phonological structure that is not identical with the separate pronunciation of each consonant plus its default inherent vowel.

Now the metaphysics.

Ordinarily, truth means a judgement's correspondence to some state of affairs, or its correctness within a particular epistemological framework.

But the question of *satya* in Abhinavagupta's argument reaches the status of the manifestness of what exists.

What is present must be present somehow.

What is known must be manifest.

His doctrine does not imply that every judgement is accurate.

A mistaken perception is still a real occurrence of awareness, even though its content can be erroneous.

Consider seeing a rope as a snake.

The snake may not be there.

But the appearance of a snake has undeniably occurred.

Recognition concerns the luminous reality of appearing, not the wholesale elimination of distinctions between correct and incorrect knowledge.

This is a foundational Pratyabhijñā distinction.

**The reality of an appearance is not identical with the truth of every proposition about its object.**

Your mind can be spectacularly mistaken.

Consciousness has nevertheless successfully manifested the spectacle.

### Sukha - सुख

*Su-* is a favourable or good-forming element.

*Kha* means an opening, cavity, or space in a range of Sanskrit usages.

The word *sukha* is established as happiness, ease, comfort, or pleasure.

Traditional explanations sometimes connect it with a well-functioning opening in a wheel or axle assembly, but historical etymological claims require caution.

We don't need that derivation to understand the passage.

What matters here is the difference between a particular pleasant object and the deeper reality of experienced delight.

Suppose you drink a wonderfully syrupy oolong tea.

The smell opens.

The taste lingers.

There is an immediately felt satisfaction.

Ordinarily you say, “This tea is delightful.”

Which, of course, it may be.

But from the Pratyabhijñā perspective, the object alone does not explain the fact that delight is consciously felt.

The tea participates in an experience.

That experience manifests within consciousness.

And its being experienced as pleasurable presupposes the self-disclosing character of consciousness.

Abhinavagupta is not denying the tea.

He is asking why its pleasures are intelligible as experiences at all.

And then he drives the question deeper.

Can the subject recognise the delight not as something ultimately imported from outside itself, but as a restricted manifestation of its own intrinsic fullness?

This does not make every sensation pleasant.

It does not erase pain.

It is not an excuse for pretending that difficult experiences are secretly enjoyable.

It is a specific metaphysical claim about the deeper nature of the experiencing subject.

### Sampat - सम्पत्

From **sam-** \+ √*pad*, meaning to attain, arrive, or come into a particular condition.

The noun can mean attainment, success, prosperity, good fortune, or wealth.

Notice the conjunct **म्प**.

The preceding nasal consonant joins the following *p*. In pronunciation, the nasal is homorganic with the labial stop.

The word's restricted meaning might involve material prosperity.

But *sampat* can also suggest a fuller condition of attainment.

In the metaphysical context, the important distinction is again between particular possessions and intrinsic fullness.

You might have £10,000.

You might have £10 million.

You might acquire all the objects you thought would make you happy.

But acquisition does not logically entail the final abolition of desire.

Because a desire satisfied at one moment can be followed by another.

Abhinavagupta's notion of fullness is not an indefinitely growing inventory of possessions.

It concerns the nature of the subject and its self-recognition.

You can own twelve palaces and remain existentially terrified.

You can sit in a hut and recognise a freedom not dependent on the contents of any palace.

The point is not economic.

It is ontological.

### Sattā - सत्ता

This is perhaps the most philosophically significant of the cluster.

*Sattā* is existence, being, or actuality.

It is related to **sat** with an abstract-noun formation.

The double त in **सत्ता** is represented by the conjunct त्त.

Now consider what happens if we analyse existence from the perspective of awareness.

An object appears as existing.

The mind says, “That exists.”

There is the object.

There is its appearance.

There is the judgement of existence.

Abhinavagupta is interested in the fact that existence, as manifest, is inseparable from consciousness's power to illuminate what appears.

This is not simply the assertion that nothing exists when an individual human being stops looking at it.

The supreme consciousness of Trika is not your personal attention span.

It is the universal ground within which subjects and objects alike are manifested.

And this brings us back to the four words.

### What does *amāyīya* actually imply?

The form is significant.

**a-māyīya**

The negative prefix *a-* negates the adjective *māyīya*, relating to Māyā or the conditioned differentiation associated with it.

Here the contrast is between ultimately real expression and its restricted condition.

The *amāyīya* nature of truth, happiness, prosperity, and being does not mean that every conventional use of these words is free from error, limitation, or dependency.

It means that what is ultimately real in them cannot be exhausted by the restrictions of Māyā.

A word can have a conventional meaning while also participating in a deeper order of manifestation.

And, within the mantra-theory being explained, the initial phoneme can be contemplated as an articulation of that deeper expressive power.

This is not a denial of ordinary semantics.

It is an attempt to ground semantics in something more fundamental.

The philosopher has now turned the humble word *sukha* into a portal to the absolute.

Your Sanskrit teacher is standing in the doorway looking increasingly concerned.


## VII. The Expert Who Hears One Letter and Knows What You Mean

### Kashmir Series p. 240

Now Abhinavagupta introduces a surprisingly ordinary phenomenon.

An exceptionally perceptive person can infer what someone intends from the beginning of an utterance.

The key expression is:

**परहृदयग्रहणेङ्गितनिपुणाः**

*parahṛdayagrahaṇeṅgitanipuṇāḥ*

People skilled at recognising another person's intention through indications or signs.

The compound is worth disassembling.

**para** - another, someone else.

**hṛdaya** - heart, centre, inner disposition; here the relevant sense is the inner intention.

**grahaṇa** - grasping, apprehension, from √*grah*, to seize or apprehend.

**iṅgita** - an indication, gesture, sign, or intimation.

**nipuṇa** - skilled, expert, discerning.

The final plural form **nipuṇāḥ** is masculine nominative plural, describing the skilled interpreters.

In the compound, *grahaṇa + iṅgita* produces **grahaṇeṅgita**, through the regular vowel sandhi *a + i → e*.

Beautiful.

A whole theory of intersubjective understanding hiding in one compound.

### His examples begin with G

Abhinavagupta mentions words such as *gagana*, *gavaya*, and *gava*-related forms.

**गगन - gagana** means sky or firmament.

**गवय - gavaya** means a kind of wild bovine.

The forms share an initial **ga**.

The commentator's point is that someone skilled in understanding another person's intention may identify the intended expression from a minimal beginning.

Imagine someone points upwards and begins:

“Ga…”

You've been talking about astronomy.

You may immediately anticipate *gagana*.

Now imagine you are discussing cattle and wild animals.

The same initial phonetic material could guide you toward an entirely different word.

The sound alone is insufficient.

Context matters.

The speaker's intention matters.

The hearer's understanding matters.

This is not a theory that every isolated G has exactly one inevitable meaning.

It is a theory about **how apparently incomplete expressive material can already participate in meaningful communication**.

And now the really interesting bit.

### The word may be incomplete, but understanding isn't merely mechanical

Suppose someone says:

“I'm going to the…”

Before they finish, you might already understand the sentence's direction.

But why?

You are not simply accumulating independent dictionary definitions.

Your understanding is anticipatory.

It integrates the situation, speaker, grammar, expectation, previous context, and possible continuations.

An utterance does not have to be complete before it begins to disclose an intention.

This is precisely the kind of phenomenon Abhinavagupta wants to exploit.

**Meaning is not simply pasted onto an acoustically completed object after the fact.**

Expression and understanding are deeply related processes.

The hearer can grasp significance through partial manifestation because linguistic understanding is not merely the passive reception of finished sound-packets.

Now, Abhinavagupta makes the metaphysically ambitious move.

If an individual phoneme can already participate in genuine communication, perhaps we should not regard phonemes as intrinsically meaningless objects whose connection to meaning is wholly external.

He calls attention to the **vāstavaṃ vācakatvam** of individual phonemes.

Here:

**vāstava** - real, actual, belonging to the nature of something.

**vācakatva** - the condition or capacity of expressing or signifying, formed from *vācaka* with the abstract suffix *-tva*.

The claim is that individual sound-units possess genuine expressive capacity at the deeper level under discussion.

But we must understand what he is-and isn't-proving.

### The potential objection

A grammarian might respond:

“Mate, an initial G can begin thousands of words. That does not mean G has a fixed meaning.”

And the grammarian would be right.

Abhinavagupta's example does not establish a universal one-to-one mapping between an isolated initial sound and a conventional lexical meaning.

The argument becomes intelligible only when we remember his broader theory.

Meaning is a manifestation of conscious expression.

A phoneme is a differentiated mode of that expression.

In particular contexts, its appearance can already disclose an intended meaning.

At the level of the supreme mantra, phonemic significance is claimed to be more fundamental than arbitrary verbal convention.

These are related but distinguishable assertions.

The first is readily intelligible through ordinary communication.

The second belongs to the specifically tantric metaphysics of language.

Abhinavagupta uses the familiar phenomenon to help articulate the more ambitious philosophical claim.

Now he calls an unexpected witness.

Someone who is definitely not a Kashmir Śaiva nondualist.

Patañjali.


## VIII. Patañjali Walks into the Room and Makes Everything More Complicated

### Authentic citation: Yogasūtra 3.17

Abhinavagupta explicitly quotes the *Yogasūtra*.

The sūtra reads:

**शब्दार्थप्रत्ययानामितरेतराध्यासात्सङ्करस्तत्प्रविभागसंयमात्सर्वभूतरुतज्ञानम् ॥**

*śabdārthapratyayānām itaretarādhyāsāt saṅkaras tatpravibhāgasaṃyamāt sarvabhūtarutajñānam.*

My literal rendering:

“Because word, object or meaning, and cognition are mutually superimposed, there is confusion. Through disciplined concentration upon their distinction, knowledge of the vocalisations of all beings arises.”

This wording is independently attested in the Pātañjala tradition; orthographic presentations may show *saṃkara* or *saṅkara*, representing the same nasal assimilation before *k*. ; ](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutra-with-bhoja-vritti-sanskrit/d/doc1217331.html); (https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/yoga-sutras-with-commentaries/d/doc1216724.html)\]

First, a critical warning.

**This is Patañjali's sūtra, not Abhinavagupta's composition.**

Abhinavagupta cites it to support a particular step in his argument.

It would be wrong to infer that Patañjali therefore accepts Abhinavagupta's account of parāvāk, mantra-consciousness, or the identity of the supreme Self with Śiva.

The two philosophical systems have significant differences.

Now the Sanskrit.

### Śabda - शब्द

Word, sound, linguistic expression.

The word is traditionally connected with √*śabd*, to sound or make noise.

In the present argument the relevant distinction is between linguistic expression and the object or meaning it communicates.

There is no need to regard the word and its object as numerically identical.

The sound “cow” is not a cow.

An actual cow will demonstrate this distinction to you by eating your vegetables while the word continues lying innocently on the page.

### Artha - अर्थ

Meaning, object, aim, purpose, referent, and other related senses.

Here it denotes the object or meaning associated with a linguistic expression.

If I say “tree,” you distinguish the sound of the word from the object being referred to.

But in ordinary understanding, the two can be so tightly associated that their difference is overlooked.

This is the problem of mutual superimposition.

### Pratyaya - प्रत्यय

A cognition, mental presentation, or determinate cognitive event.

The term has numerous grammatical and philosophical uses.

In this sūtra it concerns cognition associated with a word and its referent.

The sūtra therefore distinguishes three components:

1. The linguistic expression.
2. Its meaning or object.
3. The cognition through which that meaning is apprehended.

These are usually experienced in close association.

A skilled practitioner, according to the Yoga tradition, can distinguish them through concentrated attention.

### Itaretarādhyāsa - इतरेतराध्यास

This is an important compound.

**itara-itara** - each with the other, mutual.

**adhyāsa** - superimposition, from *adhi-* \+ √*as*, to place upon, in an extended sense.

Thus **itaretarādhyāsa** means reciprocal superimposition.

Its ablative singular ending is **-āt**:

*itaretarādhyāsāt* - “because of mutual superimposition.”

That is a grammatical point, not merely a metaphysical interpretation.

The sūtra uses the ablative to identify the condition producing confusion.

### Saṅkara - सङ्कर

Mixing, intermingling, confusion.

The form is nominative singular masculine:

*saṅkaraḥ*.

Before the following word, the visarga is represented through external sandhi.

In the continuous text:

*saṅkaras tat-*.

Do not mistake this for the proper name Śaṅkara.

Different initial consonant.

Different word.

The Sanskrit is not suddenly introducing an Advaita Vedānta philosopher into Patañjali's meditation instructions.

### Tatpravibhāgasaṃyamāt

Now the monster compound.

**tat** - that, referring to the elements just described.

**pra-vibhāga** - precise differentiation, separation, distinguishing.

**saṃyama** - the combined discipline of concentration, meditation, and absorption defined earlier in the *Yogasūtra*.

The ending **-āt** is again ablative singular.

So the compound expresses the means or cause: through *saṃyama* on their distinction.

The exact construction is crucial.

Patañjali is not simply telling someone to think intellectually about the difference between a word and a thing.

He is describing a disciplined yogic practice believed to yield an extraordinary capacity of understanding.

### Sarvabhūtarutajñānam

**sarva** - all.

**bhūta** - beings, creatures.

**ruta** - sound, cry, vocalisation, connected with √*ru*, to sound or cry out.

**jñāna** - knowledge, from √*jñā*, to know.

The compound therefore means knowledge of the sounds of all beings.

Within the *Yogasūtra*, this belongs to the *vibhūti* teachings concerning capacities allegedly attained through yogic concentration.

Such a capacity is a traditional yogic claim, not an independently established scientific ability.

But the philosophical structure of the passage remains fascinating.

### The tension between Patañjali and Abhinavagupta

This is where a superficial commentary would simply announce:

“See? Patañjali proves the magic alphabet.”

No.

He doesn't.

Patañjali's explicit emphasis is on **distinguishing** sound, referent, and cognition.

Abhinavagupta's emphasis is on understanding their relation to a more fundamental conscious expressive reality.

These are not identical positions.

The Pātañjala framework retains its own account of *puruṣa*, *prakṛti*, mind, and liberation.

The Trika framework understands all manifestation through the freedom of supreme consciousness.

Why, then, use the sūtra?

Because the ordinary conflation of sound, referent, and cognition conceals their actual relationship.

By bringing the elements into focus, one can understand expression more precisely.

Abhinavagupta can appropriate that principle without adopting the whole of Patañjali's metaphysics.

This is characteristic of his philosophical method.

He draws evidence from other traditions, tests how it relates to his own framework, and integrates it where appropriate.

Which means the sacred scriptures are now conducting something resembling an inter-school seminar.

Except one participant has three eyes and another is promising knowledge of animal cries.

A very productive Thursday.

### Is there a metre?

**No.**

Yogasūtra 3.17 is a prose aphorism, not an eight-syllable-pāda śloka.

Its length and syntax should not be forced into anuṣṭubh metre merely because it is a famous Sanskrit text.

This distinction matters.

A verse can be metrically tested.

A prose sūtra must be analysed through its syntax, compound structure, and technical vocabulary instead.


## IX. The Single-Letter Particles: Grammar Is Apparently Secret Theology

Now comes one of the most subtle parts of the passage.

Abhinavagupta turns to particles and grammatical endings, including expressions associated with the letters **a** and **ca**.

His argument concerns linguistic units that may function without naming separate substantial objects.

Here is the problem.

A noun like *ghaṭa*, pot, can denote an independently identifiable object.

But what exactly does the particle **ca**, “and,” denote?

An extra object called “and”?

A mysterious little entity floating between two pots?

You have a pot.

You have another pot.

You say “pot and pot.”

Where is the third object corresponding to “and”?

There isn't one.

The particle contributes a relation or coordination.

And that is precisely why this passage matters.

### What is a nipāta?

**निपात - nipāta**

A grammatical particle, conventionally placed within the indeclinable vocabulary.

The term is traditionally analysed through *ni-* \+ √*pat*, “fall down.”

Particles include various connective, restrictive, emphatic, or other elements, depending on the grammatical classification.

Their significance often concerns the relation between other expressions rather than an independently identifiable substance.

Take:

**च - ca**

“And.”

Its written form is one Devanāgarī akṣara, with an inherent vowel *a*.

At the phonological level, the consonantal element is *c*, conventionally named *cakāra*.

That difference between the character as pronounced and the consonantal *varṇa* is important.

Do not confuse a single graphic akṣara with a simple English alphabetic letter.

Now an ordinary example:

*śivaḥ ca* undergoes external sandhi, producing *śivaś ca* in a continuous expression.

The particle has not turned into a physical object.

It is participating in a relation of conjunction.

And yet it plainly means something.

Its semantic function is real.

### What is a vibhakti?

**विभक्ति - vibhakti**

A grammatical inflectional ending, particularly associated with nominal case and verbal inflection in traditional grammatical terminology.

It is related to *vi-* \+ √*bhaj*, to divide or distribute.

Some grammatical endings are extremely short, and the actual phonetic shape of an ending can change under inflectional rules and sandhi.

We must not assume that every grammatical suffix is simply a complete word with an independent dictionary meaning.

But its contribution to an utterance can still be indispensable.

Consider:

**रामः पश्यति - rāmaḥ paśyati**

“Rāma sees.”

Now:

**रामं पश्यति - rāmaṃ paśyati**

“$$Someone$$ sees Rāma.”

The lexical base *Rāma* has not changed.

But its grammatical role has.

In the first sentence, *rāmaḥ* is nominative singular.

In the second, *rāmaṃ* is accusative singular.

The ending contributes crucial information.

Yet you do not normally identify the accusative suffix as an independently existing object.

This is why grammatical relationships are philosophically interesting.

A linguistic element can have genuine meaning while not denoting a separate *thing*.

### The peculiar word asattva

Abhinavagupta describes this sort of significance with the term **asattvabhūta**.

The temptation is to translate that as “nonexistent.”

That would be misleading.

In the grammatical context, *asattva* is concerned with the absence of independent substantival status.

It does not mean that particles have no semantic function.

Quite the opposite.

They can meaningfully operate without naming standalone substances.

The contrast is between independent object-reference and relational or functional signification.

And Abhinavagupta sees in this an analogy to something much deeper.

**The ultimate subject does not become a separate object merely because language can indicate it.**

A particle can contribute meaning without naming an extra entity.

Similarly, the supreme *Aham* cannot be adequately understood as simply another object among objects.

There is a radical difference between a term indicating the subject and treating the subject as one more content of awareness.

### Idantā and ahantā

Recall these two terms from our earlier readings.

**idantā** - “this-ness,” objectivity, the condition of being presented as “this.”

**ahantā** - “I-ness,” subjectivity, the mode of self-reference.

Abhinavagupta's broader doctrine holds that the subject-object distinction emerges within consciousness.

In ordinary experience, the object is presented as “this.”

The limited subject identifies as “I.”

But the supreme *Aham* is not just another individual ego confronting an external universe.

It is the universal self-awareness within which both poles are manifest.

The passage's account of particles supports the general possibility of meaning that is not reducible to naming a separate thing.

That is a modest linguistic point.

Abhinavagupta places it inside a very ambitious metaphysical framework.

And this is exactly where we need to separate his prose from the later editor's explanatory notes.

### The editorial intervention

The 1918 edition adds a substantial bracketed explanation of particles and their lack of independent substantival meaning. It draws a further comparison with a quotation from the *Īśvarapratyabhijñā*.

That bracketed explanation is **not a new passage of Abhinavagupta's prose**.

It is an editorial attempt to elucidate the argument.

The editor is making explicit the relation between grammatical non-substantiality and the structure of subjectivity.

This is helpful, but it cannot be attributed wholesale to Abhinavagupta.

The printed brackets matter. In the Muktabodha-derived transcription, they are preserved as bracketed annotations; the GRETIL transcription omits these long editorial explanations and preserves the shorter continuous text. ; \]

There is an additional philosophical risk here.

We must not treat the grammar of particles as proof of nondual metaphysics.

The grammatical example illuminates the distinction between semantic contribution and independent objecthood.

The identity of everything with supreme consciousness requires the larger Trika argument.

Abhinavagupta is not deriving Śiva from the word “and” through three lines of Paninian analysis.

Although, given his talent for unexpected connections, you would be unwise to challenge him publicly.


## X. Bhartṛhari Arrives: Is the Sentence a Word, a Collection, or One Indivisible Meaning?

### Kashmir Series p. 241 - Vākyapadīya 2.2

Now Abhinavagupta invokes Bhartṛhari.

This is a particularly important move because Bhartṛhari's philosophy of language provides an intellectual framework in which the relation between linguistic units and complete meaning becomes a profound metaphysical question.

The *Parātriṃśikāvivaraṇa* quotes only the opening half of *Vākyapadīya* 2.2.

Its reading is:

**पदमाद्यं पृथक्सर्वं पदं साकाङ्क्षमित्यपि ।**

*padam ādyaṃ pṛthak sarvaṃ padaṃ sākāṅkṣam ity api.*

But the independently transmitted full *Vākyapadīya* verse, as found in the SARIT text, reads:

**पदमाद्यं पृथक्सर्वं पदं सापेक्षमित्यपि ।**

*padam ādyaṃ pṛthak sarvaṃ padaṃ sāpekṣam ity api.*

The remaining half is:

**वाक्यं प्रति मतिर्भिन्ना बहुधा न्यायदर्शिनाम् ॥**

*vākyaṃ prati matir bhinnā bahudhā nyāyadarśinām.*

**My literal translation:**

“With regard to the sentence, the views of theorists differ in many ways: the initial word, every word separately, and the word dependent upon others $$have each been considered relevant to what constitutes a sentence$$.”

The significant textual distinction is *sākāṅkṣam* in Abhinavagupta's quotation versus *sāpekṣam* in the independent text. They are close in meaning but not identical forms. The second half is supplied from the independent *Vākyapadīya*, not from Abhinavagupta's quotation. ; ; \]

This verse is not a simple declaration that every word is an indivisible sentence.

It records different positions concerning what a sentence is.

That distinction matters immensely.

### The first proposal: the initial word

**padam ādyaṃ**

*Padam* - a word, inflected form, linguistic unit, or step, depending on context.

*Ādyam* - first, initial, from *ādi*.

One view gives primacy to the initial word in determining the sentence.

But why would anyone think the initial word could have sentence-like status?

Because utterances are processed in time.

Meaning begins emerging as language unfolds.

A hearer doesn't normally remain completely semantically inactive until the final syllable.

The initial word already begins structuring expectation.

Imagine someone says:

“Fire!”

You might react immediately.

You don't need the completed sentence:

“There is currently a fire in the building, and, having considered the relevant circumstances, I would like to recommend a controlled evacuation.”

By the time that beautifully grammatical sentence ends, the building is a tantric homa offering.

The word itself can function as an utterance.

### The second proposal: every word separately

**pṛthak sarvam**

*Pṛthak* - separately, individually.

*Sarvam* - all, every.

Another position allows individual words to carry the sentence's significance.

The exact interpretation belongs to the broader debate among linguistic theorists.

What matters for our present passage is that the relation between word and utterance is not exhausted by treating the sentence as a mere chain of independently complete lexical units.

### The third proposal: an expectant or dependent word

Here the two readings become interesting.

**sākāṅkṣam** - possessing expectation or dependence upon completion.

**sāpekṣam** - dependent, requiring something else.

In the first form, *sa-* combines with *ākāṅkṣa*, related to expectation or desire.

In the second, *sa-* combines with *apekṣa*, dependence or regard.

The meanings overlap: a word may require other words or a larger syntactic structure for its communicative role to be fulfilled.

For example:

“Rāma gave…”

The verb immediately creates expectations.

Gave what?

To whom?

Under what circumstances?

A hearer is not collecting isolated sound-objects without any forward-directed understanding.

The partially heard utterance already possesses a structure of expectation.

And that brings us directly back to Abhinavagupta's previous example.

A skilled hearer can recognise intended meaning from incomplete expression.

Now Bhartṛhari gives us the larger grammatical debate in which this can be situated.

### Why Bhartṛhari matters to Trika

Bhartṛhari's philosophy of language is not reducible to a theory of individual word definitions.

He is deeply concerned with linguistic cognition, the relation between parts and wholes, and the unity involved in the comprehension of an utterance.

The concept of **sphoṭa** is particularly relevant in the wider tradition: linguistic meaning is not adequately explained by treating a sentence as a mere sequence of disconnected acoustic events.

Abhinavagupta develops his own account through the stages of speech and the metaphysics of supreme consciousness.

These two systems have genuine conceptual affinities.

But they are not identical.

Bhartṛhari's *śabdabrahman* should not simply be relabelled Śiva without examining the differences in their respective accounts.

That would be dreadful intellectual history.

The serious comparison concerns how the unity of meaning relates to temporal linguistic manifestation.

Abhinavagupta's answer ultimately involves the freedom and self-awareness of the supreme subject.

A sentence appears successively.

Its understanding may have a unity not equivalent to the sum of isolated acoustic moments.

Similarly, in his metaphysics, cosmic manifestation is differentiated, but its ground is not assembled by adding together the differentiated manifestations.

This is the philosophical resemblance.

### A little metrical investigation

Unlike the prose of Yogasūtra 3.17, Bhartṛhari 2.2 is an **anuṣṭubh śloka**, with four eight-syllable pādas.

The independent reading scans as follows.

| Pāda | Syllabic division | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| 1 | pa-dam-ā-dyaṃ-pṛ-thak-sar-vaṃ | 8 |
| 2 | pa-daṃ-sā-pek-ṣam-i-tya-pi | 8 |
| 3 | vāk-yaṃ-pra-ti-ma-tir-bhin-nā | 8 |
| 4 | ba-hu-dhā-nyā-ya-dar-śi-nām | 8 |

The Kashmir Series quotation with *sākāṅkṣam* also preserves the eight-syllable structure of the second pāda:

*pa-daṃ-sā-kāṅ-kṣam-i-tya-pi.*

Therefore metre alone cannot decide the textual question.

Both readings fit.

This is important.

It would be easy to announce that one must be corrupt because the other sounds more familiar.

But the metrical evidence does not supply that conclusion.

The actual relationship between the transmitted *Vākyapadīya* reading and Abhinavagupta's citation needs textual rather than purely metrical investigation.

It might reflect citation from a different textual form, a variation in quotation, or later transmission.

We should not assert a lost manuscript variant without evidence.

### The philosophical punchline

Bhartṛhari is examining theories concerning the unity and composition of linguistic meaning.

Abhinavagupta invokes him while arguing that phonemes can have genuine expressive capacity.

But it would be inaccurate to claim that Bhartṛhari's verse straightforwardly proves the isolated existence of independent meanings for every phoneme.

The verse records differing accounts of what constitutes a sentence.

Its relevance lies in showing that linguistic meaning cannot be understood by one naive model of independently labelled objects.

The argument gets its distinctively Trika force from Abhinavagupta's metaphysics of consciousness.

Thus our philosophical gathering now contains:

Patañjali, insisting that linguistic sound, object, and cognition must be distinguished.

Bhartṛhari, explaining that the very nature of a sentence is disputed.

Abhinavagupta, insisting that language is ultimately intelligible through the self-expression of consciousness.

And the reader, wondering whether the Sanskrit particle *ca* can ever again mean simply “and.”


## XI. Does This Mean You Can Derive Any Secret Meaning from Any Sanskrit Word?

Here comes the decisive qualification.

Abhinavagupta says that explanations based on resemblance of phonemes can be appropriate in Vedic, grammatical, mantric, and initiatory contexts.

But immediately restricts the principle.

**न नियतिवशादिति न लोकपर्यन्तम्**

The surrounding prose contrasts such interpretation with ordinary established usage and invokes *niyati*, determinacy or restriction.

The important conclusion is that **mantric or phonemic interpretation cannot simply be extended without restriction across all everyday language**.

This is arguably the most easily overlooked sentence in the entire passage.

Because if you ignore it, you can turn Abhinavagupta into an unlimited Sanskrit word-association machine.

And the results would be intellectually catastrophic.

Someone notices that *sukha* begins with S.

Then decides that every word beginning with S must literally mean supreme bliss.

Then invents a metaphysical interpretation of *samosa*.

Then announces that the triangular shape of a samosa represents the three Śaktis and that its spicy filling is the concealed fire of Bhairava.

Now, a tantric ritual symbolism of food could certainly be constructed.

But it would be **our construction**, not an attested explanation by Abhinavagupta.

And that is exactly the sort of distinction a rigorous reading must preserve.

### What is nirvacana?

**nirvacana** means explanation, interpretation, or etymological derivation.

It is related to *nir-* \+ √*vac*, to speak.

In Sanskrit intellectual traditions, *nirvacana* can include traditional semantic explanation rather than only historical linguistic etymology in the modern comparative sense.

A tantric *nirvacana* may disclose a ritual or metaphysical significance.

A grammatical derivation explains a word according to linguistic rules.

A historical etymology investigates actual linguistic development.

These are different enterprises.

They can illuminate one another.

But they should not be conflated.

For example, a tantric author might associate a particular phoneme with a deity or a cosmic function.

That association belongs to the author's doctrinal system.

It does not thereby become proof that all Sanskrit speakers historically used that sound with the same meaning.

### The significance of niyati

In Trika's system of thirty-six tattvas, **niyati** belongs to the limiting conditions associated with Māyā.

It is often understood as restriction, order, or particular determination.

Here it helps explain why ordinary linguistic usage does not operate through unconstrained semantic possibility.

A word used in a particular community has an established meaning.

Its conventional function is governed by linguistic practice and contextual determination.

The fact that all language ultimately arises within consciousness does not abolish these limits.

It gives them a place within the larger metaphysical account.

This is extremely important for philosophical consistency.

If every phoneme can mean absolutely anything in every context, then the very distinction between successful and unsuccessful communication collapses.

But Abhinavagupta has just appealed to successful communication as evidence.

Therefore his argument cannot simply abolish all linguistic determinacy.

His position must permit both:

The ultimate freedom of expressive consciousness.

And the determinate structures through which particular expressions become meaningful.

In other words, **svātantrya does not entail semantic anarchy.**

The absolute freedom of Śiva is not the same as your freedom to invent Sanskrit and insist that Abhinavagupta secretly meant it.

### The editor's bracketed objection

The 1918 Kashmir Series supplies a lengthy bracketed explanation at precisely this point.

The objection runs, in substance:

If phonemic correspondences ground explanation, why not interpret every word everywhere through arbitrary phonemic resemblance?

The editor then explains Abhinavagupta's appeal to determinacy as a restriction on that extension.

This is a helpful clarification of the argument, but again the bracketed objection and explanation are **editorial material**, not an independent passage from Abhinavagupta's running prose. ; \]

An important distinction.

We can use the editor's explanation to reconstruct the argument.

We cannot introduce it as if Abhinavagupta personally wrote the bracketed objection.


## XII. The Philosophical Synthesis: Why Sound, Meaning, and Consciousness Are Inseparable but Not Identical

We've covered an extraordinary amount of ground.

Let's assemble the argument.

Abhinavagupta began with exceptional manifestations.

Certain forms are iconographically extraordinary.

Certain substances occupy particular ritual roles.

Certain phonemes have specially significant roles within a mantra.

Their distinction does not contradict universal nonduality because differentiation is itself an expression of consciousness.

Then he moved to *sakāra*.

Its supreme significance in the present mantra is associated with bliss, expression, and the entire network of phonemes.

Bodily manifestations of intense pleasure supply concrete examples of the relationship between experience and spontaneous expression.

Words such as *satya*, *sukha*, *sampat*, and *sattā* become occasions for distinguishing ordinary conditioned meanings from their deeper grounding.

The skilled hearer's ability to anticipate incomplete speech shows that linguistic significance can begin appearing before an utterance is acoustically complete.

Patañjali reminds us that linguistic sound, object, and cognition must be distinguished.

Bhartṛhari reveals the philosophical complexity of the relation between individual words and the sentence.

And Abhinavagupta finally restricts the extension of mantric phonemic interpretation through *niyati*.

What does all of this amount to?

### Consciousness is not a giant dictionary

A shallow reading would conclude:

“Every Sanskrit letter contains every meaning.”

But that is too imprecise.

A more defensible interpretation is:

**The possibility of determinate linguistic expression presupposes a more fundamental expressive consciousness, and particular phonemes can be understood as specialised manifestations of that power.**

Ordinary words operate within linguistic conventions.

A mantra operates within particular theological, ritual, and contemplative structures.

The supreme level of speech belongs to consciousness's fundamental self-awareness.

These levels are connected.

They are not interchangeable.

### The relationship between parā and vaikharī

Recall the earlier analysis.

At the level of *parā*, there is no fully articulated separation between speaker, speech, and meaning.

At *paśyantī*, differentiation begins to be conceivable within a more unified manifestation.

At *madhyamā*, the distinction between expressive form and what is expressed is more clearly present.

At *vaikharī*, speech is externally articulated.

The same expressive consciousness operates through these increasingly differentiated conditions.

But the lower level does not simply disappear when the higher is recognised.

A person can speak ordinary sentences while remaining established in recognition.

This connects directly to the Utpaladeva verse from which we resumed.

The liberated person's conceptual activity can continue.

The difference lies in recognition.

The universe is no longer apprehended as an alien collection of objects existing outside the scope of the supreme Self.

It is understood as the differentiated manifestation of one consciousness.

And speech is no exception.

### Why this is not mere idealism

We should be precise about the metaphysics.

The claim is not simply that individual thoughts manufacture an independently nonexistent world.

The fundamental subject of Pratyabhijñā is not the empirical ego.

It is universal consciousness.

The empirical ego, with its limited memory, sensations, personality, and linguistic habits, is itself a contracted manifestation within that reality.

Consequently, the statement that everything is the Self's manifestation is not equivalent to:

“I personally invented the universe.”

That would be the most spectacular case of metaphysical narcissism in history.

The supreme *Aham* is not the autobiographical personality.

It is the universal self-referential luminosity through which the distinction between subject and object becomes possible.

The individual recognises identity with that supreme consciousness rather than expanding the little ego into a cosmic dictator.

This is the essential movement of Pratyabhijñā.

### The significance of camatkāra

*Prakāśa* is illumination, manifestness.

*Vimarśa* is reflexive awareness or self-apprehension.

*Camatkāra* is the wonder, relish, or self-savouring aspect of awareness that frequently appears in Abhinavagupta's vocabulary.

The three cannot simply be treated as interchangeable dictionary synonyms.

But they help us see why bliss and language belong together in his account.

Consciousness is not merely a passive light falling upon external objects.

Its own manifestness includes self-awareness and freedom.

Expression is one dimension of that freedom.

And delight can be understood as a mode of consciousness's self-relish.

This provides a philosophical background for the special treatment of *sa* as supreme bliss in the present passage.

Not because the sound /s/ mechanically produces metaphysical ecstasy.

But because the sound is interpreted as a particular form of an expressive principle whose ultimate nature is consciousness.

### An example from meditation

Imagine sitting in meditation and hearing a sound outside.

At first:

“There is a sound.”

Then:

“I am hearing that sound.”

Then perhaps you become aware that the hearing itself is occurring within a larger field of conscious experience.

The sound need not disappear.

The distinction between sound and hearing need not be forcibly denied.

A change may instead occur in how the entire experience is apprehended.

You cease treating awareness as a tiny observer trapped inside a skull facing an entirely external world.

The field of manifestation becomes the object of enquiry.

Now suppose you repeat a mantra.

There is the audible phoneme.

There may be an internally imagined phoneme.

There is the act of intending the mantra.

There is the awareness in which all of these occur.

Abhinavagupta's philosophy asks you to investigate the relation between these.

The point is not to hallucinate letters of fire or induce extraordinary physical sensations as proof of attainment.

Such imagery may occur in contemplative practice, but the argument itself concerns recognition of the conscious ground of expression.

The crucial issue is whether the distinction between the mantra as a perceived sound and the consciousness manifesting that sound is understood correctly.

This is where the philosophy becomes practical without ceasing to be rigorous.


## XIII. The Metrical Audit: What Actually Scans?

There are three relevant textual forms in this instalment: Utpaladeva's closing verse, Patañjali's prose aphorism, and Bhartṛhari's quoted verse. The approaching Mālinīvijayottara quotation supplies one further metrical example.

### Utpaladeva, Īśvarapratyabhijñā 4.12

This is anuṣṭubh, four pādas of eight syllables.

| Pāda | Scansion by syllable | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *sarvo mamāyaṃ vibhava* | sar-vo-ma-mā-yaṃ-vi-bha-va | 8 |
| *ity evaṃ parijānataḥ* | it-ye-vaṃ-pa-ri-jā-na-taḥ | 8 |
| *viśvātmano vikalpānāṃ* | viś-vāt-ma-no-vi-kal-pā-nāṃ | 8 |
| *prasare 'pi maheśatā* | pra-sa-re-'pi-ma-he-śa-tā | 8 |

The division *ity evaṃ* is continuous through sandhi in recitation, but the syllable count remains eight.

The fourth pāda, *prasare 'pi maheśatā*, is particularly powerful:

**Even in the expansion of conceptual thought, divine sovereignty.**

The syntax makes the point with tremendous economy.

### Patañjali, Yogasūtra 3.17

**Prose.** There is no honest eight-syllable-pāda analysis to perform.

It is an aphorism whose compound syntax must be resolved grammatically.

### Bhartṛhari, Vākyapadīya 2.2

**Anuṣṭubh**, as demonstrated above.

Both *sāpekṣam* and *sākāṅkṣam* preserve the eight-syllable pāda; metre cannot discriminate the reading.

### The approaching Mālinīvijayottara 4.25

This also uses anuṣṭubh.

Its four pādas are metrically regular in Abhinavagupta's quoted form, and the independently transmitted alternative first pāda also scans with eight syllables.

This is a useful reminder that **metrical correctness does not guarantee textual identity**.

Several different textual readings can occupy the same metrical structure.

A genuinely critical reading requires both philology and metre.


## XIV. The Next Authentic Transition: From Sakāra to the Complete Heart-Mantra

### Kashmir Series p. 241 - End of this instalment

Abhinavagupta now concludes the present explanation of the first phoneme.

He says, in substance:

**तदेवं सकार ईदृशः । औकारविसर्गावपि व्याख्यातौ ।**

*tad evaṃ sakāra īdṛśaḥ. aukāravisargāv api vyākhyātau.*

“Thus the phoneme *sa* is of this nature. The vowel *au* and visarga have also been explained.”

This is the precise transition we have been approaching.

And the implications are enormous.

Because the heart-mantra is not simply *sa*.

It is:

# सौः

## sauḥ

# स

sa

# औ

au

# ः

visarga

Analytical components, not the ordinary written sequence of three independent characters.

In normal Devanāgarī writing, the vowel *au* appears through the dependent vowel sign **ौ** attached to स, followed by the visarga **ः**.

Thus:

**स + ौ + ः = सौः**

The first phoneme has been interpreted through the nature of bliss and its relationship to the wider field of differentiated manifestation.

The *au* and visarga are linked to the remaining dimensions of the heart-mantra.

And here Abhinavagupta explicitly changes authority.

He introduces a quotation from the **Śrīpūrvaśāstra**, identified with the *Mālinīvijayottaratantra*.

### The quotation at the transition

**सार्णेन त्रितयं व्याप्तं त्रिशूलेन चतुर्थकम् ।**

*sārṇena tritayaṃ vyāptaṃ triśūlena caturthakam.*

**सर्वातीतं विसर्गेण परा व्याप्तिरुदाहृता ॥**

*sarvātītaṃ visargeṇa parā vyāptir udāhṛtā.*

My translation:

“Through the phoneme *sa*, the triad is pervaded; through the trident, the fourth. Through visarga, that which transcends all is pervaded: this is declared to be the supreme pervasion.”

This is the form quoted by Abhinavagupta. The independent *Mālinīvijayottara* transmission has the more explicit *sārṇenāṇḍatrayaṃ vyāptam*-“the triad of cosmic spheres is pervaded by the phoneme sa”-rather than the shorter *sārṇena tritayaṃ vyāptam*. Some independent witnesses also differ in the closing wording, including *parāyā vyāptir iṣyate*. These variations should be recorded, not silently normalised. ; ; \]

This is a genuinely consequential transition.

**Sa** pervades the three lower cosmic spheres: the earthly, the *prākṛta*, and the *māyīya*.

**Au**, represented through the trident symbolism, relates to the fourth, the Śākta sphere.

**Visarga** concerns the reality beyond these differentiated spheres.

These are doctrinal identifications within the tantric system, not ordinary phonetic definitions.

And we have reached the edge of the next argument.

Because now the phonemes are no longer being considered primarily through examples of conventional language or bodily expression.

The discussion is turning toward the **pervasion of the cosmic levels by the complete mantra**, and then the transmission of that knowledge through the guru.

At the opening of the next section, the text continues with another quotation from the *Mālinīvijayottaratantra*, concerning the conditions under which a satisfied guru transmits the teaching.

This will give us a fresh problem:

If the supreme reality is already the nature of consciousness, why does its recognition require a teacher, initiation, mantra, or the satisfaction of a guru?

And why does Abhinavagupta insist that these apparently external requirements have a place within the manifestation of absolute freedom?

That is the next philosophical explosion.


## Closing: The Alphabet Has Become a Mirror

We began with a four-armed figure, a three-eyed deity, and unusual ritual substances.

We discovered that a special phoneme can be understood as a mode of manifesting supreme bliss.

We examined how bodily sensations become expressive, how linguistic elements can convey meaning without naming independent substances, and how the distinction between word, object, and cognition complicates the apparently simple business of understanding speech.

And we learned why Abhinavagupta does not permit unlimited arbitrary phonemic interpretations.

The entire argument has a single deep concern.

**How can consciousness become the infinitely articulated world without ceasing to be itself?**

Speech makes the problem intimate.

You utter a sound.

It arises.

It changes.

It ceases.

Then another sound arises.

The meanings differ.

The words differ.

The languages differ.

But the fact that any of this is manifest at all presupposes the illuminating power of awareness.

Abhinavagupta's claim is that the supreme form of that awareness is not a passive spectator of a language-filled universe.

It is the very freedom through which expression and meaning are possible.

And the extraordinary significance of the mantra lies in recognising that relationship directly.

The supreme consciousness does not have to escape the alphabet.

It can reveal itself through the alphabet.

The ordinary person hears a consonant.

The grammarian hears a phonemic unit.

The philosopher investigates its meaning.

The tantric practitioner engages its place within the mantra.

And Abhinavagupta asks the question that makes all four uncomfortable:

**What is the reality in which the sound, its meaning, the hearer, and the act of hearing are already appearing?**

The sound disappears.

The question remains.

And the light by which you understand the question has never needed to announce its arrival.
