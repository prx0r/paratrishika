## 1. The three divine powers exist together

Here is the decisive opening from p. 161:

## तिसृणामपि चासां युगपत् स्थितिर्भवत्येव।

*Tisṛṇām api cāsāṃ yugapat sthitir bhavaty eva.*

A close translation:

The three, indeed, also exist simultaneously.

Now, this looks simple.

But the grammar is doing something interesting.

### Tisṛṇām - तिसृणाम्

This is a feminine genitive plural form of the numeral three.

Let's learn the paradigm.

| Gender | Nominative form | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Masculine | **त्रयः - trayaḥ** | Three masculine things |
| Feminine | **तिस्रः - tisraḥ** | Three feminine things |
| Neuter | **त्रीणि - trīṇi** | Three neuter things |

And now:

**तिसृणाम् - tisṛṇām**

Of the three, feminine.

Why feminine?

Because the relevant manifestations are understood as powers of the Goddess: Parā, Parāparā and Aparā, expressed in the differentiated levels of speech and purification.

It would be misleading to infer the grammatical gender from the English words "purifier," "means" and "object." The Sanskrit immediately preceding this passage uses neuter forms for the purifying functions, while **tisṛṇām** points to their feminine divine manifestations.

Now that's exactly the sort of detail that gets lost in a loose translation.

### Cāsām - चासाम्

Separate it:

**च - ca**

And, also.

**आसाम् - āsām**

Of these feminine entities.

Genitive plural of the demonstrative pronoun *idam* in its feminine paradigm.

Sandhi gives:

**ca + āsām → cāsām**

Because:

**a + ā → ā**.

So the phrase:

*tisṛṇām api cāsām*

Means something like "of these three also."

### Yugapat - युगपत्

Now here's the star of today's show.

**युगपत् - yugapat**

Simultaneously, at once, together.

It functions as an adverb.

In ordinary Sanskrit, it is not itself a finite verb or a plural noun.

It modifies the assertion that the three modes exist.

And:

### Sthitiḥ - स्थितिः

**स्थिति - sthiti**

Standing, remaining, established condition, existence.

From √**स्था - sthā**, to stand or abide.

The nominative singular is:

**स्थितिः - sthitiḥ**.

Then:

**भवति - bhavati**

Is, occurs, becomes.

From √*bhū*, to be or become.

And:

**एव - eva**

Indeed, precisely.

Notice that *bhavati* is singular because its grammatical subject is *sthitiḥ*, the singular noun "existence" or "established condition."

We aren't saying "three goddesses becomes one" because the verb happens to be singular.

We're saying that **the existence of these three is simultaneous**.

Now the philosophical question.

Does this mean three separate consciousnesses happen to operate at the same moment?

No.

Abhinavagupta has spent the preceding discussion rejecting the independent existence of the three modes.

They are distinguishable manifestations of one conscious reality.

But that still leaves an interesting problem.

Can one person actually experience multiple modes of cognition together?

Well.

Abhinavagupta thinks the answer is so obvious that he immediately appeals to ordinary experience.


## 2. You're speaking one thing, thinking another, and perceiving a third

Now comes one of the most immediately recognizable examples in the entire text.

Abhinavagupta describes a person who speaks about one thing, entertains another through conceptual thought, and perceives something else nonconceptually.

Three modes.

Apparently together.

And he relates them to:

**वैखरी - Vaikharī:** articulated speech.

**मध्यमा - Madhyamā:** inner verbal or conceptual articulation.

**पश्यन्ती - Paśyantī:** a subtler mode of apprehension.

Now imagine you're talking to someone in a café.

You're saying:

"Yes, absolutely. That sounds like a very interesting business opportunity."

Meanwhile your internal monologue is thinking:

"How much is this going to cost me?"

And your visual field includes a tiny gecko inching along the wall behind the person.

You're speaking.

Thinking.

Seeing.

All within an apparently unified conscious experience.

The external speech isn't identical to the internal thought.

And neither is identical to the visual presentation.

Yet you don't normally conclude that three independently existing conscious subjects have been assembled inside your head.

One to operate the mouth.

One to complain internally.

And one to supervise the gecko.

### Vikalpa - विकल्प

**विकल्प - vikalpa**

Conceptual construction, determination, differentiation.

Related to *vi-* and √*kḷp*, to arrange or form.

In the present discussion, the word identifies conceptual activity.

A particular thought is articulated.

Something is distinguished from something else.

Now compare:

**अविकल्प - avikalpa**

Without conceptual differentiation.

And:

**अविकल्पम् - avikalpam**

The neuter form or adverbial use, according to context.

Abhinavagupta's description of perceiving something *avikalpam* does not mean you're unconscious.

It means the perceptual content is not, at that level, being apprehended through the same sort of explicit conceptual construction as the internal verbal thought.

That's a crucial distinction.

### Jalpati - जल्पति

**जल्पति - jalpati**

Speaks, mutters, chatters or talks.

From √**jalp**, to speak or chatter.

Here the commentator uses it in connection with inner articulation.

Now compare:

**वक्ति - vakti**

He or she speaks.

From √**vac**, to speak.

Both belong to the semantic field of speaking, but they're different verbal roots.

And:

**पश्यति - paśyati**

He or she sees.

From √**paś**, to see.

Three verbs.

Speaking.

Conceptually articulating.

Seeing.

Now here's the subtlety.

We should not treat this illustration as a scientific proof that the brain executes exactly three independent mental processes with perfect temporal coincidence.

It's a phenomenological and metaphysical illustration.

Abhinavagupta appeals to experience to show that differentiated conscious functions can be apprehended together.

And their ultimate unity doesn't require reducing them to one identical function.

### Bhairava's fullness

He then says that within this entire differentiated manifestation, Bhairava alone is complete.

**परिपूर्ण - paripūrṇa**

Completely full.

From:

*pari-* \+ *pūrṇa*.

And *pūrṇa* belongs to the root-family of √*pṝ*, to fill.

Now why *paripūrṇa*?

Because the universal consciousness is not exhausted by any one mode.

Not by outward speech.

Not by conceptual thought.

Not by nonconceptual perception.

All can manifest within its fullness.

This is exactly the principle we've been developing since the beginning.

The differentiated powers are not independent conscious substances.

Their unity is grounded in Bhairava.

And now Abhinavagupta anticipates the objection.

A very irritating objection.

The sort of objection that makes a philosopher put down his tea and prepare for violence.

Perhaps these events aren't really simultaneous at all.

Perhaps they're merely so rapid that you fail to notice their succession.

And now-

**The flowers arrive.**


## 3. The śirīṣa flowers - A hundred events that look simultaneous

The opponent introduces an example involving the piercing of many delicate **śirīṣa** flowers and buds.

The idea is simple.

Imagine a hundred extremely thin petals or blossoms arranged together.

A sharp instrument passes through them.

To an observer, the whole action might seem instantaneous.

But perhaps the instrument actually passes through each flower in succession.

The intervals are simply too fine to notice.

Therefore, argues the opponent, apparent simultaneity need not establish genuine simultaneity.

This is a serious objection.

And it matters because Abhinavagupta has just appealed to the apparently concurrent functioning of speech, thought and perception.

The opponent says:

"Maybe you're not doing all three together."

"Maybe they're happening one after another so rapidly that the transitions escape observation."

In other words:

**Apparent simultaneity may be an illusion produced by imperceptibly rapid succession.**

Now this is where the Sanskrit becomes especially satisfying.

### Śirīṣa - शिरीष

A tree whose delicate blossoms supply the traditional comparison.

The word is conventionally transliterated:

**śirīṣa**.

Notice the two different sibilants.

**श - śa**

Palatal.

**ष - ṣa**

Retroflex.

The final consonant is ष, not श.

This is worth noticing because transliteration with ordinary English "sh" conceals the distinction.

### Kusuma - कुसुम

Flower, blossom.

### Pallava - पल्लव

Young leaf, shoot, bud.

The conjunct:

**ल्ल = ल् + ल**

A doubled lateral consonant.

### Vyatibheda - व्यतिभेद

Piercing, penetration, splitting through.

The word belongs to the *bheda* family we've already studied.

**भेद - bheda**

Difference, division, splitting.

Related to √*bhid*, to split.

The compound includes the prefix *vyati-*, conveying crossing or piercing through.

Now imagine the actual event.

A hundred flowers.

One needle.

The observer says:

"That happened all at once."

The philosopher replies:

"Actually, there were one hundred successive penetrations, each separated by an interval too small for you to perceive."

Now, this is not a ridiculous explanation.

It is perfectly sensible to distinguish **what appears simultaneous** from **what is genuinely simultaneous**.

And Abhinavagupta doesn't simply deny that rapid sequences can escape ordinary perception.

Instead he asks a much deeper question.

What do you mean by "at the same time"?

And how are you establishing the existence of the temporal relation?

That's where the argument turns.

Because the opponent thinks he's introduced an innocent analogy.

But he's accidentally opened the entire philosophy of time.

And Abhinavagupta is about to walk straight through the door.

; \]


## 4. “At the same time” - Abhinavagupta interrogates the interrogator

Now we reach the central challenge.

The opponent says the three modes might be successive rather than genuinely simultaneous.

Abhinavagupta replies, in essence:

What exactly do you mean by the word *yugapat*?

And the opponent says:

**समानकालम् - samānakālam**

At the same time.

Excellent.

Now let's see what you've actually established.

### Samāna - समान

Same, equal, common.

**काल - kāla**

Time.

So:

**समानकाल - samānakāla**

Having the same time, belonging to one temporal interval.

The compound is straightforward.

But the philosophy isn't.

Because if you define simultaneity as occurring at the same time, you now have to explain **what time is** and how time applies to conscious experience.

And Abhinavagupta immediately asks what time can mean in relation to the inward, conscious Self.

### Antarmukha - अन्तर्मुख

Inward-facing, turned within.

Break it down:

**अन्तर् - antar**

Inside, within.

**मुख - mukha**

Face, mouth, direction.

Thus:

**अन्तर्मुख - antarmukha**

Inward-facing.

Now combine this with:

**संविदात्मन् - saṃvidātman**

Having consciousness as one's nature; the conscious Self.

*Saṃvid* means consciousness or awareness.

*Ātman* means Self.

And the locative singular:

**संविदात्मनि - saṃvidātmani**

In or with regard to the conscious Self.

This is important.

Abhinavagupta isn't asking whether clocks are useful.

Nor is he denying the practical possibility of distinguishing morning from afternoon.

He's asking whether temporal determination can be treated as **more fundamental than the consciousness in which time is known**.

That is a different philosophical question.

### The opponent's hidden assumption

The opponent's reasoning is roughly:

1. All conscious events occur in time.
2. Two events that appear simultaneous may occupy different microscopic temporal positions.
3. Therefore the apparently concurrent modes of consciousness may really be successive.

Abhinavagupta challenges the first premise as an unexamined metaphysical assumption.

You may certainly establish temporal distinctions among experienced events.

But does the temporal framework exist independently of the conscious manifestation that makes such distinctions possible?

Within Trika, the answer is no.

Time itself belongs to the manifesting powers of consciousness.

Therefore it cannot simply be assumed as an independent external container in which consciousness must exist.

### The cosmic stopwatch

Imagine somebody running a stopwatch on Śiva.

"Lord, your first cognition occurred at 12:00:00.000001."

"Your second cognition occurred at 12:00:00.000002."

"Therefore you cannot experience both together."

Bhairava asks:

"And what makes these temporal distinctions manifest?"

The philosopher checks his stopwatch.

"Time, Lord."

"And what is time?"

"Well, Lord, it is the thing your consciousness must obey."

"According to whom?"

A considerable silence follows.

The stopwatch has become a metaphysical liability.

The point isn't that a stopwatch doesn't measure physical intervals.

It does.

The point is that **the measurable relations among objects and events do not automatically explain the ultimate nature of consciousness**.

Abhinavagupta is attacking the assumption that they do.


## 5. Time appears through succession and its absence

Now Abhinavagupta develops the relation between time and differentiated experience.

The Sanskrit discusses manifestations involving:

**प्राणगमागम - prāṇagamāgama**

The movement and return of vital activity.

Let's dismantle that.

### Prāṇa - प्राण

Life-breath, vital activity.

The word belongs to the root-family of √*an*, to breathe, with the prefix *pra-*.

In Śaiva contemplative physiology, *prāṇa* is more than an ordinary physical breath, though bodily breathing is frequently used to illustrate or engage its activity.

### Gama - गम

Going, movement.

Related to √**gam**, to go.

### Āgama - आगम

Coming, arrival, tradition, revealed scripture, depending on context.

From:

*ā-* \+ √*gam*.

In this compound, the relevant sense is movement toward or return.

So:

**गमागम - gamāgama**

Going and coming.

And:

**प्राणगमागम - prāṇagamāgama**

The going and coming of vital activity.

Now, don't immediately translate this as "inhale and exhale" in every context. The term participates in a larger account of subtle activity and its differentiation.

But the respiratory analogy is certainly relevant.

You experience the breath arising.

Passing.

Returning.

Pausing.

The distinctions contribute to the apprehension of succession.

And succession is central to our ordinary experience of time.

Abhinavagupta also invokes the **absence** of such activity.

Because the presence and absence of movement can both become objects of apprehension.

The movement appears.

Its cessation appears.

The distinction is known.

And this brings us back to the central argument.

**Temporal differentiation is itself manifested and apprehended.**

It is not an unquestionable external principle that can be inserted above awareness without explanation.

### Ābhāsa - आभास

Appearance, manifestation, presentation.

From:

*ā-* \+ √*bhās*, to shine or appear.

This is one of the most important terms in Kashmir Śaiva philosophy.

An *ābhāsa* is not necessarily a deceptive illusion.

It is an appearing or manifestation.

And the distinction between manifested succession and the universal consciousness in which it appears is fundamental.

### Why this matters for meditation

Now here's a subtle connection.

When observing breath, you may notice succession.

Inhalation.

Exhalation.

Pause.

Another inhalation.

And perhaps an unusual stillness in which the distinction between the phases becomes less prominent.

But Abhinavagupta's argument does not imply that breath retention or the absence of an ordinary sense of time is sufficient for liberation.

A state can be temporally unusual without constituting complete recognition.

His concern is the reality in which both succession and the apprehension of its absence occur.

The point isn't simply to suppress time-consciousness.

It is to understand its dependence upon awareness.

And that becomes philosophically explosive when the opponent tries to make time an independent authority over cognition.


## 6. Can a known object overpower the consciousness that knows it?

Now the opponent modifies the argument.

Very well, perhaps time is known.

But perhaps time, even as an object of knowledge, can impose its restrictions upon the conscious subject.

In other words:

"Yes, consciousness knows time, but time still determines how consciousness must operate."

And Abhinavagupta asks what justifies that priority.

This is where the Sanskrit introduces the relationship between:

**ज्ञेय - jñeya**

What is to be known.

And:

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge, knowing.

Both belong to √**jñā**, to know.

But their formations are different.

### Jñeya - ज्ञेय

A gerundive formation meaning knowable or what is to be known.

Look at the initial conjunct:

**ज्ञ = ज् + ञ**

In careful IAST:

*jña*.

Now:

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowing, knowledge.

The long ā matters.

These are distinct forms.

And the philosophical distinction is equally important.

*Jñeya* refers to the known or knowable side.

*Jñāna* refers to the knowing side.

Abhinavagupta's opponent wants the temporal determination of the known to govern the nature of knowing.

But Trika's account says that the known is manifest within consciousness.

That makes the proposed independence difficult to justify.

### Upādhi - उपाधि

Condition, adjunct, limiting qualification.

This word is important in several Indian philosophical traditions, though its precise use differs.

Here the opponent is imagining time as a condition belonging to the knowable field.

But if the condition is itself known, Abhinavagupta asks how its reality can be established independently of knowing.

Now, be precise.

This isn't the claim that one ordinary human being creates physical time by thinking about it.

Nor that science cannot investigate temporal processes.

It's the metaphysical claim that **the appearance and cognizability of temporal distinctions depend upon consciousness**.

The discussion is about ultimate grounding, not practical clock-making.

### Avaskand - अवस्कन्द्

To invade, rush upon, overwhelm or encroach.

The Sanskrit uses a verbal form conveying the possibility of the known temporal condition overrunning or imposing itself upon knowledge.

And Abhinavagupta challenges the intelligibility of that movement.

If time is being invoked as a knowable condition, how does it establish an independent relation capable of determining the ultimate nature of awareness?

You would have to explain the relation.

But explaining it requires invoking knowledge.

And here the argument begins to fold back upon itself.

### The epistemological landlord

Imagine an object called Time arriving at the door of Consciousness.

"I am your landlord."

"Interesting. On what basis?"

"I determine everything you experience."

"And how is your existence established?"

"By being known."

"Known through what?"

"Consciousness."

"Then perhaps we should investigate which of us is really the landlord."

The rent negotiations have become rather complicated.

Abhinavagupta isn't denying differentiated temporal relations.

He is denying that those relations can be treated as metaphysically self-sufficient explanations of consciousness.

And the next step is a classic philosophical problem.

**Mutual dependence.**


## 7. Itaretarāśraya - The circularity problem

Now comes a beautiful technical expression.

**इतरेतराश्रय - itaretarāśraya**

Mutual dependence.

Let's dismantle it.

**इतर - itara**

Other, another.

Repeated:

**इतरेतर - itaretara**

One another, mutually.

And:

**आश्रय - āśraya**

Support, ground, dependence, refuge.

Related to *ā-√śri*, to resort to or depend upon.

Thus:

**itaretara + āśraya → itaretarāśraya**

Mutual dependence.

Now this is an important logical notion in Indian philosophy.

An explanation may be circular if each of two things is established only by assuming the other.

For example:

A is established by B.

But B is established only by A.

And neither has any independent ground.

Now Abhinavagupta applies the problem to the relationship between time and knowing.

If temporal differentiation is invoked to explain the distinctions of consciousness, but temporal differentiation itself requires conscious apprehension to be established, the explanation threatens to become circular.

The opponent might distinguish the two more carefully.

But the distinction itself has to be presented and justified.

And that presentation still takes place within knowledge.

### The Sanskrit: Svataḥ-bheda

**स्वतः - svataḥ**

From oneself, independently, of itself.

**भेद - bheda**

Difference, distinction.

The expression concerns difference claimed from the standpoint of independent being.

Abhinavagupta's difficulty is that an independent separation between known and knowing cannot simply be asserted without accounting for their relationship.

If the known is completely unrelated to knowing, how is it known at all?

If it is related, what establishes the relation?

And if the relation is established through knowledge, we're back inside the conscious field.

The apparent escape route returns to its starting point.

### Why this is not a trivial word game

This is a foundational issue in epistemology.

How do we explain the relation between an experience and its object?

Does the known exist independently?

Does knowledge require a mediating representation?

How do we establish the relation between representation and represented object?

Different Indian philosophical traditions answer these questions differently.

Abhinavagupta's response belongs to the Pratyabhijñā account of self-luminous consciousness.

He does not simply assume the ordinary subject and object are the same thing in every respect.

He argues that their intelligible manifestation depends upon an underlying unity of awareness.

And that is why the independent temporal condition cannot simply be used to explain consciousness without further argument.

### The result

The opponent wanted to establish rapid succession by invoking extremely small temporal intervals.

Abhinavagupta asks how those intervals are known.

If they are objects of knowledge, their temporal distinctions appear within awareness.

If they are invoked as independent conditions determining awareness, the relation still has to be established.

And attempts to establish it risk circularity.

Thus the supposedly simple flower analogy has turned into a problem about the foundation of all cognition.

Which is a remarkable achievement for a hundred delicate blossoms.

But Abhinavagupta isn't finished with the flowers yet.

He has one more objection.

And it involves atoms.


## 8. The atom problem - When making everything too small destroys explanation

Abhinavagupta now returns to the hundred flowers.

The opponent argues that apparently simultaneous events might really be extremely rapid successive events.

Fine.

But Abhinavagupta challenges what happens when this explanation is pushed to its limit.

If every apparent unity must be dissolved into imperceptibly smaller component events, how are you establishing the activity that connects those components?

The discussion invokes:

**परमाणु - paramāṇu**

An ultimate minute particle or atom in classical Indian philosophical theories.

Now let's learn the morphology.

### Paramāṇu - परमाणु

**परम - parama**

Supreme, ultimate, most extreme.

**अणु - aṇu**

Minute, tiny, an atom.

So:

**परम + अणु → परमाणु**

*parama + aṇu → paramāṇu*.

Because:

**a + a → ā**.

Notice the retroflex nasal:

**ण - ṇa**

Not the dental **न - na**.

In many Indian philosophical systems, *paramāṇu* is a technical concept referring to a maximally minute constituent of matter.

But don't automatically equate it with an atom in contemporary particle physics.

The metaphysical and scientific concepts have different histories.

### Avayava - अवयव

Part, component, constituent.

The commentary speaks about parts and their relationship to the minute limiting constituent.

Now the problem.

Suppose every apparently unified operation is explained as a collection of minute operations.

And each of those is explained through even smaller operations.

At some point, the explanation must account for how the components form a coherent action.

It isn't enough merely to assert that sufficiently small components exist.

Their relationship must be made intelligible.

### The needle and the flowers, again

Imagine the needle passing through a hundred flowers.

The opponent says:

"That was actually one hundred separate penetrations."

Fine.

Now Abhinavagupta asks how that claim is being established.

If the distinction is too subtle to be directly noticed, what is the relation between the supposed individual penetrations and the apprehended whole?

And if every action dissolves into smaller, independently unapprehended units, what makes the whole an action?

Now this is not an argument that physical motion is impossible.

Nor proof that atomic theories are false.

It's an objection to using an imagined, imperceptible succession as a self-sufficient explanation of conscious experience.

Especially when the explanatory relation itself has not been established.

### Karman - कर्मन्

Action, deed, activity.

From √**kṛ**, to do or make.

And:

**कर्म - karma**

The familiar nominative/accusative singular neuter form.

Now the Sanskrit problem involves whether an action can be accounted for solely by an accumulation of minute components without addressing their unity.

Abhinavagupta's larger point is epistemological.

The opponent cannot simply make experience disappear into imperceptible fragments and then assume the fragments somehow explain the coherent experience.

The relation between the fragments and the apprehended whole is precisely what requires explanation.

### The absurd conclusion

Imagine the opponent saying:

"Your speech, thought and perception are not simultaneous. They're really tiny successive events."

Abhinavagupta replies:

"Explain the relation."

"More tiny events."

"And their relation?"

"Even tinier events."

"And how are those apprehended together?"

"Through an enormous number of extremely tiny events."

My brother.

You've built a philosophical onion.

Every time we peel a layer, we discover another onion.

At some point, someone has to account for the conscious unity through which the whole explanation is being entertained.

And that's the heart of Abhinavagupta's objection.

**Decomposing experience into constituents does not, by itself, explain their apprehended unity.**

Now this naturally brings us to memory.

Because memory is one of the most direct ways we experience relations among events.

And Abhinavagupta uses it to press the argument further.


## 9. Anusaṃdhāna - What makes one cognition connect with another?

Now comes an extremely important Sanskrit word.

**अनुसन्धान - anusaṃdhāna**

Connection, linking, continuous inquiry, recollective synthesis or sustained attention, depending on context.

The word belongs to the family of:

**अनु - anu**

Following, along, in sequence.

**सम् - sam**

Together.

And √**धा - dhā**

To place, establish or put.

The combined nominal formation expresses bringing things together in a continuing relation.

In this passage, the issue is the connection or continuity among cognitions.

Now think about something ordinary.

You hear the first half of a sentence.

Then the second half.

You understand the whole sentence.

How?

The first half isn't presently sounding when the second half arrives.

Yet the earlier words remain relevant to your understanding.

The present cognition is connected with what came before.

And this is where memory enters.

### Smṛti - स्मृति

Memory, recollection.

From √**स्मृ - smṛ**

To remember.

Now compare:

**स्मरति - smarati**

He or she remembers.

**स्मृति - smṛti**

Memory.

And:

**स्मरण - smaraṇa**

Remembering, recollection.

Same broad root-family.

Different formations.

Now here's the crucial relation.

Memory depends upon prior experience.

An entirely unexperienced content cannot simply be recovered as a memory of that experience.

At least, not in the ordinary sense that Abhinavagupta is discussing.

Thus the relation between an earlier experience and later recollection requires explanation.

### Anubhava - अनुभव

Experience, direct apprehension.

From:

**अनु - anu**

Following, along.

**भव - bhava**

Becoming, occurring, being.

The word is related to √*bhū*, to become or exist, through its established nominal formation.

Now, the literal pieces are worth recognizing, but the technical philosophical meaning cannot be reduced to "following-becoming."

In epistemological usage, *anubhava* concerns experience or apprehension.

And:

**अनुभवोपजीवित्व - anubhavopajīvitva**

Dependence upon experience for one's existence or operation.

Let's dissect that enormous construction.

**अनुभव - anubhava**

Experience.

**उपजीविन् - upajīvin**

Depending upon, living from, deriving support from.

**-त्व - -tva**

Abstract condition.

Thus:

*anubhava-upajīvitva*

The condition of depending upon experience.

Now the argument.

Memory is not an entirely self-sufficient operation disconnected from what is remembered.

It depends upon an antecedent apprehension.

And the recognition of continuity among experiences depends upon the relation between the earlier and later conscious episodes.

### The sentence that survives its own sounds

Imagine listening to:

"Śiva is the supreme consciousness."

By the time you hear "consciousness," the opening sound "Śi" is long gone.

It isn't still vibrating in the speaker's mouth.

And yet you understand the complete sentence.

If the successive phonetic events were entirely independent and no conscious relation united them, how would the sentence be understood as one meaningful expression?

The same problem appears in recognizing a melody.

You hear one note.

Then another.

Then another.

The notes unfold in time.

But the melody is apprehended through their relation.

Now Abhinavagupta's argument is not simply a modern theory of auditory working memory.

His conclusion is metaphysical.

He argues that the possibility of connecting experiences cannot be adequately explained by treating each cognition as wholly independent.

The conscious unity making recognition possible must be addressed.

### What the opponent would need to prove

Suppose the opponent maintains that every cognition is a distinct instant.

Then:

What accounts for the connection?

Memory?

But memory depends on prior experience.

Another cognition?

Then the relation between that cognition and the others must also be explained.

And if the connecting act is itself disconnected, the problem reappears.

Thus the issue is not whether cognition can exhibit temporal succession.

Of course it can.

The issue is whether succession, treated as an independent series of isolated events, adequately explains **recognition of the series as one intelligible whole**.

Abhinavagupta's answer is that the underlying self-revealing consciousness provides the ground of that relation.

And here's the distinction you should keep clear.

**The unity of awareness is not simply one additional remembered object inserted between other objects.**

If it were, it would require another act to connect it to the rest.

The consciousness in question is the ground of the relation itself.

That is the Pratyabhijñā move.


## 10. The difference between memory and self-awareness

Now we can make the philosophical structure more explicit.

Suppose you have three experiences.

At one moment, you see blue.

At another, you hear a bell.

At a later moment, you remember the blue object while listening to another sound.

The contents differ.

And their temporal relationships can be distinguished.

But what allows you to recognize them as belonging to one intelligible stream of experience?

Now, different philosophical traditions answer this differently.

Some invoke causal continuity among cognitions.

Others posit a persisting self.

Others analyse memory through impressions and subsequent mental events.

Abhinavagupta is working within the Pratyabhijñā account of **self-revealing consciousness**.

### Svasaṃvid - स्वसंविद्

Self-awareness, self-cognition.

**स्व - sva**

Own, oneself.

**संविद् - saṃvid**

Consciousness.

Thus:

**svasaṃvid**

One's own awareness.

But don't imagine two objects:

A self.

And another thing called consciousness looking at it.

The doctrine concerns consciousness's reflexive self-revelation.

And now:

### Prakāśa - प्रकाश

Luminosity, manifestation.

From *pra-* and √*kāś*, to shine or appear.

And:

### Vimarśa - विमर्श

Reflexive awareness, self-apprehension.

From *vi-* and √*mṛś*, to touch, examine or apprehend.

These are analytically distinguishable but ultimately inseparable in Abhinavagupta's metaphysics.

A mere luminous appearance without the power of self-apprehension would not capture the full reality of consciousness as he understands it.

And the connection among differentiated cognitions is ultimately grounded in this self-revealing reality.

### The impossibility of replacing awareness with another remembered object

Imagine you're trying to explain memory.

You posit a mental image.

Fine.

But the mental image must be apprehended.

So you posit another mental act that apprehends it.

But that act must also be apprehended.

You could keep constructing additional representations indefinitely.

Or you could investigate the intrinsic self-revealing character of awareness.

Abhinavagupta chooses the latter.

The point isn't that representations never occur.

They do.

The point is that their occurrence doesn't remove the need to explain the consciousness through which they are manifest.

### Returning to simultaneity

Now recall our original question.

Can speech, internal thought and nonconceptual apprehension coexist?

The opponent says they might be rapidly successive.

Abhinavagupta questions whether the mere invocation of succession explains the conscious relation among them.

Time itself is known.

The relation among events is apprehended.

Memory depends upon experience.

And the whole argument about their temporal order occurs within awareness.

Thus the ultimate conscious ground cannot simply be subordinated to an independently assumed temporal sequence.

The differentiated modes can manifest through succession.

But their unity is not produced merely by the observer assigning them the same timestamp.

**The supreme consciousness is not simply a collection of mental events happening quickly enough to look unified.**

That's the philosophical conclusion.

And now Abhinavagupta does something very characteristic.

He informs us that he's already treated this matter elsewhere.

At great length.

Naturally.


## 11. The Padārthapraveśanirṇayaṭīkā - “I've already written about this, you absolute lunatics”

Abhinavagupta refers to his own **Padārthapraveśanirṇayaṭīkā**.

That is a work or commentary in which, according to his statement here, he has examined the matter more extensively.

The title is rather magnificent.

### Padārtha - पदार्थ

Object, referent, category, meaning of a word.

From:

**पद - pada**

Word, expression, position.

**अर्थ - artha**

Meaning, object, purpose.

Thus:

**padārtha**

The meaning of a word or an object/category of knowledge.

### Praveśa - प्रवेश

Entry, entrance, introduction.

From *pra-* and √*viś*, to enter.

### Nirṇaya - निर्णय

Determination, ascertainment, decisive conclusion.

From *nir-* and √*nī*, to lead or determine.

### Ṭīkā - टीका

Commentary, explanatory gloss.

Thus the compound can be understood as a commentary on a determination concerning entry into, or understanding of, categories or objects of knowledge.

And here Abhinavagupta explicitly says that he has considered the problem extensively there.

The work is known from references in the catalogues of his writings, but the relevant discussion cannot simply be reconstructed verbatim from this passing mention.

### His wonderfully impatient conclusion

He then asks why he should create an enormous net of words that would interfere with the instruction currently being given.

Now the Sanskrit expression is beautiful.

**वृथावाग्जाल - vṛthāvāgjāla**

A useless net of speech.

Break it down.

**वृथा - vṛthā**

In vain, uselessly.

**वाक् - vāk**

Speech.

**जाल - jāla**

Net, network.

Thus:

**vṛthā-vāk-jāla**

A net of useless words.

And the compound in the sentence receives an instrumental ending.

Now, this is the most delicious irony imaginable.

Abhinavagupta has just spent an extraordinary amount of time discussing whether consciousness can experience multiple things simultaneously.

He has investigated the relation between time and awareness.

He's argued about piercing flowers.

He's discussed atoms.

He's challenged circularity.

He's invoked memory.

And now he announces that a longer discussion would be an unnecessary net of words.

My brother in Bhairava.

You are the net.

You have constructed the entire net.

We are all sitting inside it.

But philosophically, he's making a serious point.

The purpose of the commentary is not to pursue every possible logical dispute indefinitely.

Its purpose is to clarify the teaching.

He has examined the objection enough for the present argument.

And he is returning to the spiritual significance of the threefold manifestation.

The inquiry is important.

But inquiry itself mustn't become an endless diversion from recognition.

That's the balance.

And now the Goddess returns.

Not simply as a metaphysical principle.

But as the operative power through which different classes of beings are elevated.

This is where the Aghorā and Brāhmī powers enter.

; \]


## 12. The Goddess returns as purifier - Aparā, Parāparā and Parā

We resume at the end of p. 162.

Having addressed the problem of simultaneity and the unity of consciousness, Abhinavagupta returns to the divine powers.

**अपरा - Aparā**

**परापरा - Parāparā**

**परा - Parā**

And their respective operations as purifying powers.

Now remember why this discussion began.

The Goddess manifests as the object of purification.

The means of purification.

And the purifier.

But the three functions don't establish three independent ultimate substances.

They're differentiated manifestations of one consciousness.

And the same holds for the three Goddesses.

### Aparā - The differentiated power

**अपरा - aparā**

The lower or more differentiated divine power.

But don't translate *apara* as "bad" or "false."

The term concerns relative position within the hierarchy of manifestation.

Aparā has a genuine function within the system.

And Abhinavagupta expressly describes her in the capacity of the purifier.

Now, think about the ordinary process of purification.

You recognize an obstruction.

You use a means to remove it.

The work operates within a particular differentiated context.

That is the sort of function associated with the more manifest powers.

But their operation remains grounded in universal consciousness.

### Parāparā - The intermediate power

Now:

**परापरा - parāparā**

The intermediate Goddess.

She belongs to the domain in which unity and differentiation are related in a more subtle manner.

And Abhinavagupta now introduces the powers known as **Aghorā and others**.

### Aghorā - अघोरा

Let's learn this beautiful word.

**घोर - ghora**

Terrible, fearful, fierce, formidable.

**अघोर - aghora**

Not terrible, nonfearful, benign or beyond the fearful condition, depending on context.

The feminine form is:

**अघोरा - aghorā**.

Now this is not simply the name of the later historical Aghorī ascetic tradition.

The terms are related within broader Śaiva religious vocabulary, but here we're dealing with divine powers in a specific Tantric cosmological hierarchy.

The feminine ending matters.

*Aghora* can refer to a masculine form or principle.

*Aghorā* specifically names a female divine power.

And the commentary speaks of the **Aghorā powers** in the sphere of Parāparā.

### The philosophy

What is the relation between Aghorā and purification?

Within the Śaiva classification, the divine powers can operate in ways that obscure or reveal the true nature of consciousness.

The nonterrible or benevolent powers are associated with grace and liberating manifestation.

Now the qualification.

We shouldn't take the ordinary dictionary meaning of *aghora* and assume that it fully explains every technical use in the Tantra.

The scripture's own classifications determine the specific function.

But the broad distinction between obscuring and revealing powers is relevant.

And Abhinavagupta now uses it to describe the elevation of particular classes of beings.


## 13. Vijñānākala - The one who knows but has not recovered full agency

Now the commentary introduces:

**विज्ञानाकल - vijñānākala**

A highly technical class of subjects within Śaiva cosmology.

We've encountered them before.

And now they're returning in a specific relation to Aghorā and the *Mantramaheśvaras*.

Let's understand the name.

### Vijñāna - विज्ञान

Differentiated or special knowledge, cognition, understanding.

From:

**वि - vi**

Differentiation, distinction.

And:

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

Now, do not always translate *vijñāna* as "science."

It can have that meaning in modern languages.

But in Sanskrit philosophical texts it frequently refers to cognition or particularized knowing.

### Akala - अकल

Without the relevant *kalā*, or lacking a particular operative power, in this technical classification.

From:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

And:

**कला - kalā**

Portion, aspect, operative capacity.

Thus:

**vijñāna + akala → vijñānākala**.

The resulting term names a class of conscious subjects possessing a form of awareness but lacking the full divine agency associated with complete liberation.

Now here's the important distinction.

A *vijñānākala* isn't simply an ordinary human being who has read too many philosophy books.

Nor is the category identical to the supreme consciousness itself.

It is a specific position in the traditional cosmology of limited subjects.

### Knowledge without complete freedom

This is an extraordinary philosophical distinction.

The being possesses knowledge in a particular sense.

But it does not possess complete universal agency.

So the tradition refuses to identify **knowledge alone, in a restricted form**, with the full recovery of divine freedom.

Remember the inseparability of:

**ज्ञानशक्ति - jñānaśakti**

Power of knowledge.

And:

**क्रियाशक्ति - kriyāśakti**

Power of action.

In the supreme consciousness, these powers are inseparable aspects of freedom.

Within limited manifestation, they may appear restricted differently.

The *vijñānākala* category expresses precisely this kind of distinction.

And now Abhinavagupta describes the role of Aghorā and related powers in elevating such beings.


## 14. Aghorā and the Mantramaheśvaras - Divine office is not identical to ordinary cognition

The commentary says that, through the relevant divine powers, yogins or practitioners associated with the *vijñānākala* state attain forms such as the **Mantramaheśvaras**.

Let's remember what we learned in Part X.

**मन्त्र - mantra**

A sacred formula, but also a class of conscious beings in Śaiva cosmology.

**महेश्वर - maheśvara**

Great Lord.

And:

**मन्त्रमहेश्वर - mantramaheśvara**

A great lord of the mantra class.

Now the distinction is important.

We're not simply discussing somebody who recites a mantra extremely loudly.

Nor somebody who has collected a million mantras.

The term denotes a class of divine beings in the tradition's cosmological system.

The commentary describes the transformation or elevation of certain practitioners into such forms through the operation of grace.

These are traditional theological claims, not independently established observations about supernatural beings.

But their philosophical significance is clear.

**The divine powers mediate different degrees of conscious agency and manifestation.**

The limited individual doesn't become a supreme divine being merely through the assertion that everything is Śiva.

The tradition distinguishes levels of realization, agency and grace.

And Abhinavagupta incorporates those distinctions into his account of purification.

### Saṃpanna - सम्पन्न

Become, accomplished, endowed with, having attained.

From:

**सम् - sam-**

Together, fully.

And a formation of √**pad**, to go or attain.

Thus:

**सम्पन्न - saṃpanna**

Having attained or become.

The form appears in the description of beings attaining higher divine conditions.

Now, this is an important word to recognize because it frequently appears in Sanskrit texts concerning accomplishment and transformation.

### What is actually transformed?

At the metaphysical level, universal consciousness is not being improved.

The limited subject's condition is transformed within the manifested order.

The restrictions associated with its former state no longer define its operation in the same way.

And that transformation is attributed to the grace of Śakti.

Now here's the very important distinction.

**The universal ground isn't produced by the transformation; the manifested condition of the subject changes.**

That is why the cosmological classifications remain meaningful even within a nondual ontology.

And now Abhinavagupta introduces another family of goddesses.

The powers beginning with Brāhmī.


## 15. Brāhmī and the divine powers - The Goddess delegates the cosmos

The next movement mentions:

**ब्राह्मी - Brāhmī**

And the powers beginning with her.

Now this word is beautiful.

### Brahmā - ब्रह्मा

The creator deity Brahmā.

### Brāhmī - ब्राह्मी

The feminine form associated with Brahmā, a goddess belonging to the *Mātṛkā* group.

Notice the long initial ā and final ī.

**ब्रा - brā**

The consonant cluster:

**ब्र = ब् + र**

With the long ā vowel.

Then:

**ह्मी - hmī**

The conjunct:

**ह्म = ह् + म**

With the long ī.

So:

**ब्राह्मी - brāhmī**

Now the commentarial point.

Abhinavagupta describes the grace of the powers beginning with Brāhmī as associated with the transformation of more limited practitioners into divine forms such as Brahmā and Viṣṇu.

Again, we are inside the Śaiva cosmological account.

This is not a historical claim that a particular person has empirically been observed becoming the creator deity Brahmā.

It explains how the tradition assigns divine agency within the hierarchy of manifestation.

### The corresponding divine family

In related Śākta iconography, Brāhmī belongs to a group of Mother-goddesses or *Mātṛkās*.

An eightfold arrangement is commonly given as Brāhmī, Māheśvarī, Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī, Vārāhī, Indrāṇī, Cāmuṇḍā and Yogeśvarī.

But here's the textual distinction.

**Abhinavagupta's immediate prose here names Brāhmī and related powers without individually enumerating that complete eightfold list.** The full identification and detailed mantra segmentation are supplied in the parallel explanatory tradition. We should not silently insert the entire later gloss into his sentence.

### Aṇu - अणु

Now another fundamental Trika word.

**अणु - aṇu**

Minute, small, atomic.

And, in the Śaiva metaphysical context:

The limited individual subject.

Now compare:

**आणव - āṇava**

Pertaining to the limited individual.

The derivative involves an initial vowel strengthening associated with the formation.

So:

**aṇu → āṇava**.

Now:

**आणवमल - āṇavamala**

The impurity or limitation associated with finite subjectivity.

The present commentary's use of *sādhakāṇava* concerns practitioners characterized by limited subjecthood.

And the divine powers are associated with their possible elevation into more expansive roles.

### Why this matters to the threefold purification

Remember the structure.

The limited individual is the object of purification.

The divine powers perform the purifying function.

But those powers are themselves manifestations of the universal consciousness.

So the purifier is not ultimately an independent substance.

And the hierarchy of divine agents doesn't establish a universe composed of separate competing absolutes.

The hierarchy belongs to manifestation.

The ground remains Bhairava.

And now Abhinavagupta states that directly.


## 16. Bhairava manufactures the gods from his own freedom

This is one of the most important moves on p. 163.

Abhinavagupta identifies the Supreme Lord, Bhairava, as completely filled with the mass of his own powers.

And he describes the divine forms as arising through the operation of that freedom.

Let's learn the relevant compounds.

### Samagra-śakti-puñja

**समग्रशक्तिपुञ्ज - samagraśaktipuñja**

The complete mass or collection of powers.

Break it down.

**समग्र - samagra**

Entire, complete, whole.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

**पुञ्ज - puñja**

Mass, collection, heap.

Thus the complete totality of divine powers.

Now:

### Paripūrṇa

**परिपूर्ण - paripūrṇa**

Completely full.

And:

### Nirbhara

**निर्भर - nirbhara**

Full, abundant, replete.

The word is associated with the root-family of √*bhṛ*, to bear or support.

Together, these terms describe the divine reality as inexhaustibly full of its own powers.

Now the important thing.

Bhairava doesn't need to borrow creative ability from an independent deity.

The powers belong to his own nature.

And his manifestation of those powers is an expression of:

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Sovereign freedom.

### Nijaśakti - निजशक्ति

**निज - nija**

One's own, innate, belonging to oneself.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

Together:

**निजशक्ति - nijaśakti**

One's own power.

And:

### Niveśana - निवेशन

Installation, placing, entry or causing entry.

Related to *ni-√viś*, to enter, through a causative-related formation.

The commentary describes the divine forms as arising through the establishment or manifestation of Bhairava's own power.

Now here's the philosophical conclusion.

Brāhmī isn't an independent supernatural authority threatening Śiva's sovereignty.

Aghorā isn't an independent source of liberation.

The Mantramaheśvaras aren't ultimately self-existing gods.

Their divine capacities are manifestations of the one conscious freedom.

And because Bhairava is the ground of all the powers, the hierarchical distinctions can exist without creating independent ultimate substances.

### The divine corporation analogy

Imagine a very large organization.

Different departments have distinct functions.

One handles knowledge.

Another action.

Another communication.

Another the maintenance of particular structures.

Now you might mistakenly believe each department is an independent universe.

But their organization belongs to a larger whole.

The analogy is limited, because Bhairava isn't literally a corporate executive standing outside a company that exists independently of him.

Nevertheless, it illustrates the relationship.

The powers have distinct operations.

Their ground is one.

And within Trika's metaphysical claim, the universal consciousness is not merely an administrator supervising independent substances.

**The powers are manifestations of its own being.**

Now this returns us to the question of purification.

If the purifying powers themselves arise through Bhairava's freedom, how can they remain absolutely independent of the reality they purify?

They can't.

And Abhinavagupta now makes this explicit.


## 17. The purifier itself can become the thing to be purified

At the end of the relevant discussion, Abhinavagupta identifies another distinguishing excellence of the Trika teaching.

Even the purifier can occupy the position of what must be purified.

Now that sounds absurd if you imagine purification as an ordinary washing procedure.

You wash the cloth.

Then you clean the washing machine.

Then you wash the cleaning brush.

Then you clean the sink.

Then you have to purify the thing that cleans the sink.

Eventually the entire household becomes a recursive sanitation catastrophe.

But Abhinavagupta is making a much more precise philosophical point.

### The original three roles

**शोध्य - śodhya**

The object of purification.

**शोधन - śodhana**

The means of purification.

**शोधक - śodhaka**

The purifying agent.

Now suppose the purifier is apprehended as something completely separate from the supreme consciousness.

As an independent spiritual agent standing over against the thing being purified.

What has happened?

A distinction has been established.

A limited identification has appeared.

And insofar as that distinction is treated as ultimately independent, the purifier itself remains within the field of apparent bondage.

Thus it too can become *śodhya*, what is to be purified.

### Bheda - भेद

Difference, distinction.

From √**bhid**, to split.

And:

**भेदांश - bhedāṃśa**

An aspect or portion of differentiation.

Now:

**उच्छलत्ता - ucchalattā**

The condition of springing forth, emerging, surging upward.

Related to *ud-* and √*chal*, to move or stir.

So the appearance of a *bhedāṃśa*, a distinguishing aspect, can give the purifier a form associated with bondage.

Not because difference as an ordinary practical distinction is inherently evil.

But because the apparently separate status of the purifier can become another form of limiting identification.

### The spiritual supervisor problem

Imagine a practitioner who has recognized that ordinary thoughts aren't the ultimate Self.

Excellent.

Then they identify with being the one who watches thoughts.

Fine.

Then they begin to believe that the watcher is a separate independent spiritual entity.

"Those thoughts are impure."

"But I, the watcher, am absolutely pure."

Now Abhinavagupta asks:

What is the reality of that watcher?

Is it an independent substance?

Or another manifested form within consciousness?

If the practitioner clings to the separate identity of the watcher, the structure of limitation hasn't necessarily disappeared.

It's simply changed costume.

The ordinary ego has put on a saffron robe and acquired a more sophisticated vocabulary.

The underlying identification may remain.

Thus even the purifier must be understood through the universal ground.

### Why this isn't an infinite regress

Now a really serious objection appears.

If every purifier requires another purifier, won't the process continue forever?

The first purifier purifies the bound subject.

The second purifies the first.

The third purifies the second.

And so on.

You could construct an infinite tower of increasingly purified metaphysical supervisors.

But Abhinavagupta rejects this.

And here's the beautiful Sanskrit term:

**अनवस्था - anavasthā**

Nontermination, infinite regress, failure to reach a stable explanatory ground.

Break it down.

**अन् - an-**

Negative prefix.

**अवस्था - avasthā**

State, standing, established condition.

Thus:

**anavasthā**

Lack of final establishment.

In philosophical argument, it frequently means an infinite regress.

Now the resolution.

The purifier, means and object are not ultimately independent entities requiring an infinite chain of further independently existing purifiers.

Their reality is one supreme consciousness.

And that consciousness is not itself established through a separate external purifier.

The regress stops because the supreme conscious ground is not another item within the series.

**The means of purification can be transcended without requiring an infinite sequence of increasingly superior means.**

That's the crucial point.

And it brings us directly back to the instruction:

Abandon even that by which you abandon.


## 18. Why Trika is said to surpass Kula

Abhinavagupta now invokes a scriptural statement asserting the superiority of Trika over Kula.

The surviving Sanskrit transcription has a lacuna before the final words, so we should not reconstruct a full stanza from the incomplete witness.

The surviving ending is:

**कुलात्परतरं त्रिकम्।**

*Kulāt parataraṃ trikam.*

Trika is higher than Kula.

Now the grammar is extremely useful.

### Kulāt - कुलात्

From or than Kula.

**कुल - kula**

Family, collection, totality.

The ablative singular is:

**कुलात् - kulāt**

Now the comparative:

### Paratara - परतर

Higher, further, more excellent.

From *para*, higher or further, with a comparative formation.

Here:

**परतरम् - parataram**

Neuter nominative or accusative singular, agreeing with:

**त्रिकम् - trikam**

The triad.

So:

*kulāt parataraṃ trikam*

Trika is higher than Kula.

Now, what is the philosophical point?

It's not simply a historical claim that every tradition called Trika is objectively superior to every tradition called Kula by every possible criterion.

Abhinavagupta is presenting the self-understanding of his own Tantric system.

And within the present argument, the reason is particularly interesting.

**Trika does not stop with one distinction between the pure and impure. It understands the purifying relation itself through a triad that can be recursively examined.**

The object.

The means.

The purifier.

All three.

And the same threefold structure can reappear within the purifying power.

Thus the teaching can include the relation between those levels without treating any one differentiated function as an independent ultimate reality.

That's the philosophical significance of the claim in this passage.

### The triad of triads

Abhinavagupta then cites a verse of his own concerning a **threefold grouping of triads**.

The transmitted quotation is again incomplete in the consulted witness.

So I won't invent the missing beginning.

But the surviving words establish the relevant image:

A triad of triads.

Now think about that.

Three modes of speech.

Three divine powers.

Three functions of purification.

And within each, further threefold relationships can be distinguished.

The structure is not simply a static triangle.

It is a way of articulating the relationship between unity and differentiation at multiple levels.

And the important thing is that the multiplication of triads does not create a multiplication of independent absolutes.

The whole structure remains grounded in one consciousness.

### The mathematical trap

You might imagine:

Three functions.

Then three aspects of each function.

That's nine.

Then three aspects of each of those.

Twenty-seven.

Then eighty-one.

Then two hundred and forty-three.

And eventually you've got a rather impressive divine organizational chart.

But Abhinavagupta's argument isn't that the Absolute contains an infinite hierarchy of increasingly subdivided independent managers.

It's that **the differentiated structures are expressions of a single reality**, and their multiplication doesn't remove the need to understand that ground.

The triadic organization reveals the structure of manifestation.

It isn't a rival infinity to Bhairava.

And this is why Abhinavagupta insists there is no *anavasthā*, no infinite regress.

The ultimate ground isn't another member of the multiplied series.


## 19. The fire of Bhairava - The real meaning of purification

Now we reach one of the most beautiful formulations on p. 163.

Abhinavagupta identifies the ultimate means of purification with the fire of Bhairava-consciousness.

Now, let's be precise.

He is not prescribing that somebody physically burn impurities away from the body.

Nor is he giving an empirical claim that a ritual fire destroys psychological suffering through ordinary physical combustion.

The fire is a metaphor for the power of recognition.

### Hutavaha - हुतवह

Fire.

Literally, the bearer of offerings.

Break it down.

**हुत - huta**

Offered, sacrificed.

From √**hu**, to offer into fire.

And:

**वह - vaha**

Bearing, carrying.

Related to √**vah**, to carry.

Thus:

**हुतवह - hutavaha**

The carrier of offerings.

A poetic name for fire.

Now here's the symbolism.

The sacrificial fire receives and consumes oblations.

Similarly, the fire of Bhairava-consciousness is described as consuming the impurities associated with limited identification.

### Mala - मल

Impurity, stain.

You've encountered:

**आणवमल - āṇavamala**

The limitation of finite subjectivity.

**मायीयमल - māyīyamala**

Impurity associated with differentiated knowledge.

**कार्ममल - kārmamala**

Impurity associated with limited action.

And:

### Ploṣa - प्लोष

Burning, scorching, consuming by fire.

The word is associated with a verbal root meaning to burn or scorch.

Now the compound:

**सर्वमलप्लोष - sarvamalaploṣa**

Burning away all impurities.

And:

**चतुर - catura**

Skilful, capable, adept.

Thus the conscious fire is described as capable of consuming all limiting impurities.

### What is actually destroyed?

Now this is the important philosophical distinction.

The world isn't necessarily physically annihilated.

The senses don't need to be literally destroyed.

The limited person doesn't have to disappear as an empirical organism.

What is removed is the fundamental misrecognition through which differentiated manifestation is apprehended as independently ultimate and the subject exclusively as a finite, incomplete being.

And this is why the fire is **Bhairava-consciousness** rather than merely a physical ritual flame.

The metaphor concerns the transforming recognition of the reality underlying manifestation.

### Anupraveśa - अनुप्रवेश

Entering into, immersion, penetration.

From:

**अनु - anu**

Along, following, into in certain compounds.

And:

**प्रवेश - praveśa**

Entry.

So:

**अनुप्रवेश - anupraveśa**

Entry into, immersion or full penetration.

Now Abhinavagupta says that when all the differentiated functions enter into their conscious ground, the result is **paripūrṇatā**, complete fullness.

### Paripūrṇatā - परिपूर्णता

**परि - pari**

Completely.

**पूर्ण - pūrṇa**

Full.

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract state.

Thus:

**परिपूर्णता - paripūrṇatā**

The condition of complete fullness.

Now this is the crucial conclusion.

The object of purification.

The means.

The purifier.

All are understood as manifestations of one consciousness.

Their apparent independence dissolves in recognition.

And the resulting fullness is not a new substance added to experience.

It is the recognition of the reality that was never fundamentally absent.

**The fire does not manufacture Śiva. It destroys the misrecognition of what is already grounded in Śiva.**

That is the heart of the teaching.

## 20. The eight Yoginīs - The entire divine family reappears

Now, just when you think we're finally done with the theological hierarchy, Abhinavagupta introduces another scriptural quotation.

It concerns eight powerful Yoginīs arising as limbs or powers of Parāparā.

The opening names the eightfold group:

**परापराङ्गसम्भूता योगिन्योऽष्टौ महाबलाः।**

*Parāparāṅgasaṃbhūtā yoginyo 'ṣṭau mahābalāḥ.*

Eight exceedingly powerful Yoginīs arising as the limbs of Parāparā.

Now let's learn the Sanskrit.

### Aṣṭau - अष्टौ

Eight.

A numeral form.

Notice the retroflex:

**ष्ट = ष् + ट**

The word is not pronounced *astau* with two ordinary dental sounds.

It's *aṣṭau*.

And:

### Yoginyaḥ - योगिन्यः

Yoginīs.

Feminine nominative plural.

Singular:

**योगिनी - yoginī**

Plural:

**योगिन्यः - yoginyaḥ**

Notice the change.

The final long ī develops the *y* glide before the plural ending.

It's a common pattern in feminine *-ī* stems.

### Mahābalāḥ - महाबलाः

Very powerful, possessing great strength.

**महा - mahā**

Great.

**बल - bala**

Strength.

Thus:

**महाबला - mahābalā**

Greatly powerful.

And the feminine nominative plural:

**महाबलाः - mahābalāḥ**

Now the grammar agrees.

*Yoginyaḥ* is feminine nominative plural.

*Mahābalāḥ* is also feminine nominative plural.

They're describing the same eight Goddesses.

### Parāparāṅgasaṃbhūtāḥ

This is the really beautiful compound.

**परापरा - parāparā**

The intermediate Goddess.

**अङ्ग - aṅga**

Limb, part.

**सम्भूत - saṃbhūta**

Arisen, produced, become manifest.

From:

*sam-* \+ √*bhū*.

So:

**parāparā-aṅga-saṃbhūta**

Arisen as the limbs or powers of Parāparā.

Now the philosophical point.

The Goddesses are described as manifestations of Parāparā's own power.

They're not introduced as eight independently existing ultimate realities.

Their multiplicity belongs to the divine manifestation.

### The eight names

The accompanying tradition identifies the group as Brāhmī, Māheśvarī, Kaumārī, Vaiṣṇavī, Vārāhī, Indrāṇī, Cāmuṇḍā and Yogeśvarī.

But again, the immediate excerpt of Abhinavagupta's commentary does not independently provide a full eight-name enumeration at this point. The names and detailed mantra-syllable breakdown appear in the explanatory material accompanying the parallel edition.

Why cite the group here at all?

Because Abhinavagupta is establishing that the powers of Parāparā encompass differentiated manifestations far beyond a single ritual system.

The divine hierarchy includes multiple powers.

Those powers have relationships to mantra, cognition and the differentiated universe.

But their reality is grounded in the supreme consciousness.

And therefore they can be included within the broader claim that **all signifiers and signified objects are encompassed by the divine powers**.

### Vācaka and Vācya return

Remember:

**वाचक - vācaka**

Signifier.

**वाच्य - vācya**

Signified.

Now Abhinavagupta extends the scope beyond Tantric mantra.

He invokes the endless range of signifiers and signifieds, including ordinary worldly language and the language of other scriptures.

That's important.

The supreme reality isn't restricted to one sacred Sanskrit formula.

The power of expression encompasses the entire field of meaningful manifestation.

The Tantric alphabet is a privileged symbolic and contemplative system within the tradition, but the conscious ground of language isn't confined to one particular ritual vocabulary.

That's the culmination of the argument.


## 21. The final logical objections - No infinite regress, no overextension

Now we're almost at the natural boundary.

Abhinavagupta anticipates the familiar logical difficulties.

If all things are included within consciousness, doesn't the explanation become so comprehensive that it loses specificity?

If every arrangement is ultimately grounded in Śakti, couldn't any arrangement be treated as equally valid?

And if the purifier can be purified, doesn't the process continue infinitely?

We've already examined the last objection.

Now let's learn the broader technical vocabulary.

### Atiprasaṅga - अतिप्रसङ्ग

Overextension, an unacceptable consequence, the implication that an argument proves too much.

Break it down.

**अति - ati**

Excessive, beyond.

**प्रसङ्ग - prasaṅga**

Consequence, connection, implication, occasion.

So:

**अतिप्रसङ्ग - atiprasaṅga**

An overextended consequence.

Now imagine somebody saying:

"All sounds are manifestations of Śakti. Therefore every random sound sequence must possess the same ritual effect."

That would be an overextension.

The universal metaphysical ground does not eliminate the differentiated conditions governing specific manifestations.

### Ativyāpti - अतिव्याप्ति

Overpervasion, an excessively broad definition.

From:

**अति - ati**

Too much.

**व्याप्ति - vyāpti**

Pervasion, inclusion.

So:

**अतिव्याप्ति - ativyāpti**

A definition or inference covering things it shouldn't.

For example, defining a cow as "an animal with four legs" includes horses.

Too broad.

The definition has *ativyāpti*.

Now, within the present argument, Abhinavagupta insists that the universality of conscious manifestation doesn't require treating every particular ritual or linguistic classification as interchangeable.

The distinctions still have appropriate functions.

### Avyāpti - अव्याप्ति

Underextension, insufficient coverage.

The opposite logical problem.

A definition excludes something that should fall within its intended scope.

Now Abhinavagupta is defending an account that must avoid both extremes.

Not every particular mapping can be indiscriminately universalized.

But no legitimate manifestation is ultimately outside consciousness.

That's a demanding philosophical balance.

### Saṅketa again

**सङ्केत - saṅketa**

Convention.

We encountered this during the great alphabet dispute.

Ordinary signs and languages may involve conventional assignments.

But Abhinavagupta insists that the existence of convention does not establish an independent reality outside consciousness.

And the mere conventionality of a particular expression doesn't negate the ultimate ground through which it becomes meaningful.

Thus the system can accommodate the infinity of ordinary and sacred signifying relationships without losing its nondual foundation.

### The resolution

The different mappings, powers, functions and processes are included within the supreme consciousness.

Their conventional and ritual distinctions remain meaningful in their appropriate contexts.

The purifying process does not require an infinite series of independent purifiers.

And the universal ground does not make every particular arrangement function identically.

**One ultimate reality, differentiated manifestations, no independent second Absolute.**

That's the solution.

And now Abhinavagupta explicitly returns to the root Tantra.

The digression is complete.

The flowers have been dismissed.

The philosophers have been thoroughly interrogated.

The Goddess has recovered her full divine family.

And we're back to the actual Sanskrit verse.


## 22. The textual boundary - Back to the Moon and Sun

At Kashmir Series p. 164, Abhinavagupta announces his return to the subject under discussion.

He resumes explaining the opening of verse five:

**अथाद्यास्तिथयः सर्वे स्वरा बिन्द्ववसानगाः।**

*Athādyās tithayaḥ sarve svarā bindvavasānagāḥ.*

And specifically the relationship among:

**अकार - akāra**

The vowel *a*.

**काल - kāla**

Time, or the relevant temporal and divine power.

**सोम - soma**

Moon.

**सूर्य - sūrya**

Sun.

**अकुल - akula**

The transcendent Śiva reality.

And **कलना - kalanā**, the measuring, differentiating or articulating power.

Now here's why this is such a fitting place to stop.

The last three pages investigated whether time can be treated as a condition independent of consciousness.

Abhinavagupta argued that it cannot.

And now he's about to explain precisely how temporal and lunar-solar powers arise within the supreme phonemic manifestation.

The next movement is not an unrelated topic.

It's the direct metaphysical application of everything we've just established.

**Time belongs to manifestation. Consciousness isn't an object manufactured by time.**

And the Moon, Sun and vowel powers will now be interpreted through that relation.

That is Part XII.


## 23. The complete philosophical progression of Part XI

Let's gather the entire argument in order.

| Movement | What Abhinavagupta establishes |
|--------|--------|
| Three divine powers | The differentiated modes coexist within one conscious reality |
| Speaking, thinking, perceiving | Distinct conscious functions can be apprehended together |
| Śirīṣa flowers | Apparent simultaneity might conceal rapid succession |
| Time objection | Temporal determination itself requires explanation |
| Known versus knowing | A temporal condition cannot simply be assumed independent of awareness |
| Mutual dependence | Explaining cognition through time while establishing time through cognition risks circularity |
| Atom and action | Decomposing experience into minute events does not automatically explain their unity |
| Memory | Recollective continuity depends upon prior experience |
| Self-awareness | The ground of conscious connection isn't merely another disconnected object |
| Padārthapraveśanirṇaya | The fuller epistemological debate is explicitly referred elsewhere |
| Aghorā and Brāhmī | Divine powers manifest different levels of spiritual agency |
| Purifier becomes purified | Treating the purifying agent as independently ultimate reproduces limitation |
| Trika's triple structure | Object, means and agent are distinct yet grounded in one consciousness |
| Bhairava's fire | Recognition removes the apparent independence of limiting distinctions |
| Eight Yoginīs | The divine powers encompass the wider network of manifestation |
| Return to verse five | The argument now prepares the explanation of time, Moon and Sun |

Now here's what makes this passage particularly important.

Abhinavagupta hasn't simply asserted that all experience is simultaneous.

He's examined what simultaneity means.

He's distinguished experienced concurrence from the possibility of imperceptibly rapid succession.

He's challenged the assumption that time exists as a wholly independent metaphysical container.

He's appealed to the dependence of memory upon experience.

And he's connected the resulting argument with the same nondual ground that underlies the divine powers of purification.

The reasoning is not reducible to the statement:

"Everything happens at once."

That would be crude.

The more precise claim is:

**The conscious ground of differentiated experience is not itself constituted by the temporal relations through which differentiated events are apprehended.**

Succession can appear.

Simultaneity can appear.

Different cognitive functions can be distinguished.

But the ultimate reality of consciousness is not simply another event occupying a particular position in the sequence.

That's the point.

## 25. The final insight - Even time is not the landlord of consciousness

We've travelled a remarkable distance.

From the apparent simultaneity of speech and thought.

Through a hundred flowers.

Into the nature of time.

Then memory.

Then the relation between awareness and its objects.

Then the divine hierarchy of Aghorā, Brāhmī and the Mantramaheśvaras.

Then the self-purification of the purifier.

And finally the fire of Bhairava-consciousness.

But the argument is one continuous movement.

Abhinavagupta is refusing to let any differentiated manifestation become an independently ultimate reality.

Not a phoneme.

Not a tattva.

Not time.

Not an object of knowledge.

Not a purifying ritual.

Not even the divine agent carrying out purification.

All are intelligible through the one self-revealing conscious ground.

And that doesn't make them meaningless.

It explains their relationship.

The world isn't simply discarded.

The distinctions aren't all declared false.

They are understood as manifestations of Śakti.

And their ultimate reality is recognized as Bhairava.

**The same consciousness can manifest succession without being produced by succession, difference without becoming fundamentally divided, and purification without requiring an independently existing purifier outside itself.**

That's the heart of Part XI.

And now, naturally, having argued that time cannot exist independently of consciousness, Abhinavagupta is about to explain the Moon, Sun, lunar phases and the powers of time in excruciating Sanskrit detail.

The man simply refuses to stop.
