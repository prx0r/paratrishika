# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

Abhinavagupta's Absolute Masterclass in Consciousness, Language and the Supreme Reality

PART I - The Goddess Asks the Ultimate Question

Right, you magnificent little contraction of infinite consciousness. We're entering some seriously outrageous territory now.

You've survived the Śiva Sūtras. You've wrestled with the Paramārthasāra. You've followed Abhinavagupta through the Tantrasāra, where the entire universe turned out to be Śiva doing increasingly elaborate things with himself.

And now we've arrived at the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*.

This is where Abhinavagupta looks at the whole business of language and declares, essentially:

**You think you're speaking Sanskrit? My brother in Bhairava, the entire cosmos is already an act of speech.**

And then he proceeds to spend an extraordinary amount of time explaining the first question.

I'm not kidding. The first verse alone receives approximately fifty pages of commentary in the Kashmir Series edition. He explores sixteen interpretations of one word, *anuttara*. Sixteen. The man has barely opened his mouth and the metaphysical machinery is already operating at maximum capacity. ; \]

We'll follow the actual order: Abhinavagupta's five opening benedictory verses, the Goddess's first question, and the opening movement of his commentary. All original Sanskrit verses will be presented in Devanāgarī and IAST, with literal word analysis, sandhi, grammatical endings, and the deeper Trika meanings clearly distinguished.

## Prologue: What the hell does the title mean?

**परात्रीशिकाविवरण**

*Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*

Start by dividing the title:

**परात्रीशिका + विवरण**

*Parātrīśikā + vivaraṇa*

- **Parā (परा):** the Supreme, the transcendent Goddess, supreme Consciousness.
- **Trīśikā (त्रीशिका):** interpreted within the Trika tradition in relation to the three divine powers.
- **Vivaraṇa (विवरण):** an explanation, exposition, or detailed elucidation.

The name also occurs in forms such as *Parātriṃśikā* and *Parātriṃśaka*. These are textual title variants, so we shouldn't pretend they all have one uncomplicated grammatical derivation.

And *vivaraṇa* is a lovely word. It describes an exposition that opens something up, making its meaning apparent.

Essentially, the scripture is a compact teaching preserved as part of the Rudrayāmala tradition, and Abhinavagupta comes along and unfolds its implications across an enormous philosophical commentary.

The central subject is not merely *what* consciousness is.

It is how the infinite, undivided consciousness of Śiva can appear as thought, language, distinction, individual experience, and the entire universe without ever ceasing to be undivided.

Now the bastard is cooking.


## The five opening verses

Before the actual Tantra begins, Abhinavagupta composes five introductory verses. These belong to **Abhinavagupta's commentary**, not to the Goddess-Bhairava dialogue itself.

### Benediction I - The cosmic parents

ORIGINAL SANSKRIT

विमलकलाश्रयाभिनवसृष्टिमहा जननी भरिततनुश्च पञ्चमुखगुप्तरुचिर्जनकः।

तदुभययामलस्फुरितभावविसर्गमयं हृदयमनुत्तरामृतकुलं मम संस्फुरतात्॥१॥

*Vimalakalāśrayābhinavasṛṣṭimahā jananī bharitatanuśca pañcamukhaguptarucirjanakaḥ.*

*Tadubhayayāmalasphuritabhāvavisargamayaṃ hṛdayamanuttarāmṛtakulaṃ mama saṃsphuratāt.*

### The reading

Abhinavagupta begins with the Mother and Father: Śakti and Śiva, the divine pair whose inseparable union manifests the universe.

The Mother is the power of ever-new creation. The Father is fullness itself, whose fivefold divine power participates in the unfolding of that creation.

And Abhinavagupta prays that his own heart might blaze forth as the meeting place of their undivided reality, filled with the nectar of Anuttara.

In other words:

May my heart stop behaving like some miserable little private apartment belonging to an anxious individual and reveal itself as the limitless living expanse of Śiva and Śakti.

Beautiful.

But now look at what he's done with the Sanskrit.

**Abhinava (अभिनव)** means fresh, new, ever-renewed.

Break it down:

- **abhi (अभि)** - towards, in the direction of, intensely.
- **nava (नव)** - new, fresh.

The complete word means new, fresh or novel; its components do not independently establish every philosophical implication.

**Sṛṣṭi (सृष्टि)** means emission, creation, manifestation, from √*sṛj*, to release or send forth.

**Jananī (जननी)** means mother, she who gives birth, related to √*jan*, to generate or be born.

**Hṛdaya (हृदय)** means heart. In Trika, the divine Heart is not merely an organ or an emotional metaphor. It is the luminous centre of all manifestation, where subject and object are not ultimately separated.

**Visarga (विसर्ग)** means emission, sending forth, release.

And this last word is going to become absolutely crucial.

Remember the two dots of the Sanskrit visarga sign, **ः**?

In ordinary Sanskrit writing, the visarga represents a particular post-vocalic sound. But in Trika's phonemic symbolism, *visarga* also acquires a cosmological significance: the power of consciousness to emit manifestation.

This does **not** mean Sanskrit grammar literally proves the metaphysics. Abhinavagupta is taking the structure of sacred language and interpreting it as a revelation of consciousness.

Now here's the cheeky bit.

The stanza can also be read as an allusion to Abhinavagupta's actual parents: his mother Vimalā and his father Narasiṃhagupta.

So his very first verse carries a double meaning: the cosmic parents and the human parents through whom he came into existence.

The universe creates Abhinavagupta; Abhinavagupta writes about the universe creating itself.

The recursive audacity is already off the charts.

### Benediction II - The universe is inside her

ORIGINAL SANSKRIT

यस्यामन्तर्विश्वमेतद्विभाति बाह्याभासं भासमानं विशृष्टौ।

क्षोभे क्षीणेऽनुत्तरायां स्थितौ तां वन्दे देवीं स्वात्मसंवित्तिमेकाम्॥२॥

*Yasyām antar viśvam etad vibhāti bāhyābhāsaṃ bhāsamānaṃ viśṛṣṭau.*

*Kṣobhe kṣīṇe 'nuttarāyāṃ sthitau tāṃ vande devīṃ svātmasaṃvittim ekām.*

### The reading

Abhinavagupta bows to the one Goddess, the consciousness of one's own Self, within whom the entire universe shines.

During manifestation, the world appears external. When the agitation of differentiation subsides, that apparent exteriority is recognized as resting within Anuttara.

Now wait.

This is one of those sentences that can completely rearrange your understanding of experience.

Look at the Sanskrit:

**विश्वम् - viśvam**: the universe, everything.

**अन्तर् - antar**: within.

**विभाति - vibhāti**: shines forth, appears.

From *vi-* plus √*bhā*, to shine.

And:

**बाह्याभासम् - bāhyābhāsam**

Break this one down carefully.

- **bāhya (बाह्य)** - external, outward.
- **ābhāsa (आभास)** - appearance, manifestation, shining forth.

Together, *bāhyābhāsa* means an appearance of exteriority.

Not necessarily something entirely false.

Something **appearing as external**.

That's a huge difference.

Trika does not simply dismiss the world as a mistake. Manifestation is real as the self-expression of consciousness. What is mistaken is the assumption that its apparent separation constitutes ultimate independence from consciousness.

Imagine looking at a tree.

Ordinary experience says: here is me, the observer, and over there is the tree, an object.

Abhinavagupta is asking you to investigate the very experience in which that separation appears.

The visual form of the tree is present to awareness. Its greenness, its shape, its location, even its apparent distance, all arise as determinations of experience.

The world is experienced *as outside*, but that outside-ness itself is manifest within consciousness.

And Trika goes further than this phenomenological observation: consciousness is not merely the private awareness of one individual witnessing a world. Universal consciousness is the ground and reality of both observer and observed.

Now watch the other important expression.

**स्वात्मसंवित्तिम् - svātmasaṃvittim**

- **sva** - one's own.
- **ātman** - self.
- **saṃvitti** - consciousness, awareness, knowing.

There is sandhi here: *sva + ātma* becomes *svātma*, because **a + ā → ā**.

And the ending *-im* marks the feminine accusative singular: the Goddess whom he is praising.

The same reality is the awareness by which you know your own being.

No second universe required.

### Benediction III - The Trika itself

ORIGINAL SANSKRIT

नरशक्तिशिवात्मकं त्रिकं हृदये या विनिधाय भासयेत्।

प्रणमामि परामनुत्तरां निजभासां प्रतिभाचमत्कृतिम्॥३॥

*Naraśaktiśivātmakaṃ trikaṃ hṛdaye yā vinidhāya bhāsayet.*

*Praṇamāmi parām anuttarāṃ nijabhāsāṃ pratibhācamatkṛtim.*

Here we have the actual triad:

**नर**

nara

The limited experiencer, the individual.

**शक्ति**

śakti

Divine power, the dynamism of consciousness.

**शिव**

śiva

The supreme conscious reality.

**Nara-śakti-śivātmakaṃ trikam** means the triad constituted by nara, Śakti, and Śiva.

That word *trika* comes from *tri*, three.

But the three are not three entirely separate substances.

The Goddess holds this whole triad within her Heart and causes it to shine forth.

The limited human being is therefore not an alien object accidentally deposited in a universe controlled by Śiva.

Nara is Śiva appearing under conditions of limitation, through his own Śakti.

And then comes my favourite expression in the verse:

**प्रतिभाचमत्कृतिम् - pratibhācamatkṛtim**

*Pratibhā* refers to intuitive illumination, the immediate flash of understanding.

*Camatkṛti* suggests wonder, astonishment, delighted recognition.

The astonishing, self-luminous flash of consciousness.

This is not just dry epistemology. Abhinavagupta insists that consciousness possesses a reflexive, experiential vitality, a capacity to recognize and relish its own manifestation.

Śiva doesn't merely know.

He knows that he knows, and the self-revelation of that knowing is not something added from outside.

And now, my friend, we have arrived at the point where epistemology begins behaving suspiciously like ecstasy.

### Benediction IV - The first teacher

ORIGINAL SANSKRIT

जयत्यनर्घमहिमा विपाशितपशुव्रजः।

श्रीमानाद्यगुरुः शम्भुः श्रीकण्ठः परमेश्वरः॥४॥

*Jayaty anarghamahimā vipāśitapaśuvrajaḥ.*

*Śrīmān ādyaguruḥ śambhuḥ śrīkaṇṭhaḥ parameśvaraḥ.*

Glory to the primordial Guru, Śambhu, the Supreme Lord, of immeasurable greatness, who frees the multitude of bound beings.

Important words:

**Ādyaguruḥ** \= *ādya* \(first, original) + *guru* \(teacher).

**Paśu (पशु)** \= literally an animal, but in Śaiva theology, a bound individual soul.

**Pāśa (पाश)** \= a bond, fetter or noose.

Traditional Śaiva interpretation connects the bound *paśu* to the *pāśa* that restricts it. These are closely related in the theological symbolism, although that is not an excuse to invent simplistic grammatical derivations.

In this system, bondage involves the contractions and obscurations preventing recognition of one's own universal nature.

The *paśu* is not a fundamentally worthless being.

It's consciousness taking its own limitation as final.

And the primordial Guru reveals that limitation for what it is.

### Benediction V - Abhinavagupta enters the chat

ORIGINAL SANSKRIT

निजशिष्यविबोधाय प्रबुद्धस्मरणाय च।

मयाभिनवगुप्तेन श्रमोऽयं क्रियते मनाक्॥५॥

*Nijaśiṣyavibodhāya prabuddhasmaraṇāya ca.*

*Mayābhinavaguptena śramo 'yaṃ kriyate manāk.*

Now this is genuinely charming.

After all that cosmic ecstasy, he tells us why he's writing.

For the illumination of his students and to refresh the understanding of those already awakened to the teaching, Abhinavagupta undertakes this small effort.

A small effort.

My brother, you are about to write one of the most demanding metaphysical commentaries in the entire tradition.

But let's learn the Sanskrit.

| Sanskrit | Literal meaning |
|--------|--------|
| *nija* | Own, personal |
| *śiṣya* | Student, disciple |
| *vibodhāya* | For awakening or understanding |
| *prabuddha* | Awakened, well-informed |
| *smaraṇāya* | For recollection |
| *ca* | And |
| *mayā* | By me |
| *abhinavaguptena* | By Abhinavagupta |
| *śramaḥ* | Effort, labour |
| *ayam* | This |
| *kriyate* | Is done, is undertaken |
| *manāk* | A little |

Two fantastic grammatical lessons.

**मयाभिनवगुप्तेन** is actually:

*mayā + abhinavaguptena*

The final ā of *mayā* and the initial a of *abhinava* combine:

**ā + a → ā**

And **श्रमोऽयं** is:

*śramaḥ + ayam → śramo 'yam*

Here the final *-aḥ* before a following initial *a* becomes *-o*, and the initial a is represented by the avagraha **ऽ**.

So now whenever you see that little apostrophe-looking sign in Devanāgarī, you know something phonological has happened. An original vowel has been elided.

This is exactly the kind of Sanskrit literacy that makes reading these texts increasingly enjoyable.


## Chapter One - The Goddess asks

And finally, we reach the first actual verse of the Parātrīśikā.

PARĀTRĪŚIKĀ - VERSE 1

## श्रीदेव्युवाच

## अनुत्तरं कथं देव सद्यः कौलिकसिद्धिदम्।

## येन विज्ञातमात्रेण खेचरीसमतां व्रजेत्॥१॥

*Śrīdevy uvāca*

*Anuttaraṃ kathaṃ deva sadyaḥ kaulikasiddhidam.*

*Yena vijñātamātreṇa khecarīsamatāṃ vrajet.*



### Literal translation

The venerable Goddess said:

How, O Lord, does Anuttara immediately grant Kaulika perfection, by the mere recognition of which one may attain equality or identity with Khecarī?

Now imagine the scene.

The Goddess is sitting before Bhairava.

She doesn't ask how to become wealthy. She doesn't ask for a better rebirth. She doesn't ask for an impressive yogic trick or a list of seventeen breathing exercises.

She goes straight for the absolute maximum.

**How does recognition of ultimate Reality bring about immediate divine perfection?**

Not in twelve lifetimes.

Not after you've paid a subscription fee to the cosmic liberation department.

Immediately.

And then Abhinavagupta says: Excellent question. Before answering it, we're going to have to discuss the nature of language, consciousness, temporality, the Goddess, causation, and the entire metaphysical constitution of the universe.

Of course we bloody are.

## The Sanskrit laboratory

We're not going to learn these words as disconnected English approximations. We're going to see how they are actually built.

### 1. अनुत्तरम् - anuttaram

THE UNSURPASSABLE

Start with:

**uttara (उत्तर)**

This can mean higher, superior, later, subsequent, or an answer, depending on context.

It contains the comparative formation associated with elevation or what is further beyond.

Now add the negative prefix **an-**:

*an + uttara → anuttara*

The prefix is *an-* here because the following word begins with a vowel.

So *anuttara* is that above which there is nothing higher.

**The unsurpassable.**

And the final **-m** in *anuttaram* is a neuter singular ending.

In this verse, *anuttaram* refers to supreme Reality and is grammatically connected with *kaulikasiddhidam*.

But Abhinavagupta is about to do something extraordinary.

He will explain *anuttara* in sixteen distinct ways.

Because the word doesn't merely suggest the highest point on an enormous ladder.

There is no ladder beyond it.

And in the deepest interpretation, Anuttara is not even one item at the top of a hierarchy.

It is the reality within which hierarchies, distinctions and the very possibility of comparison appear.

The difference matters.

If you say Śiva is the highest *thing*, you've made him into a thing.

And now you need another metaphysical explanation for the existence of that thing.

Congratulations. You've created another problem.

Abhinavagupta refuses the premise.

### 2. कथम् - katham

HOW? IN WHAT MANNER?

The interrogative base *ka-* plus the adverbial formation *-tham* gives *katham*, meaning how or in what way.

The Goddess is asking about the manner of realization.

And notice something philosophically important.

She doesn't simply ask:

*What is Anuttara?*

She asks how Anuttara grants immediate perfection.

She is bringing together ontology and realization.

What is real?

And how is it recognized?

For Trika these cannot be separated indefinitely, because the supreme reality is consciousness itself, not a remote object waiting to be discovered by another consciousness.

### 3. सद्यः - sadyaḥ

IMMEDIATELY

This is an indeclinable adverb meaning straightaway, at once.

The final sign **ः** represents visarga.

Notice that this is an actual Sanskrit phonological feature, independently of the later mystical meaning Abhinavagupta gives to *visarga*.

In *sadyaḥ kaulika-*, the final visarga is followed by *k*.

In careful traditional recitation, the realization of visarga can be influenced by the following consonant and by the recitational tradition.

But the meaning remains:

**Immediately.**

And that's philosophically explosive.

A result normally appears after a cause has operated.

So how can the highest realization be immediate?

The Trika answer cannot simply be that somebody performs a ritual ten thousand times more quickly.

It must concern the nature of what is being realized.

If supreme consciousness is already the ground of experience, its recognition is not the production of something previously absent.

### 4. कौलिकसिद्धिदम् - kaulikasiddhidam

This glorious compound deserves to be dismantled.

*Kaulika + siddhi + dam*

First:

**Kula (कुल)**

Ordinarily, a family, clan or group.

In Kaula theology, the word acquires a much larger meaning, referring to integrated manifestation and the totality of divine powers.

One important contrast is between **kula**, the field of manifested diversity, and **akula**, the transcendent dimension of Śiva, though the two are ultimately inseparable.

From *kula* we have the derivative forms *kaula* and *kaulika*.

The vowel transformation **u → au** is an example of Sanskrit vowel strengthening, *vṛddhi*, in derivative formation.

And the **au** sound is represented by:

**औ**

So:

कु - *ku*

कौ - *kau*

Different vowel, different written sign.

Next:

**Siddhi (सिद्धि)**

From √*sidh*, to succeed, accomplish, attain.

It can signify attainment, perfection or spiritual accomplishment.

And finally:

**-da (द)**

A giver, from √*dā*, to give.

In this compound *-dam* means granting or giving, with the neuter ending agreeing with *anuttaram*.

Thus:

**kaulika-siddhi-da**

That which grants Kaulika perfection.

But this is not necessarily a promise of supernatural abilities.

The central philosophical interpretation concerns the perfection of consciousness in which the individual recognizes its inseparability from the divine totality.

So don't read it as:

Learn the right spell and unlock premium wizard powers.

Read it as:

What is the perfection in which the contracted experiencer knows itself as the very reality of the whole?

That's the bigger question.

### 5. विज्ञातमात्रेण - vijñātamātreṇa

This one is particularly beautiful.

Break it into:

**vi + jñāta + mātreṇa**

The verbal root is √**jñā**, to know.

The prefix **vi-** can indicate distinction, differentiation or particularization.

*Vijñāta* means known, understood or recognized.

Then:

**mātra (मात्र)** - mere, only, simply the measure or extent of something.

And the ending:

**-eṇa** - instrumental singular.

The compound expresses the idea of **by the mere knowing or recognition**.

Not by building a new reality.

Not by manufacturing Śiva in a laboratory.

By recognition.

This is where the relationship with *pratyabhijñā*, recognition, becomes interesting, although the terms are not grammatically identical.

**Pratyabhijñā** involves recognizing what is already somehow known but not presently recognized in its true identity.

A traditional analogy is seeing someone and suddenly recognizing: this is that very person I knew before.

In the Trika application, the recognition is radically deeper.

It is not merely recognizing a particular object.

It is recognition of the universal nature of the experiencer.

One important qualification: *vijñātamātreṇa* does not automatically mean a shallow intellectual statement such as "I am Śiva" is equivalent to realization. The kind of knowing at issue is transformative recognition, not just possession of a proposition.

### 6. खेचरीसमताम् - khecarīsamatām

Two words brought together:

**Khecarī + samatā**

Let's break down *khecarī* first.

- **kha (ख)** - sky, space, expanse.
- **carī (चरी)** - moving, going, from √*car*, to move.

So *khecarī* is she who moves through space.

But here the space is not merely the physical sky.

Within Trika, Khecarī can denote a divine power of consciousness associated with the freedom of awareness.

The suffix gives a feminine form.

Then:

**samatā (समता)**

From *sama*, equal, even, alike.

The abstract noun *samatā* means equality or sameness.

And *samatām* is accusative singular, the state one reaches.

So *khecarīsamatāṃ vrajet* means one might attain sameness with Khecarī.

Not necessarily float above the floor.

It is about identity with a divine mode of consciousness, rather than remaining confined to an apparently isolated personal awareness.

### 7. व्रजेत् - vrajet

From √*vraj*, to go, proceed, attain.

The form is third-person singular optative.

It means **one might attain**, **one should reach**, or **one would come to**, depending on context.

Notice the consonant cluster in Devanāgarī:

**व्र = व् + र**

Here the *r* is attached after the *v* as a consonant conjunct. This is different from the repha sign used when *r* comes first in certain consonant clusters.

So *vrajet* is not *varajet*.

The implicit *a* after व has been suppressed by the consonant combination.

And now the whole verse becomes something you can actually read and hear meaning in, rather than simply memorize.


## Now Abhinavagupta starts explaining

Here's where the commentary gets wild.

The original begins by discussing supreme Śiva and his divine Śakti, particularly the power of grace, **anugraha**.

He insists that the Goddess is not ultimately separate from Śiva.

An important line of the Sanskrit commentary is:

**नहि शक्तिः शिवाद्भेदमामर्शयेत्।**

*Na hi śaktiḥ śivād bhedam āmarśayet.*

That is, Śakti does not conceive of herself as separate from Śiva.

The relationship is not between two independent beings.

Śakti is Śiva's own power of knowing, willing, manifesting and recognizing.

But Abhinavagupta is asking something subtler still.

**What makes the Goddess's question itself possible?**

Because there's already a problem here.

The Goddess says something.

Bhairava will respond.

We seem to have two speakers, two acts, and an interval between question and answer.

But if all is one consciousness, what is actually happening?

Welcome to the metaphysics of speech.

## The four levels of Vāk

*Vāk (वाक्)* means speech, word or expressive power.

Abhinavagupta treats language not merely as a human convention, but as something emerging from increasingly differentiated levels of consciousness.

**1. परा - Parā** (Undivided)

Supreme speech. Before the distinction between word, meaning, speaker and spoken object.

Emerging differentiation

**2. पश्यन्ती - Paśyantī**

The seeing or visionary level. Meaning begins to become distinguishable, without fully articulated separation.

**3. मध्यमा - Madhyamā**

Inner speech. The distinction between the signifying word and what it signifies becomes mentally apparent.

**4. वैखरी - Vaikharī**

Articulated speech. Words manifest in their differentiated, audible form.

This is a schematic account, not four separate substances or a universal empirical neuroscience model.

Let's make it funny and practical.

Imagine you're thinking about a mango.

At the level of articulated speech you say:

"I want a mango."

Congratulations. *Vaikharī*.

A moment before that, the entire sentence exists as an internal linguistic intention.

*Madhyamā*.

Before this, an apprehension of the desired fruit may be present without its explicit formulation in words.

This provides a rough analogy for *Paśyantī*.

But **Parā is not simply a still earlier unconscious thought about mangoes.**

It is the undivided ground of awareness from which the entire event of wanting, knowing, naming, and experiencing is possible.

This distinction is essential.

Otherwise we turn a metaphysical teaching into a very elaborate description of thinking quietly.

Now consider what Abhinavagupta is doing.

The Goddess's question begins in consciousness itself.

At the supreme level, question and answer are not separate.

At subtler levels, their distinction begins to emerge.

At the level of articulated speech, the Goddess asks and Bhairava answers.

And the reader believes there is a dialogue occurring between two independent entities.

Abhinavagupta effectively says:

**My friend, consciousness is having a conversation with itself, and you've mistaken the conversation for evidence that there are two ultimate speakers.**

But he's not dismissing the dialogue as meaningless.

The differentiation is itself a real manifestation of divine power.

That's the extraordinary Trika move.

Difference exists as manifestation, without establishing ultimate separation.

## Why does the Goddess say “uvāca”?

Now here's a peculiarly Abhinavaguptan moment.

The opening says:

**श्रीदेव्युवाच - Śrīdevy uvāca**

The Goddess *said*.

Why the past tense?

If supreme consciousness is not fundamentally located in ordinary time, why is the scripture referring to her as though she spoke at some previous moment?

This is not just a grammatical curiosity for Abhinavagupta.

It points to the relationship between timeless consciousness and temporal manifestation.

At the undivided level, the distinction between having spoken, speaking now, and speaking later does not apply in the ordinary manner.

But once consciousness manifests the structures of differentiated cognition, sequence becomes possible.

Past and future are experienced.

The question becomes a prior utterance, and the answer becomes something following it.

Yet the underlying consciousness remains the condition of the entire sequence.

Abhinavagupta develops this through comparisons involving states that are apprehended retrospectively, including the report of having spoken during sleep or intoxication. He is not equating supreme realization with unconsciousness; the comparison concerns the way a state can subsequently be referred to as past without its original character being that of ordinary differentiated cognition.

So even the word **uvāca** matters.

And you thought he was going to let the introductory formula slide.

Absolutely not.

Every syllable is getting investigated.

## The philosophical punchline of Part I

Look at what has happened already.

The Goddess asks how the unsurpassable grants immediate perfection through recognition.

But Abhinavagupta begins by showing that the very possibility of asking and answering depends upon the supreme consciousness whose realization she asks about.

The question is not external to its answer.

The teacher is not ultimately external to the student.

Language is not ultimately external to what it expresses.

And Śakti is not ultimately external to Śiva.

That does **not** eliminate the practical distinction between teacher and student, or make every ordinary utterance automatically liberating. Abhinavagupta is identifying the deeper ground in which these distinctions arise, and from which liberating recognition becomes possible.

The entire *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* will increasingly investigate how **sound, the alphabet, mantra, and the great awareness expressed as *aham* - I - disclose the creative freedom of consciousness**.

The funny thing is that the Goddess's first question already contains most of the problem.

How can knowing reality grant perfection immediately?

Because, from this perspective, liberation is not the acquisition of a second reality.

It is the recognition of what consciousness has always been, even while appearing as a limited experiencer.

And that's where Abhinavagupta is leading us.

Not to a bigger philosophical concept.

To the very light in which concepts, questions, and answers appear.
