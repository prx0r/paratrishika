# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa

PART XIV - Vowel Sandhi and the Trident of Śakti

*Abhinavagupta takes the Sanskrit rules for combining vowels, turns them into a theology of divine interpenetration, summons Patañjali to defend an exceptionally inconvenient pronunciation, and then makes the entire universe collapse into one tiny dot before Śiva exhales it all again.*

Commentary on verses 5-8 · Kashmir Series, pp. 174-178


## Previously, in the cosmic madhouse…

We've finally reached the point where ordinary Sanskrit grammar and Tantric metaphysics are about to collide head-on.

In Part XIII, we discovered that *kriyāśakti*, the power of action, emerges through the intermingling of will and knowledge. The six original vowel-powers remain grounded in Anuttara and Ānanda; the Absolute does not cease to be complete when manifestation begins.

Then came four more vowel-powers:

**ऋ ॠ ऌ ॡ** - *ṛ, ṝ, ḷ*, and the specially extended vocalic *ḷ*.

Abhinavagupta associated them with entry into luminous and motionless conditions, and compared them to **burnt seeds**. Not because they're useless, but because their particular generative function is attenuated without their divine seed-character being completely lost.

And now, at the end of p. 174, he announces the next movement.

The earlier vowels are going to enter one another.

Not as independent substances being mechanically glued together, but as distinguishable powers manifesting within a common conscious reality.

The sounds **ए ऐ ओ औ - e, ai, o, au** will express those relationships.

The fascinating part is that Abhinavagupta draws on real Sanskrit phonological rules. He doesn't merely invent random sound associations.

But he also gives those rules a Tantric interpretation that goes well beyond ordinary historical linguistics.

Keeping those two levels distinct is the key to understanding this installment.

And once we've reached *au*, the entire triad of will, knowledge and action will be gathered into **bindu**, then manifested outward through **visarga**.

The sacred alphabet has become a model of consciousness returning to itself and expressing itself again.

Right. Let's see how the bastard works.


## 1. The next movement - Will and Īśana enter the supreme ground

At Kashmir Series p. 174, Abhinavagupta begins with the compound:

**तदेवमिच्छेशनम्**

*Tad evam iccheśanam.*

"Thus, will and Īśana..."

The full sentence explains how these powers enter the prior domains of Ānanda and Anuttara without losing their own nature. He then names the emerging vowel:

**ए - e**. ; \]

Let's unpack the grammar.

### Icchā - इच्छा

Will, desire, intention.

In the preceding phonemic sequence, this corresponds principally to **इ - i**.

But we've already learned that supreme *icchā* isn't an ordinary craving caused by incompleteness.

It is a manifestation of divine freedom.

### Īśana - ईशन

Sovereign power, mastery, the exercise of lordship.

Associated here with **ई - ī**.

The previous discussion treated *ī* as will oriented toward the emergence of knowledge.

Now these two powers are considered together.

### Iccheśana - इच्छेशन

A compound involving *icchā* and *īśana*.

The joining produces:

*icchā + īśana → iccheśana*.

Why?

Because **ā + ī → e** under the ordinary vowel-sandhi rules.

So the very word naming will and Īśana demonstrates the kind of vowel combination we're studying.

That's rather wonderful.

The grammar has become an illustration of the argument.

But don't confuse the two operations.

The ordinary sandhi explains how the Sanskrit compound is pronounced.

The Tantric interpretation concerns the interaction of the divine powers that the vowels symbolize.

The first is phonological.

The second is theological.

### Ānandavapus - आनन्दवपुस्

**आनन्द - ānanda**

Bliss.

**वपुस् - vapus**

Form, body, nature.

Thus:

*ānandavapus* - having the form or nature of bliss.

And:

### Anuttaraparadhāman - अनुत्तरपरधामन्

The supreme abode or state of Anuttara.

**अनुत्तर - anuttara**

Unsurpassable.

**पर - para**

Supreme, transcendent.

**धामन् - dhāman**

Abode, domain, radiant locus or state.

These terms identify the domains into which the will-powers enter.

Now here's the important point.

The powers aren't importing some foreign material into Śiva.

They're entering the supreme conscious ground from which their very manifestation is already inseparable.

Abhinavagupta explicitly says the ground does not depart from its own nature.

The language of entry therefore describes a mode of conscious manifestation.

Not a physical journey.

### The divine immigration office

Imagine *i* approaching Anuttara.

"Hello. I'd like to enter the supreme state."

"Where are you coming from?"

"Your own freedom."

"And what will you become when you arrive?"

"A new phonemic expression of your own power."

"Excellent. No passport necessary."

The joke illustrates a serious point.

**The powers cannot become fundamentally foreign to the consciousness they express.**

Now the result is *e*.

And here's where ordinary Sanskrit sandhi becomes useful.


## 2. ए - E: The first compound vowel

# ए

e - the combined phonemic expression

The ordinary Sanskrit rule is:

**अ / आ + इ / ई → ए**

*a / ā + i / ī → e*

In standard external vowel sandhi, both short and long members of the two vowel classes yield *e*.

Try the ordinary vowel combination

First vowel

अ

आ

Second vowel

इ

ई

# ए

The selected combinations all yield *e* in the ordinary guṇa-sandhi rule. This is an illustrative grammar exercise, not a mantra prescription.

Now Abhinavagupta cites the grammatical statement:

**अवर्ण इवर्णे ए**

*Avarṇa ivarṇe e.*

The *a*-class before the *i*-class yields *e*.

### Avarṇa - अवर्ण

The class of *a* vowels.

**वर्ण - varṇa**

Letter, phoneme, sound-category.

So:

**अवर्ण - avarṇa**

The *a*-class.

Here this expression refers to the phonetic category, not simply an adjective meaning "colourless."

### Ivarṇa - इवर्ण

The *i*-class.

In the expression:

**इवर्णे - ivarṇe**

The locative singular specifies the environment: before or in the presence of an *i*-class vowel.

And:

**ए - e**

The result.

That's a compressed grammatical formulation.

Now, this is *guṇa* sandhi, not an arbitrary sacred combination.

Sanskrit grammar independently recognizes the phonological result.

### But here's the distinction

Ordinary Sanskrit grammar explains why *a + i* is realized as *e* in the relevant sandhi environment.

Abhinavagupta uses that relationship as an image of **the entry of the will-powers into the supreme ground**.

These are two different kinds of explanation.

The first describes conventional sound behaviour.

The second gives a doctrinal interpretation of the sound.

You should understand both without pretending the linguistic rule proves the theology.

### The order matters

Now comes an absolutely crucial point.

**अ + इ → ए**

*a + i → e.*

But:

**इ + अ → य**

More precisely, *i* before another vowel develops a *y*-glide, so *i + a* is represented as *ya*, not *e*.

The order is not interchangeable.

This is exactly why Abhinavagupta distinguishes the entry of the will-power into Anuttara from the reverse relationship.

And it leads directly to the next question.

If *a + i* and *ā + i* normally yield the same *e*, does the distinction between Anuttara and Ānanda completely disappear?

Abhinavagupta says no.

There's a subtle difference in the modes of manifestation.

And this is where Patañjali enters.


## 3. One vowel, two degrees of manifestation - The shorter and fuller *e*

Now the argument becomes more delicate.

If the will-power enters Anuttara, **अ - a**, or Ānanda, **आ - ā**, both combinations ordinarily yield *e*.

So why should Abhinavagupta distinguish them?

His answer uses two terms:

**स्फुटता - sphuṭatā**

Distinctness, explicit manifestation.

And:

**सूक्ष्मता - sūkṣmatā**

Subtlety.

He associates entry into the Ānanda state with the more explicit form, and contact with Anuttara with a subtler form.

The phonological issue is that ordinary Classical Sanskrit recognizes *e* as long, whereas some older recitational traditions acknowledge a shorter realization. ; \]

### Sphuṭa - स्फुट

Clear, distinct, manifest, fully evident.

Notice the conjunct:

**स्फ = स् + फ**.

The related noun:

**स्फुटता - sphuṭatā**

The condition of being explicit or distinct.

The suffix **-tā** forms an abstract noun.

Now:

### Sūkṣma - सूक्ष्म

Subtle, minute, delicate.

The conjunct:

**क्ष्म = क् + ष् + म**.

The noun:

**सूक्ष्मता - sūkṣmatā**

Subtlety.

Now we have two interpretive possibilities for the same vowel-class.

One more distinctly manifest.

One comparatively subtle.

But the ordinary phonology doesn't supply two standard Classical Sanskrit *e* phonemes corresponding to these categories.

Abhinavagupta therefore appeals to evidence from a specific Vedic recitational tradition.

And that's the interesting thing.

He isn't content to say:

"Well, divine freedom permits a short *e*, therefore a short *e* must exist in ordinary Sanskrit."

He invokes grammatical authority.

In fact, one of the most formidable authorities imaginable.

**Patañjali.**

### The distinction between *e* and a short *e*

In standard Classical Sanskrit, **ए - e** is traditionally a long vowel.

It isn't normally paired with an independently recognized short **e** in the same way that:

**इ - i** and **ई - ī**

form a short-long opposition.

This is precisely what makes the passage unusual.

Abhinavagupta discusses a more subtle or shorter manifestation of *e* and invokes a special recitational precedent.

That precedent is important.

It shows that the sound-category was not entirely without historical grammatical discussion.

But it doesn't mean you should now pronounce every ordinary Sanskrit *e* as short whenever you see the vowel associated with Anuttara.

The evidence is narrower.

And the distinction between sacred phonemic symbolism and ordinary linguistic practice remains.

### The philosophical payoff

Now consider the two divine states.

**Anuttara** is supreme conscious freedom.

**Ānanda** is its blissful expansion.

Will may be considered as entering either.

The outward phonemic result belongs to the *e*-class.

But the degree of explicitness differs.

So the same general phonemic form can disclose different aspects of the divine relationship.

Not because there are two independent consciousnesses fighting for possession of the letter.

But because the manifestation can be considered through different relations within one awareness.

And now Patañjali is called as an expert witness.


## 4. Patañjali enters the courtroom - A half-*e* and half-*o* in the Sāmaveda

Abhinavagupta introduces the authority as:

**भगवान् भुजगविभुः - bhagavān bhujagavibhuḥ.**

The venerable Lord of Serpents.

In this context, the identification is **Patañjali**, traditionally associated with the serpent Ananta.

And the cited grammatical observation concerns the Sāmavedic schools known as the Sātyamugris and Rāṇāyanīyas.

The quotation includes:

**अर्धमेकारम् अर्धमोकारम्**

*Ardham ekāram ardham okāram.*

A half-*e* and a half-*o*.

Abhinavagupta is drawing on a real grammatical report concerning the treatment of these vowels in particular Sāmavedic recitations. Independent grammatical references confirm the association with Patañjali's *Mahābhāṣya* and these two recitational schools. ; ; \]

Now, let's actually understand the Sanskrit.

### Chandogānām - छान्दोगानाम्

**छान्दोग - chāndoga**

A singer or reciter associated with the Sāmaveda.

The root sense is associated with *chandas*, Vedic metre or hymn.

The genitive plural:

**छान्दोगानाम् - chāndogānām**

Of the Sāmavedic chanters.

Notice the long initial:

**छा - chā**.

The consonant **छ** is an aspirated palatal stop.

It isn't the same as:

**च - ca**.

### Sātyamugri - सात्यमुग्रि

The name of a Vedic recitational lineage or school.

This is a proper name.

We shouldn't take the syllables apart and manufacture an esoteric meaning from them merely because some elements resemble familiar Sanskrit roots.

Proper names have historical and textual contexts.

### Rāṇāyanīya - राणायनीय

Another Sāmavedic recitational school.

Notice:

**णा - ṇā**.

The retroflex nasal.

And:

**यी - yī**.

The long ī.

The transmitted quotation coordinates the names in a compact grammatical construction. Different scholarly discussions describe the Sātyamugris as a subdivision or associated group of the Rāṇāyanīya tradition, rather than simply assuming two fully unrelated schools.

### Ardha - अर्ध

Half, part.

Now:

**अर्धम् - ardham**

Half.

In this grammatical report, it concerns the duration or form of the vowel.

Not literally cutting a written character into two pieces.

### Ekāra - एकार

The vowel *e*.

**ए - e**

Plus:

**कार - kāra**

A common suffix-like naming element in phonemic terminology.

Thus:

**एकार - ekāra**

The *e*-sound.

And:

**ओकार - okāra**

The *o*-sound.

Now the important point.

Patañjali's observation is not a general declaration that all Sanskrit *e* and *o* vowels are short.

It's a report about particular recitational practices.

The grammatical tradition recognizes the exceptional status of those realizations.

And Abhinavagupta uses that exceptional precedent to support his discussion of subtle and fully manifest vowel-forms.

### Why this matters

The opponent might object:

"There's no short *e* in ordinary Sanskrit. You've invented it because your metaphysics requires one."

Abhinavagupta can answer:

"Not quite. There are special recitational precedents."

And that is precisely why he invokes Patañjali.

Now the citation doesn't establish the Tantric interpretation.

Patañjali is not thereby endorsing Abhinavagupta's entire metaphysics of Anuttara and Ānanda.

The grammatical evidence supports the possibility of a relevant phonetic distinction in the cited tradition.

The theological significance is a further interpretive claim.

That's the intellectual separation we need.

### The snake-god and the vowel dispute

Imagine Abhinavagupta calling Patañjali into a philosophical courtroom.

"Sir, is it true that certain Sāmavedic schools use shorter *e* and *o* sounds?"

"Yes, in the specified recitational context."

"Excellent."

"Does that mean Śiva has personally manifested the power of bliss through every instance of *e*?"

"That is not a question of ordinary grammar."

Exactly.

Two different inquiries.

Both legitimate within their respective disciplines.

But one doesn't automatically prove the other.

And Abhinavagupta's discussion becomes much clearer once we retain that distinction.


## 5. The vernaculars and the Śaiva ritual systems - Why *e* and *o* can have special values

Now the commentary continues immediately.

Abhinavagupta points to regional vernacular speech, including *Prākṛta* language varieties, as further evidence that pronunciation need not be restricted to one idealized Sanskrit pattern.

And he notes that Śaiva scriptural systems make distinctions involving the relative shortness of **e** and **o** when compared with **ai** and **au**, especially in ritual assignments involving parts or faces.

Now this is a very interesting historical observation.

Because the sound-inventory of Classical Sanskrit is not identical in every respect with the phonetic inventory of every Indo-Aryan vernacular or Vedic recitational tradition.

The grammar of Sanskrit supplies an organized description of its own language.

But Abhinavagupta is aware that actual linguistic and ritual practice is more diverse.

### Loka - लोक

World, ordinary usage, common people.

The locative singular:

**लोके - loke**

In the world, in ordinary practice.

### Prākṛta - प्राकृत

Natural, ordinary, vernacular, and the name applied to several Middle Indo-Aryan language traditions.

Related to:

**प्रकृति - prakṛti**

Nature, original form.

But a warning.

*Prākṛta* isn't a single uniform language with one pronunciation system.

The broader term covers different regional and literary varieties.

So we should not infer one precise universal phonetic rule from Abhinavagupta's passing reference to vernacular speech.

### Deśabhāṣā - देशभाषा

Regional language.

**देश - deśa**

Region, country, place.

**भाषा - bhāṣā**

Language, speech.

Together:

**देशभाषा - deśabhāṣā**

Regional speech.

Now the commentary's point is that the phonetic distinction has recognizable precedents outside the simplest description of the classical alphabet.

### Aṅga - अङ्ग

Limb, component, member.

In ritual contexts, the term can designate subsidiary mantra-components.

### Vaktra - वक्त्र

Mouth, face, speaking organ.

Related to √**vac**, to speak, through its established nominal formation.

In Śaiva mantra systems, *vaktra* can also designate a ritual face or one of the divine faces.

Abhinavagupta mentions such ritual assignments when discussing the relative quantity of the vowels.

Now we must be precise.

He is **not** saying that the vowels *e* and *o* always become short when they appear in sacred mantras.

He is discussing specific traditional uses where the distinction is relevant.

And he interprets those uses through the earlier relation between subtle and explicit manifestation.

### The actual conceptual distinction

A phoneme can have a conventional value within the language.

A special phonetic realization in a recitational tradition.

And a further esoteric significance within a Tantric mantra system.

These three mustn't be collapsed.

Consider **ए - e**.

Ordinary Sanskrit grammar: long *e*.

Special Vedic recitation: a shorter realization may occur in the cited traditions.

Tantric interpretation: the degree of manifestation symbolizes the way will enters Anuttara or Ānanda.

That's the relationship.

And now Abhinavagupta connects the vowels with two semivowel sequences.

**Aya** and **ava**.

Which brings us to another ordinary Sanskrit rule.


## 6. अय् and अव् - The hidden semivowels inside *e* and *o*

Now the commentary invokes the relations:

**अय् - ay**

And:

**अव् - av**.

These are important because Sanskrit sandhi does not only combine vowels into other vowels.

It also changes certain vowels into semivowel-containing sequences before following vowels.

The familiar rule is expressed in Pāṇinian grammar through the substitutions:

*e → ay*

*o → av*

*ai → āy*

*au → āv*

in the relevant vowel-following environment.

Now let's see why that matters.

### The first example: e before a vowel

Consider:

**हरे - hare**

A vocative form of *Hari*.

And:

**इह - iha**

Here.

When joined under the relevant external-sandhi rule:

*hare + iha → haray iha*.

The final **e** develops an **ay** sequence before the following vowel.

Depending on orthographic conventions, the resulting semivowel and word boundary are represented accordingly.

Now the important point isn't the devotional phrase.

It's the phonological alternation.

The vowel *e* has a relationship to *ay*.

And Abhinavagupta invokes that relationship as part of his explanation of the phonemic powers.

### The second example: o before a vowel

Consider:

**गो - go**

A form belonging to the noun *go*, cow.

In the appropriate environment, final *o* can develop an *av* sequence before a following vowel.

Thus the broader phonological relationship is:

**ओ → अव्**

*o → av*.

Now here's the crucial distinction.

This isn't a universal statement that you should pronounce the independent vowel *o* as *ava* whenever you encounter it.

The substitution is conditioned by the sandhi environment.

And a written phoneme's internal historical relationship is not identical with its ordinary pronunciation in every word.

The grammatical rule is conditional.

The Tantric symbolism interprets that conditional relationship.

### The theological significance

Now recall the previous sequence.

The divine will-powers enter the supreme ground and appear through *e*.

But that vowel remains capable of further phonological transformation.

It isn't an inert, independently sealed object.

Likewise, the power of knowledge can be expressed through *o*, and its phonological relations are available for symbolic interpretation.

The letters can change their outward form according to their relationships.

And Abhinavagupta uses these transformations as analogies for the mutual interpenetration of Śakti's powers.

Once again:

The grammar describes sound combinations.

The metaphysics describes conscious power.

The two illuminate one another in the commentator's system.

But they're not the same level of explanation.

### The alphabet's family connections

Think of a family tree.

A form can be related to another form.

But the relationship doesn't imply that all forms are interchangeable in every context.

Similarly:

*e* is connected with *a* and *i* through guṇa-sandhi.

It develops *ay* before a following vowel in the specified environment.

And it participates in the further formation of *ai* through vṛddhi-sandhi.

Different relations.

Different conditions.

One coherent phonological system.

Abhinavagupta's esoteric interpretation takes these relations as expressions of a deeper generative unity.

And now we reach the second compound vowel.

**ऐ - ai.**


## 7. ऐ - Ai: When the combined power enters the supreme ground again

# ऐ

ai - the further expansion of the combined power

Now we reach the next formation.

The ordinary Sanskrit rule is:

**अ / आ + ए / ऐ → ऐ**

*a / ā + e / ai → ai*

This is a *vṛddhi*-sandhi relation.

And the distinction between *guṇa* and *vṛddhi* is worth learning properly.

### Guṇa - गुण

Quality, constituent, property, strand.

In grammatical terminology, *guṇa* also names a particular grade of vowel transformation.

The common vowel relationships include:

**इ / ई → ए**

*i / ī → e*

And:

**उ / ऊ → ओ**

*u / ū → o*

When considered through the relevant grammatical operations.

### Vṛddhi - वृद्धि

Growth, increase, augmentation.

From √**vṛdh**, to grow or increase.

The familiar vowel-grade relationships include:

**इ / ई → ऐ**

*i / ī → ai*

And:

**उ / ऊ → औ**

*u / ū → au*

Again, the exact transformations depend on grammatical rules and environments.

Now, why is this relevant?

Because Abhinavagupta interprets the movement from *e* to *ai* as another level of the interpenetration of conscious powers.

The first compound vowel has already arisen.

Then it enters the prior divine ground in a more comprehensive manner.

And the phonemic result is *ai*.

### The ordinary grammar

Consider:

**अ + ए → ऐ**

*a + e → ai.*

And:

**आ + ए → ऐ**

*ā + e → ai.*

In the relevant sandhi environment, both produce the same resulting vowel.

But now note what Abhinavagupta is doing.

He isn't claiming that the physical sound *e* is a deity travelling across a metaphysical landscape.

He's interpreting the relationship between the sound-classes through a theory of conscious manifestation.

The *e*-power represents an earlier combination.

Its further entry into the supreme ground produces another differentiated expression.

And the distinction between *e* and *ai* becomes a symbol of increasing explicitness.

### Śabalībhūta - शबलीभूत

Now here's an interesting expression in the passage.

The GRETIL transcription gives:

**śabalībhūta**

Variegated, made multiform or mottled.

Related to:

**शबल - śabala**

Variegated, spotted, multicoloured.

And:

**भूत - bhūta**

Become.

So:

**śabalībhūta**

Having become variegated.

The Kashmir Series Devanāgarī appears to transmit **शवलीभूत** at this point, whereas the parallel transliteration has *śabalībhūta*. The latter is linguistically straightforward, but I wouldn't silently pretend that the two witnesses have identical spelling.

Now the image is beautifully appropriate.

Consciousness takes on differentiated appearances.

It becomes *variegated* in manifestation.

But the manifesting power hasn't become fundamentally independent of its own source.

Think of white light displaying different colours through an optical arrangement.

The colours are distinguishable.

Their manifestation isn't simply unrelated to the light.

Now the analogy is limited: the optics of white light don't establish a metaphysical theory of consciousness.

But it illustrates what *śabalībhūta* is doing.

**The one conscious reality appears through distinct forms without ceasing to be the ground of those forms.**

### Why *ai* is not a new independent power

Abhinavagupta's prose emphasizes that the same form continues in its own nature.

So don't imagine:

*a* is one substance.

*e* another.

*ai* a third.

Each is a completely independent consciousness.

That would be exactly the dualistic fragmentation he has spent the whole commentary rejecting.

The phonemic powers are distinguishable in expression.

Their ultimate reality is one.

And their combinations make the interdependence visible.

Now the same process occurs with the power of knowledge.

And that gives us **ओ - o**.


## 8. ओ - O: Knowledge enters the first and second vowels

# ओ

o - the combined expression involving the knowledge-power

The relevant ordinary Sanskrit rule is:

**अ / आ + उ / ऊ → ओ**

*a / ā + u / ū → o*

And that's another *guṇa*-sandhi relationship.

Now look at the Devanāgarī.

**अ - a**

**आ - ā**

**उ - u**

**ऊ - ū**

And:

**ओ - o**

The traditional grammar relates these vowel-classes through specific operations.

Now Abhinavagupta says that the same interpretive principle applies to **unmeṣa**, the opening of knowledge.

### Unmeṣa - उन्मेष

Opening, blossoming, emergence.

From a formation involving *ud-* and the root-family √*miṣ*, to open the eyes.

In the preceding sequence:

**उ - u** expressed the opening of *jñānaśakti*.

And:

**ऊ - ū** its fuller manifestation bearing differentiated contents.

Now those powers enter the supreme ground.

The result is expressed through *o*.

### Why the knowledge-power isn't entirely separate from will

Here's the crucial philosophical point.

Abhinavagupta explains that the knowledge-power, even when entering a condition associated with apparent emptiness or nonobjectification, includes the forms of will and Īśana.

So the power of knowledge isn't a completely independent substance that simply appears after will has vanished.

The earlier powers remain relevant.

And the resulting manifestation includes their relationship.

### The word unmeṣa does not mean physical blinking

This is worth repeating.

In ordinary language, *unmeṣa* can refer to opening the eyes.

But in Trika, it becomes a metaphor and technical term for the emergence of manifestation.

The divine power doesn't literally possess biological eyelids.

And the phoneme *u* doesn't physically originate from an eye opening.

The interpretation concerns the relation between **emerging awareness and the possibilities of manifestation**.

Now, at this stage, knowledge is no longer simply an unexpressed possibility.

Its power has become more explicit.

But the earlier will-powers haven't disappeared.

And now the next vowel gives us the further expansion of that relationship.

**औ - au**.

This is where the trident appears.

But first, the grammar.


## 9. औ - Au: The compound vowel that contains the trident

# औ

au - culmination of the inner expansion of kriyāśakti

Now the ordinary Sanskrit rule is:

**अ / आ + ओ / औ → औ**

*a / ā + o / au → au*

Again, *vṛddhi* sandhi.

The result is **औ - au**.

Let's put all four relationships together.

| अ / आ + ए / ऐ | | ## ऐ |
| अ / आ + उ / ऊ | | ## ओ |
| अ / आ + ओ / औ | | ## औ |

The grammatical transformations are regular sandhi relations. Their assignment to divine powers is Abhinavagupta's separate Tantric interpretation.

Now here's the important difference.

In the ordinary phonological explanation, *au* is the result of a vowel-combination.

Within the Tantric interpretation, it marks the full expansion of **kriyāśakti**, the power of action.

And here's why.

The combined expression contains the powers of:

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action.

Their manifestation has become complete in the *au*-stage.

And this is why Abhinavagupta identifies **औ - au** with the trident.

### Triśūla - त्रिशूल

**त्रि - tri**

Three.

**शूल - śūla**

Spear, point, trident component.

Thus:

**त्रिशूल - triśūla**

Trident.

Now here's the important thing.

**The trident is not being derived from the physical shape of the written character औ.**

The symbolic connection concerns the three divine powers present within the phonemic culmination.

One power of will.

One of knowledge.

One of action.

The threefold Śakti.

That is the trident.

### Why the three powers are not three independently ultimate realities

Imagine three departments of one process.

Will expresses orientation toward manifestation.

Knowledge expresses the intelligibility of what can be manifest.

Action expresses the ordered manifestation itself.

The three can be distinguished.

But none is ultimately independent of the self-revealing freedom of consciousness.

And the *au*-stage is interpreted as the culmination of their combined movement.

Now Abhinavagupta has already explained that *kriyā* arises through the intermingling of will and knowledge.

So the trident is not an unexpected decorative addition.

It is the logical result of the previous analysis.

**The three powers are present together in the complete expression of action.**

### The theological consequence

The supreme consciousness doesn't acquire a new independent substance called Action.

Its own freedom manifests as will, knowledge and action.

The differentiation is real within manifestation.

But the underlying power remains one.

Now here's the extraordinary twist.

At the *au*-stage, the outwardly developing sequence reaches its completion.

The powers have unfolded through their phonemic relations.

The trident has been formed.

And what happens next?

The entire manifesting movement is gathered back into Anuttara.

Everything returns.

And the resulting sign is tiny.

**अं - aṃ.**

A single point.

The whole cosmic drama has been reduced to a dot.

Abhinavagupta has constructed the entire metaphysical alphabet, and then apparently selected the world's smallest storage format.

But that dot is not a trivial punctuation mark.

It is **bindu**.

And understanding it requires us to distinguish the conventional Sanskrit sign from the theological symbol.


## 10. अं - Aṃ: The entire universe gathers into one point

# अं

aṃ - bindu, the point of reabsorbed conscious manifestation

Now Abhinavagupta describes the powers of will and knowledge, strengthened through their entry into Anuttara, as relinquishing the differentiated vibration associated with their limiting qualifications.

The result is a condition in which what remains is **a point of awareness**.

And the phonemic symbol is:

**अं - aṃ**.

This is not merely the next independent vowel following *au* in the ordinary phonological sense.

We're entering the system's treatment of the special phonemic signs associated with Bindu and Visarga.

### Bindu - बिन्दु

Point, drop, dot.

Now, ordinarily, the word *bindu* can refer to a physical point or drop.

In Sanskrit writing, a dot-like sign is associated with *anusvāra* and with certain nasal indications.

But in Tantric phonemic interpretation, Bindu acquires a much more extensive meaning.

It can represent concentrated conscious power.

The point where a differentiated manifestation is gathered into an undivided state.

And, in the present passage, the remaining self-awareness after the preceding powers relinquish their differentiated qualifications.

### The Sanskrit word avasāna

**अवसान - avasāna**

Ending, cessation, conclusion.

From:

**अव - ava**

Down, away.

And a formation related to √**sā / so**, to finish or bring to an end, in its established lexical history.

Now remember the root Tantra's phrase:

**बिन्द्ववसानगाः - bindvavasānagāḥ**

Reaching their termination at Bindu.

Let's break it down.

**बिन्दु - bindu**

Point.

**अवसान - avasāna**

Ending.

**ग - ga**

Going, reaching.

Thus:

**bindu + avasāna + ga**

Reaching the termination in Bindu.

And the masculine nominative plural:

**बिन्द्ववसानगाः - bindvavasānagāḥ**.

Why *bindv-*?

Because final *u* becomes *v* before the following vowel:

**u + a → va**.

So:

*bindu + avasāna → bindvavasāna.*

Now that's a beautiful sandhi point.

The word's actual grammar already embodies a transformation of the vowel.

And in the Tantric interpretation, the word describes the culmination of the phonemic powers in Bindu.

Again, the grammar and the symbolism are distinct.

But they fit together beautifully.

### The end is not annihilation

Here's the crucial philosophical distinction.

When the differentiated powers return to Bindu, their ultimate conscious nature hasn't been destroyed.

It's not a transition from existence into absolute nonexistence.

Rather, the differentiated qualifications no longer stand forth in the same way.

The conscious reality remains.

And that is why Bindu can represent a concentrated remainder of awareness.

### Vedanāmātra - वेदनामात्र

Now here's the central expression.

**वेदना - vedanā**

Awareness, experience, apprehension or feeling, depending on context.

Related to √**vid**, to know.

**मात्र - mātra**

Only, mere, nothing but.

Thus:

**वेदनामात्र - vedanāmātra**

Awareness alone.

In this passage, it does not mean merely an ordinary painful or pleasant sensation.

The term concerns the remaining awareness after differentiated manifestation has been gathered into non-difference.

Now Abhinavagupta makes the point particularly explicit.

Bindu is the condition in which **awareness alone remains**.

Not the independently existing pot.

Not the independently existing cognitive instrument.

Not a separate finite doer standing outside the field of knowing.

The differentiated distinctions have been reabsorbed into their conscious ground.

### Abheda - अभेद

Non-difference.

From:

**अ - a-**

Negative prefix.

**भेद - bheda**

Difference.

Thus:

**अभेद - abheda**

Non-difference.

And:

### Ārohaṇa - आरोहण

Ascending, rising, mounting.

From *ā-√ruh*, to ascend.

Now the passage describes the powers rising into non-difference.

This isn't simply a physical movement upward.

It is a transition in the mode of manifestation.

What was differentiated becomes gathered into its undivided conscious ground.

And the phonemic expression is Bindu.

### The very important distinction between Bindu and nasal sound

The written **ं** can represent nasalization or related nasal phenomena in actual Sanskrit orthography.

Its pronunciation depends upon context and traditional recitational conventions.

We should not claim that the mere physiological production of a nasal sound is identical to the metaphysical reabsorption of the universe.

The Tantric symbolism assigns a contemplative meaning to the dot.

That meaning does not replace conventional phonology.

And this is especially important because the same written sign can participate in different systems of notation and ritual interpretation.

### The cosmic zip file

Imagine taking an enormous archive.

Thousands of documents.

Millions of words.

An entire library.

Then compressing it into one file.

The file is much smaller.

But the information hasn't simply vanished.

Now, as an analogy, this is useful for imagining the gathering of a differentiated totality into a concentrated representation.

But Bindu isn't literally an information-compression algorithm.

Nor does the tradition establish a physically measurable storage capacity for a dot.

The philosophical claim is that **the differentiated powers remain grounded in undivided awareness even when their explicit distinctions cease**.

The point symbolizes that condition.

And now a very subtle distinction appears.

The Sanskrit speaks about the conscious subject remaining in a form that is not simply identical with the ordinary limited *puruṣa* of the tattva hierarchy.

We need to handle that carefully.


## 11. The conscious Puruṣa - Not merely the limited ego

The passage includes a dense expression involving:

**चिन्मयपुरुषतत्त्व - cinmayapuruṣatattva**

The principle of the conscious subject, consisting of consciousness.

Now the terminology is important because *puruṣa* can mean different things in different philosophical contexts.

### Puruṣa - पुरुष

Person, subject, spirit, experiencer.

In the thirty-six-tattva scheme, Puruṣa is a specific limited conscious principle.

But the word can also be used more broadly to discuss subjectivity.

And the present compound must be understood in its context of ascent into non-difference.

### Cinmaya - चिन्मय

Consisting of consciousness.

**चित् - cit**

Consciousness.

**मय - maya**

Made of, constituted by.

Thus:

**चिन्मय - cinmaya**

Consisting of consciousness.

Now this isn't a claim about a physical material called consciousness.

The suffix *-maya* expresses what something is constituted by or characterized through.

And:

### Tattva - तत्त्व

Principle, reality, thatness.

From:

**तत् - tat**

That.

And the abstract suffix:

**-त्व - -tva**.

Thus:

**तत्त्व - tattva**

The condition of being that, a principle of reality.

Now the philosophical point.

The finite subject, considered as a wholly separate, limited experiencer, belongs to differentiated manifestation.

But the conscious reality underlying subjectivity is not independently separate from universal awareness.

So when Abhinavagupta describes the powers returning to the condition of consciousness alone, we shouldn't imagine that he has simply recreated the ordinary limited ego in a tiny dot.

The point concerns **the conscious remainder after differentiated limitation has been relinquished**.

### The danger of confusing the ego with universal subjectivity

Imagine the finite individual saying:

"I am the consciousness in which objects appear."

That statement may express a contemplative insight.

But if the individual then concludes:

"Therefore every personal desire I have is a command of the Absolute, and I possess unrestricted divine agency,"

the philosophical distinction has collapsed.

The limited individual is not identical in its practical capacities with Bhairava's universal freedom.

The recognition concerns the ground of subjectivity.

Not the inflation of the psychological ego.

And the Bindu passage is precisely about moving beyond the apparent independent reality of differentiated qualifications.

Now here's the lovely consequence.

The *au*-stage expressed the complete expansion of the three powers.

The Bindu-stage represents their return to awareness alone.

But the divine manifestation isn't finished.

Because the same supreme consciousness can express the universe outwardly.

And that movement is represented by the next sign.

**अः - aḥ.**

Visarga.


## 12. अः - Aḥ: The universe is emitted again

# अः

aḥ - visarga, emission

Now the passage turns from the concentrated Bindu to **Visarga**.

The Sanskrit describes the Supreme Lord as gathering the world into himself and emitting it through his own freedom.

The operation has two related aspects.

One emphasizes the possessor of Śakti.

The other emphasizes Śakti's own power of emission.

And the phonemic symbol for the second is:

**अः - aḥ**.

### Visarga - विसर्ग

Emission, release, sending forth.

From:

**वि - vi**

Apart, outward, variously.

And √**सृज् - sṛj**

To release, emit, send forth, create.

The noun:

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission.

Now here's an important linguistic distinction.

In Sanskrit writing, **ः** is the *visarga* sign, commonly associated with a breathy postvocalic realization in recitation.

Its pronunciation depends on phonetic context and tradition.

But Abhinavagupta's *visarga* is also a technical metaphysical principle.

It represents the expressive movement through which the divine powers manifest the universe.

The ordinary linguistic sign and the Tantric doctrine are related through symbolic interpretation.

They are not identical explanations.

### Visṛjati - विसृजति

He releases, emits, sends forth.

From:

**वि - vi**

Apart or forth.

**√सृज् - sṛj**

To emit, create or release.

So:

**विसृजति - visṛjati**

Third-person singular present.

Now the commentary uses this verb to describe the Supreme Lord's emission of the universe.

Notice the relationship:

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission.

**विसृजति - visṛjati**

Emits.

Same broad verbal family.

And that is a genuine lexical relationship.

Unlike certain esoteric phonemic associations, this connection is ordinary Sanskrit morphology.

### Śaktimat - शक्तिमत्

Possessing power.

We've learned this before.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

The possessive adjective:

**शक्तिमत् - śaktimat**

Possessing Śakti.

Now the passage distinguishes:

**शक्तिमद्रूपप्रधानता - śaktimadrūpapradhānatā**

Predominance of the form of the possessor of Śakti.

And:

**शाक्तविसर्गप्रधानता - śāktavisargapradhānatā**

Predominance of Śākta emission.

These are dense compounds.

Let's dissect them.

### Pradhāna - प्रधान

Principal, predominant, chief.

And:

**प्रधानता - pradhānatā**

Predominance, being principal.

So:

**śaktimad-rūpa-pradhānatā**

The predominance of the Śakti-possessor aspect.

Now:

**शाक्त - śākta**

Pertaining to Śakti.

And:

**विसर्ग - visarga**

Emission.

Thus:

**śākta-visarga-pradhānatā**

The predominance of the Śākta emissive aspect.

Now the philosophical distinction is extremely subtle.

In Bindu, the emphasis is upon the gathered conscious condition, the possessor of power in his fullness.

In Visarga, the emphasis shifts toward the expressive Śakti through which manifestation is emitted.

But these are not two independent absolutes.

The same supreme reality is considered through two different modes.

### Bindu and Visarga are not simple opposites

Don't think:

Bindu = good, pure, divine.

Visarga = bad, impure, fallen.

That would be completely wrong.

Both are manifestations of divine freedom.

Bindu represents a concentrated condition of awareness.

Visarga represents the expressive release of the powers.

The same supreme consciousness is involved in both.

And the movement from one to the other isn't an externally imposed mechanical process.

It is the self-expression of Śakti.

### The two dots of Visarga

The written form **ः** consists of two vertically arranged dots in standard Devanāgarī.

It's tempting to interpret the two dots immediately as Śiva and Śakti.

Some Tantric traditions do elaborate symbolic accounts of the two-dot form.

But we must not invent a specific interpretation and attribute it to this passage merely because the graphic shape suggests one.

The secure point here is that Abhinavagupta uses **Visarga** to express the Śākta mode of emission.

That is explicitly grounded in the Sanskrit.

The rest requires its own textual evidence.

And here's the philosophical beauty.

The universe is not a foreign substance emitted from a divine machine.

The emission belongs to the same consciousness in which the differentiated manifestation is ultimately reabsorbed.

Thus the return to awareness and the expression of the world are two aspects of one divine freedom.

And now we're ready to understand why *au* was called the trident.

Because all three powers are gathered there.

And their manifestation has a beginning, middle and end.


## 13. The trident is not a weapon here - It is the structure of conscious freedom

Let's return to:

**त्रिशूल - triśūla**

Trident.

Now the text explicitly associates the *au*-stage with the trident because it includes:

**इच्छा - icchā**

Will.

**ज्ञान - jñāna**

Knowledge.

**क्रिया - kriyā**

Action.

And the movement of *kriyāśakti* reaches completion there.

The primary prose makes the connection directly.

Abhinavagupta then invokes a *Ṣaḍardha* scriptural reference concerning the trident.

But the transmitted citation is incomplete.

The Kashmir Series preserves only the ending of the verse, including:

**त्रिशूलेन चतुर्थकम्**

*Triśūlena caturthakam.*

Its preceding words are missing in the consulted witness, and the GRETIL transcription likewise indicates a lacuna.

So we cannot responsibly reconstruct the entire verse.

And we shouldn't present the surviving phrase as though we possessed a complete, securely transmitted stanza. ; \]

### Tri - त्रि

Three.

### Śūla - शूल

Point, spear, trident prong.

Now:

**त्रिशूल - triśūla**

The three-pointed weapon.

But in this context, the symbol is specifically the triad of divine powers.

Think about the structure.

Will.

Knowledge.

Action.

Three distinguishable functions.

One supreme freedom.

So the trident represents the gathering of those three powers into one conscious reality.

### Why *au* is the culmination

We've followed the sequence:

The will-powers enter the supreme ground.

The knowledge-powers enter the supreme ground.

Their intermingling becomes more explicit.

And the power of action reaches its complete expression at *au*.

Thus the three powers are present.

And the phoneme is associated with the trident.

Not simply because the written character looks like an exotic weapon.

But because the *theological structure* is triadic.

### The trident and the fourth

Now here's a subtle point.

The surviving fragment includes **caturthakam**, the fourth.

That naturally raises the question of what the fourth member represents in relation to the trident.

But because the preceding portion of the verse is missing, we cannot establish the full grammatical and doctrinal context from the fragment alone.

Abhinavagupta's surrounding prose identifies the *au*-form with the triadic powers, and immediately proceeds to Bindu.

That supports the broad relation between the trident and the further concentrated conscious state.

But it doesn't authorize inventing the exact missing sequence of the original verse.

This is one of those places where philological restraint is essential.

We know the trident connection.

We know the following discussion of Bindu.

We do not possess the complete quotation.

And that's fine.

The missing manuscript words do not become magically available because the symbolism is attractive.

### The trident without three independent gods

Now imagine someone saying:

"Will is one deity. Knowledge is another. Action is a third. Therefore the universe has three independent supreme principles."

But that is precisely what Abhinavagupta rejects.

The three powers are distinguishable expressions of one *svātantrya*.

Their differences belong to manifestation.

The ground remains Bhairava.

Thus the trident represents **unity through distinguishable powers**, not three rival absolutes.

And this is what makes the symbol philosophically powerful.

The three points are distinguishable.

But they belong to one structure.

A physical trident is merely an analogy.

The doctrinal point concerns the nondual relationship among the powers.

### The editorial expansion

Immediately after the incomplete *Ṣaḍardha* citation, the Kashmir Series contains a bracketed verse interpreting Bindu as a Śiva-Bindu, a point of pure manifestation.

That verse supplies a helpful explanatory bridge.

But it belongs to the edition's bracketed material, not necessarily to Abhinavagupta's unbroken main prose.

The main text itself continues by identifying Bindu with the remaining awareness and then explaining Visarga.

That is the secure argumentative sequence.

And it completes the major phonemic movement.

From the compound vowels.

Through the trident.

Into Bindu.

And outward through Visarga.

Now the commentary turns to the **three stages of the same divine freedom**.

And this is the perfect philosophical synthesis of everything we've studied.


## 14. Three phases of one power - The desire to manifest, manifestation, and completion

Now we move into Kashmir Series p. 177.

The commentary describes the supreme freedom as expressing three phases.

Not three independent substances.

Three distinguishable aspects of one power.

The Sanskrit uses three particularly beautiful words:

**उल्लिलसिषा - ullilasiṣā**

**उल्लसत्ता - ullasattā**

**उल्लसितता - ullasitatā**

Let's learn them carefully.

### Ullilasiṣā - उल्लिलसिषा

The desire or impulse to shine forth.

Related to the root √**las**, to shine, play or manifest, through a desiderative formation.

The key is the desiderative sense.

The power is oriented toward manifestation.

It is not yet considered through the same explicitly unfolded form as the later stages.

And so it corresponds naturally to **icchāśakti**, the power of will.

Now, the word has a beautifully repetitive sound.

*Ullilasiṣā.*

It almost performs the idea of something preparing to burst forth.

### Ullasattā - उल्लसत्ता

The condition of shining forth or becoming manifest.

Related to the participial form:

**उल्लसत् - ullasat**

Shining forth, manifesting.

And the abstract suffix:

**-ता - -tā**

Thus:

**उल्लसत्ता - ullasattā**

The condition of active manifestation.

This corresponds naturally to the middle phase in which the manifestation becomes explicit.

### Ullasitatā - उल्लसितता

The condition of having shone forth or become manifest.

From:

**उल्लसित - ullasita**

Manifested, expanded, unfolded.

With:

**-ता - -tā**.

Thus:

**उल्लसितता - ullasitatā**

The condition of completed manifestation.

Now we have a threefold progression.

The impulse to manifest.

The unfolding manifestation.

The manifested condition.

And these are interpreted through:

Will.

Knowledge.

Action.

### A crucial qualification

We shouldn't imagine these as three separate temporal events through which the Absolute is forced to pass.

Remember Abhinavagupta's earlier warning.

The verbal exposition is sequential.

The supreme conscious ground is not bound by that sequence.

So the three phases describe **the intelligible articulation of divine freedom**.

They don't establish three independent beings.

### The cosmic filmmaking analogy

Imagine making a film.

First, the possibility of the film is entertained.

Then its structure becomes clearer.

The scenes, their relationships and their visual forms become intelligible.

Finally, the film is produced.

You can distinguish intention, knowledge and production.

But they're not three completely unrelated activities.

They belong to one expressive process.

Now apply that analogy cautiously to the divine powers.

The Absolute isn't a finite film director with a deadline and a production budget.

But the relation between intention, intelligibility and execution provides a useful way to understand the threefold manifestation.

And Abhinavagupta explicitly connects the phases with the sovereignty of Bhairava.

### The subtlety of the phases

At the level of the finite individual, intention can fail.

Knowledge can be mistaken.

Action can be obstructed.

But the supreme *svātantrya* isn't described as a finite psychological process subject to independent external obstacles.

The threefold relation belongs to the divine freedom itself.

So when the commentary uses these stages, it is not simply projecting a human decision-making model onto a cosmic person.

It is interpreting the structure of manifestation through the powers of consciousness.

Now, at the end of this passage, Abhinavagupta compares the scheme with another Śaiva scripture.

The *Svacchanda* tradition.

And here the threefold classification acquires different names.


## 15. Prabuddha, Prasaraṇa, Āvaraṇa - The Svacchanda comparison

Abhinavagupta now says that the relevant phases are described in procedural scriptures such as the *Svacchandatantra* through terms involving:

**प्रबुद्ध - prabuddha**

Awakened, brought forth, become manifest.

**प्रसरण - prasaraṇa**

Expansion, extension, spreading forth.

**आवरण - āvaraṇa**

Covering, enclosure, veiling.

Now this is a fascinating triad.

But we have to be careful about the exact mapping.

The Kashmir Series includes a bracketed explanation assigning the terms respectively to will, knowledge and action.

That explanation is useful, but the direct unbracketed prose attributes the threefold terminology to Śaiva procedural scriptures without pausing to unpack each term in the same manner.

So let's distinguish what the passage securely does.

It identifies the preceding phases of divine manifestation with classifications known from other Śaiva texts.

And it treats them as descriptions of the same underlying powers.

### Prabuddha - प्रबुद्ध

From:

**प्र - pra**

Forth.

**√बुध् - budh**

To awaken or understand.

Past participle:

**बुद्ध - buddha**

Awakened.

With the prefix:

**प्रबुद्ध - prabuddha**

Fully awakened or brought into explicit awareness.

Now, this term doesn't automatically refer to the historical Buddha.

It's an ordinary Sanskrit adjective with a broad semantic range.

And the Tantric context gives it a specific role.

### Prasaraṇa - प्रसरण

Expansion, spreading forth.

Related to √**सृ**, to move or flow.

We've encountered:

**प्रसर - prasara**

Expansion.

**प्रसरन्ती - prasarantī**

Expanding.

Now:

**प्रसरण - prasaraṇa**

The action or process of expanding.

All members of the same verbal family.

### Āvaraṇa - आवरण

Covering, veil, enclosure.

From:

**आ - ā**

Toward or around.

**√वृ - vṛ**

To cover, enclose or obstruct.

Thus:

**आवरण - āvaraṇa**

Covering.

Now here's something important.

In Indian philosophical and religious texts, *āvaraṇa* can mean a veil concealing reality.

But in a procedural classification, covering or enclosure may also function as a positive structural operation.

The word doesn't automatically mean evil ignorance in every context.

Its role depends upon the system.

And in the present comparison, it belongs to the description of divine manifestation.

### The philosophical significance

Why can the same powers receive different names?

Because the names emphasize different aspects of the operation.

Will.

Expansion.

Enclosure.

Awakening.

Manifestation.

These are not always interchangeable concepts.

But they can participate in a common theological explanation.

Now we've come full circle.

Remember the great Mālinī-Mātṛkā problem?

Different scriptures classify and arrange the powers differently.

Abhinavagupta's solution was not that every difference is meaningless.

It was that the differences can be intelligible as distinct expressions of one universal freedom.

And now the same principle applies to the descriptions of *icchā*, *jñāna* and *kriyā*.

The *Svacchanda* terminology is another mode of articulation.

The divine freedom remains one.

And now, finally, Abhinavagupta returns to one of the earliest masters of the Pratyabhijñā tradition.

**Somānanda.**


## 16. Somānanda returns - The three powers are contained in supreme bliss

Abhinavagupta cites the seventh *āhnika* of the Śivadṛṣṭi.

This is enormously important historically and philosophically.

Because Somānanda is one of the foundational thinkers of the Pratyabhijñā tradition.

And Abhinavagupta is not inventing his doctrine from nothing.

He's developing a teaching inherited through a lineage of philosophical masters.

The cited passage describes the supreme Self as filled with extraordinary bliss and containing the three powers in a condition of inward absorption.

One securely transmitted expression is:

**निलीनशक्तित्रितये**

*Nilīnaśaktitritaye.*

In whom the triad of powers has become absorbed.

Let's learn it.

### Nilīna - निलीन

Absorbed, merged, hidden within.

From:

**नि - ni**

Down, into.

And √**ली - lī**

To cling, dissolve, become absorbed.

Past participial form:

**लीन - līna**

Merged or absorbed.

With the prefix:

**निलीन - nilīna**.

Now:

### Śaktitritaya - शक्तित्रितय

The triad of powers.

**शक्ति - śakti**

Power.

**त्रितय - tritaya**

A group of three.

Thus:

**śaktitritaya**

The threefold power.

And the locative singular:

**निलीनशक्तित्रितये - nilīnaśaktitritaye**

In whom the three powers are absorbed.

Now, the philosophical meaning.

Will, knowledge and action are not absent from the supreme reality.

They're present in an undifferentiated unity.

Their distinct functions may not yet be outwardly articulated.

But their underlying power isn't destroyed.

That's precisely what Abhinavagupta has been arguing throughout the vowel sequence.

The powers are intrinsically included within consciousness.

And their explicit distinctions are modes of manifestation.

### The extraordinary bliss

The Śivadṛṣṭi citation also uses expressions involving:

**आह्लाद - āhlāda**

Delight, joy, bliss.

And:

**भरित - bharita**

Filled, full.

Related to √**bhṛ**, to bear or fill.

The supreme Self is described through overflowing delight and completeness.

Now, remember the crucial distinction from earlier.

This is not ordinary psychological pleasure caused by acquiring an object.

It's the self-repose and fullness of consciousness.

And the three powers are present within it.

### The pot returns

The subsequent Śivadṛṣṭi excerpt compares the manifestation of the universe from Śakti to a pot formed from a lump of clay.

Now that's wonderfully appropriate.

We began Part XIII with a pot.

And here the pot has returned.

This time as an analogy for the relationship between underlying reality and manifested form.

A lump of clay becomes a pot.

The form changes.

But the pot is not an independent substance unrelated to its material basis.

Similarly, Śakti manifests the universe without becoming fundamentally other than consciousness.

The analogy is not exact.

Clay is a physical substance.

Consciousness, in Trika, is the ultimate self-revealing ground.

Nevertheless, the relation of dependent manifestation to underlying reality is intelligible.

### A textual lacuna

The Kashmir Series and GRETIL witnesses preserve an incomplete portion of the clay-and-pot stanza.

So I won't invent its missing words.

The surviving part establishes the comparison.

And the next complete statement supplies the philosophical conclusion.

**One principle alone is ultimate; numerical multiplicity does not create additional independent ultimate reality.**

This is perhaps the most important point of the whole installment.

Because we've now studied:

Six original vowel-powers.

Four nectar-seeds.

Four compound vowels.

Bindu.

Visarga.

Three divine powers.

And multiple modes of their manifestation.

One could mistakenly conclude that reality has become an enormous collection of independent entities.

Somānanda's statement reminds us that the entire plurality remains grounded in one principle.

And now the Sanskrit says so with extraordinary directness.


## 17. एकमेव हि तत्तत्त्वम् - There is only one reality

The quotation concludes with the assertion:

**एकमेव हि तत्तत्त्वं न सङ्ख्यातोऽतिरिक्तता।**

*Ekam eva hi tat tattvaṃ na saṅkhyāto 'tiriktatā.*

There is indeed only that one principle; numerical multiplicity does not establish something beyond it.

Now this is an absolutely beautiful sentence.

And it's the natural philosophical conclusion of the entire phonemic argument.

Let's learn the grammar.

### Ekam - एकम्

One.

Neuter nominative or accusative singular.

Here agreeing with:

**तत्त्वम् - tattvam**.

### Eva - एव

Indeed, precisely, just.

### Hi - हि

Indeed, for, because.

A particle emphasizing the statement.

### Tat - तत्

That.

The demonstrative pronoun.

Thus:

**एकमेव हि तत्तत्त्वम्**

That principle is indeed one alone.

Now:

### Saṅkhyā - सङ्ख्या

Number, counting, enumeration.

Related to:

**सम् - sam**

Together.

And √**ख्या - khyā**

To count, declare, make known.

The ablative singular:

**सङ्ख्यातः - saṅkhyātaḥ**

From number, by reason of number.

In the transmitted continuous phrase:

**सङ्ख्यातोऽतिरिक्तता**

*Saṅkhyāto 'tiriktatā.*

The final sound undergoes visarga sandhi before the following vowel.

And:

### Atiriktatā - अतिरिक्तता

The condition of being additional or separate beyond.

From:

**अति - ati**

Beyond, excessive.

**रिक्त - rikta**

Empty, left over, separated or additional in relevant compounds.

And:

**-ता - -tā**

Abstract state.

Thus:

**अतिरिक्तता - atiriktatā**

Additionality, the condition of being something beyond.

Now the point.

You can count different manifestations.

One.

Two.

Three.

Fifty phonemes.

Thirty-six tattvas.

An extraordinary number of divine powers.

But the numerical distinction does not establish fifty independently ultimate consciousnesses.

The plurality belongs to manifestation.

Its ground remains one.

### The philosophical meaning

Now imagine somebody saying:

"If there are three Śaktis, there must be three ultimate substances."

But that doesn't follow.

Three distinguishable operations don't necessarily establish three independent ontological grounds.

Likewise, fifty phonemic powers can participate in one conscious reality.

The numbers classify the manifestation.

They don't manufacture separate absolutes.

That's the heart of the argument.

And it connects with something we've been studying since Part VII.

**Sarvasarvātmakatva.**

Everything's reality is ultimately implicated in the totality.

The differentiated principles are meaningful.

But not independently self-sufficient.

Now the consequence is enormous.

The entire Sanskrit alphabet can be interpreted as an expression of Śiva's freedom.

But none of its individual letters can become an external authority standing above that freedom.

The alphabet is a sacred representation of manifestation.

Not a cage around the Absolute.

And this is why Abhinavagupta can combine ordinary vowel sandhi with Tantric interpretation without reducing the divine reality to a mechanical sound transformation.

The sounds are real as linguistic phenomena.

The interpretations are meaningful within the tradition.

But the supreme ground is not identical with the sequence of linguistic rules.

**The grammar explains the sounds; the Tantra interprets them as expressions of consciousness.**

And the whole system remains grounded in one self-revealing reality.

## 19. Ordinary grammar versus Tantric *nirvacana* - The distinction that makes the whole text intelligible

We've now seen three related but different forms of analysis.

| Level | Example | What it establishes |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **Conventional phonology** | *a + i → e* | A rule of Sanskrit sound combination |
| **Traditional phonemic symbolism** | *e* as the entry of will into the supreme ground | A Tantric correspondence between sound and divine power |
| **Trika metaphysics** | Will, knowledge and action are expressions of one consciousness | A philosophical thesis about the nature of manifestation |

Now the essential point.

You can accept that the grammatical rule is correct without thereby accepting the metaphysical interpretation.

Likewise, you can understand Abhinavagupta's doctrine of consciousness without pretending the Sanskrit language literally causes the physical universe to exist through vowel transformations.

The levels are related within the tradition.

But they aren't interchangeable.

### Nirvacana - निर्वचन

Interpretive derivation, explanation or etymological interpretation.

From:

**निर् - nir**

Out, forth, fully.

And √**वच् - vac**

To speak.

The noun:

**निर्वचन - nirvacana**

Explanation or interpretive derivation.

In traditional Indian scholarship, *nirvacana* can be a disciplined way of uncovering symbolic meanings through word relationships.

But a Tantric *nirvacana* is not automatically the same as a historical linguistic derivation.

For example:

The association of *au* with the three Śaktis is a theological interpretation.

The ordinary grammatical relationship:

**a + o → au**

Is a phonological rule.

We should understand both.

And distinguish them.

### One more grammatical precision

In the preceding verse from the Śivadṛṣṭi, the form **सङ्ख्यातः - saṅkhyātaḥ** should not be treated as the ordinary ablative singular of the feminine noun *saṅkhyā*, which would be *saṅkhyāyāḥ*. Here the expression works in the sense "from the standpoint of number" or "on account of enumeration." The exact grammatical analysis requires recognizing the adverbial force of the *-tas* ending rather than mechanically applying the regular feminine declension.

That is precisely the level of care these texts reward.

A beautiful philosophical interpretation can still depend on a very small grammatical distinction.

And a small grammatical mistake can distort the argument.

### Four writing exercises

**1. Guṇa sandhi**

## अ + इ → ए

a + i → e

The first major compound vowel.


**2. Vṛddhi sandhi**

## अ + ए → ऐ

a + e → ai

A further vowel combination.


**3. The solar counterpart**

## अ + उ → ओ → औ

a + u → o; a + o → au

Two separate sandhi operations, not one automatic three-vowel transformation.


**4. The concentrated and emissive forms**

## अं - अः

aṃ - aḥ

Bindu and Visarga in the sacred phonemic interpretation.


## 21. The final insight - The alphabet is the movement of consciousness

Now gather the whole sequence.

The earlier vowel-powers represented the supreme reality through Anuttara, Ānanda, Icchā, Īśana and the opening of knowledge.

The unusual vocalic vowels represented further differentiated relationships of action, including entry into more subtle and void-like conditions.

Then the compound vowels expressed the interpenetration of those earlier powers.

**ए - e**

A combined expression of will entering the supreme ground.

**ऐ - ai**

Further expansion of that combined power.

**ओ - o**

The corresponding manifestation involving knowledge.

**औ - au**

The culmination of *kriyāśakti*, symbolically containing the threefold power of will, knowledge and action.

Then:

**अं - aṃ**

Bindu, the gathering of differentiated manifestation into awareness alone.

And:

**अः - aḥ**

Visarga, the emission of the universe through Śakti.

Now the entire structure becomes intelligible.

The sacred alphabet is a symbolic exposition of the relation between the supreme conscious ground and its expressive power.

The sounds are distinguishable.

Their combinations are meaningful.

Their traditional ritual applications can differ.

But the supreme consciousness is not independently manufactured by those distinctions.

And the culmination is the triad.

Will.

Knowledge.

Action.

All three.

One freedom.

Abhinavagupta's philosophical thesis is not that the universe literally obeys Sanskrit spelling rules.

It's that the power through which linguistic differentiation becomes manifest is not ultimately separate from the consciousness through which all other differentiation is possible.

And this is why he can interpret ordinary vowel transformations through the structure of Śakti.

The grammar supplies the relationships.

The Tantra supplies the symbolism.

The Pratyabhijñā philosophy supplies the metaphysical ground.

And the result is an extraordinary account of nondual manifestation.

**Śiva does not become free by performing the universe. The universe manifests through the freedom that is already Śiva's nature.**

That is the heart of Part XIV.

And now, after all the complicated vowel combinations, the trident and the cosmic emission, Abhinavagupta has brought us back to one reality.

The alphabet has expanded.

The universe has appeared.

And the Heart remains undivided.
