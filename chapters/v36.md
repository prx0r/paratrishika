# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa - Part XXXVI

The Grand Finale: Abhinavagupta, the Trident of Knowledge, and the Goddesses Who Have the Last Word

## “The Greatest Philosopher in Kashmir Has Had Enough of Fraudulent Gurus. Unfortunately, He Has Also Been One of Their Students.”

We are finally here.

Thirty-five instalments. Hundreds of Sanskrit passages. An alphabet that turned into a universe. A universe that turned out to be the self-manifestation of consciousness. A supreme Heart that was apparently present before we began searching for it.

And now Abhinavagupta is going to do something even more extraordinary than explaining the metaphysics of Śiva.

**He's going to explain himself.**

Not through another anonymous scriptural quotation, but through his own concluding verses.

He will tell us where his ancestors came from, how his father understood the world, why he calls himself Abhinavagupta, whom he hopes to reach, and what he thinks of people who claim to be spiritual teachers without understanding what they're teaching.

Spoiler: he is not impressed.

Then comes a confession. He himself has been misled by such teachers.

Then a revelation concerning the grace of Śiva and the teaching of Somānanda.

And finally, after an enormous composition about divine language, Abhinavagupta asks the goddesses to forgive the **restlessness of his speech and mind**.

The man has spent hundreds of pages explaining why consciousness is the ultimate reality.

And he ends by apologising for talking so much.

Honestly, that's a rather perfect ending.

### The textual boundary

We resume at authorial epilogue verse 11, following the ten verses studied in Part XXXV. The complete epilogue has **21 numbered verses**, preserved in both the 1918 Kashmir Series of Texts and Studies XVIII edition and Somadeva Vasudeva's GRETIL transcription, which carries Gnoli's 1985 pagination.

The eleven verses here run through approximately **KSTS printed pp. 279-282**, corresponding to **Gnoli pp. 284-286**. After verse 21, the texts give a completion statement, an additional unnumbered summarising stanza, and colophon material. The 1918 printed edition then continues with verses belonging to its modern publication history-not Abhinavagupta's composition. ; \]

I am using GRETIL as the running romanised witness and the digitised KSTS edition as the Devanāgarī witness. Where they disagree, I'll identify the difference, especially when an apparent error is probably electronic rather than a securely attested manuscript variant. The original photographic scan could not be retrieved for direct page-image collation.

And now, after all the metaphysics, let's meet the family.


## I. Verse 11 - Atrigupta Arrives in Kashmir, and the Family History Begins

KSTS p. 279-280 · Gnoli p. 284 · Śālinī metre

**Devanāgarī**

> अन्तर्वेद्यामत्रिगुप्ताभिधानः
> प्राप्योत्पत्तिं प्राविशत्प्राग्र्यजन्मा ।
> श्रीकाश्मीरांश्चन्द्रचूडावतारै-
> र्निःसंख्याकैः पावितोपान्तभागान् ॥११॥

**IAST**

> *antarvedyām atriguptābhidhānaḥ*
> *prāpyotpattiṃ prāviśat prāgryajanmā &#124;*
> *śrīkāśmīrāṃś candracūḍāvatārair*
> *niḥsaṃkhyākaiḥ pāvitopāntabhāgān &#124;&#124;*

**Literal translation:**

“A man of eminent birth named Atrigupta, having been born in Antarvedī, entered glorious Kashmir, whose surrounding regions had been sanctified by innumerable manifestations of the Crescent-crested Lord.”

This is our first explicit ancestral statement.

**Atrigupta.**

A Brahmin associated with *Antarvedī*, who came to Kashmir and became the forebear of the lineage from which Abhinavagupta descended.

Now something important.

The verse does **not** say that Atrigupta was Abhinavagupta's father.

Nor his grandfather.

Nor does it provide a complete sequence of generations between them.

He is an earlier ancestor.

The next verse explicitly speaks of his great lineage, and only then introduces Varāhagupta and Cukhala.

So we must not compress three centuries of family history into a convenient three-generation family tree.

### Antarvedī - अन्तर्वेदी

A geographical designation conventionally associated with the region between the Gaṅgā and Yamunā rivers in northern India.

The term is particularly important to the family's claimed origins.

The locative singular **antarvedyām** means “in Antarvedī.”

It establishes the place of Atrigupta's birth.

The 1918 editor's historical introduction connects this Atrigupta with the reign of Kashmir's king Lalitāditya and the tradition that the king invited the learned Brahmin to Kashmir. That additional account comes from the historical and editorial tradition, not from the four pādas of this verse alone.

The distinction matters.

The verse directly attests Atrigupta's birth in Antarvedī and entry into Kashmir.

The royal invitation requires the supporting historical tradition.

### Atriguptābhidhānaḥ

**अत्रिगुप्ताभिधानः**

*atri-gupta-abhidhānaḥ.*

**Atri** - a personal name, also famous as the name of a Vedic sage.

**Gupta** - a proper-name element; its ordinary meaning relates to protection or concealment.

**abhidhāna** - name, designation, appellation.

Thus:

“Bearing the name Atrigupta.”

The compound does not establish that the historical person was literally the ancient ṛṣi Atri.

Atri is part of his name.

That distinction is particularly worth preserving in traditional genealogies, where mythic and historical names can easily become conflated.

### Prāpyotpattim

**प्राप्योत्पत्तिम् - prāpyotpattim**

This is:

*prāpya + utpattim.*

**prāpya** - having obtained, an absolutive from *pra-* \+ √*āp*, to obtain.

**utpatti** - arising, birth, origin.

The accusative singular is **utpattim**.

The vowel sandhi is:

*a + u → o.*

Hence *prāpyotpattim*.

The sense is “having obtained birth.”

### Prāviśat

**प्राविशत् - prāviśat**

“He entered.”

From *pra-* \+ √*viś*, to enter, with a past-tense verbal form.

The GRETIL and KSTS electronic witnesses diverge around this word, with the KSTS transcription losing part of the prefixed form. The intended reading *prāviśat* is supported by GRETIL and by the grammatical construction of entering Kashmir. This should be treated as a probable transcription defect, not a meaningful doctrinal variant. ; \]

### Prāgryajanmā

**प्राग्र्यजन्मा - prāgryajanmā**

Of eminent or excellent birth.

**prāgrya** - foremost, eminent, distinguished.

**janman** - birth.

The compound describes Atrigupta's high social standing within the Brahmanical world of the passage.

It is praise of lineage, not an independent demonstration of spiritual attainment.

And that matters because Abhinavagupta's earlier teaching explicitly distinguished the supreme recognition from the social qualifications associated with birth.

The epilogue acknowledges the family's inherited prestige.

The metaphysics does not therefore make caste the producer of consciousness.

### Śrīkāśmīrān

**श्रीकाश्मीरान् - śrīkāśmīrān**

Glorious Kashmir.

The plural form is conventional for the geographical designation.

It is the accusative object of *prāviśat*, entered.

The verse celebrates Kashmir as a sacred and prestigious region.

And now it invokes the crescent-crowned Lord.

### Candracūḍāvatārair

**चन्द्रचूडावतारैः - candracūḍāvatāraiḥ**

“By manifestations or descents of the Crescent-crested Lord.”

**candra** - Moon.

**cūḍa** - crest.

**avatāra** - descent, manifestation.

The instrumental plural ending **-aiḥ** specifies the agency through which Kashmir's regions are described as sanctified.

The Lord is Śiva, identified by the crescent Moon on his crest.

The claim is religious and laudatory.

It tells us how the poet understands the sacred status of Kashmir.

It does not independently establish a countable series of historical divine incarnations.

### Niḥsaṃkhyākaiḥ

**निःसंख्याकैः - niḥsaṃkhyākaiḥ**

Innumerable.

**niḥ-** - without.

**saṃkhyā** - counting, enumeration, number.

The instrumental plural agrees with the divine manifestations.

The sense is that the number cannot be enumerated.

### Pāvitopāntabhāgān

**पावितोपान्तभागान् - pāvitopāntabhāgān**

“Whose surrounding regions have been purified.”

**pāvita** - purified, sanctified.

**upānta** - surrounding region, vicinity, borderland.

**bhāga** - portion, part, region.

The accusative masculine plural agrees with Kashmir.

And so the verse gives a compact picture:

A learned Brahmin of eminent birth.

Born elsewhere.

Entering Kashmir.

Kashmir understood as sacred through Śiva's innumerable manifestations.

The family's Kashmiri history begins with a movement from one region into another.

### The metre: Śālinī

This is **Śālinī**, a four-pāda metre of eleven syllables per pāda.

Its characteristic pattern is:

**- - - - - ⏑ - - ⏑ - -**

For the first pāda:

*an-tar-ve-dyām-a-tri-gup-tā-bhi-dhā-naḥ*

The *a* before the conjunct *tr* counts heavy by position, so the fifth syllable is not light merely because its vowel is short.

The following *tri* is light.

That gives the expected sequence.

The second pāda also fits this pattern:

*prāp-yot-pat-tiṃ-prā-vi-śat-prā-grya-jan-mā.*

Again, the fifth syllable is long.

The sixth is short.

And the ninth is short.

The metrical pattern supports the transmitted construction.

We have gone from the Vasantatilakā, Śārdūlavikrīḍita, and Āryā of the first ten verses to a stately eleven-syllable genealogical stanza.

Abhinavagupta is changing metres as he changes subjects.

Not because he has lost control of the composition.

Because he's an exceptionally accomplished Sanskrit poet.

### The historical conclusion

What this verse securely establishes is simple but important.

**Atrigupta came from Antarvedī into Kashmir and is presented as an ancestor of Abhinavagupta's family.**

The particular connection with Lalitāditya belongs to the wider historical tradition and must be evaluated separately.

And now the next verse supplies the crucial intervening names.


## II. Verse 12 - Varāhagupta, Cukhala, and the Father Who Had Apparently Had Enough of Saṃsāra

KSTS p. 280 · Gnoli p. 285 · Upajāti metre

**Devanāgarī**

> तस्यान्ववाये महति प्रसूताद्
> वराहगुप्तात्प्रतिलब्धजन्मा ।
> संसारवृत्तान्तपराङ्मुखो यः
> शिवैकचित्तश्चुखलाभिधानः ॥१२॥

**IAST**

> *tasyānvavāye mahati prasūtād*
> *varāhaguptāt pratilabdhajanmā &#124;*
> *saṃsāravṛttāntaparāṅmukho yaḥ*
> *śivaikacittaś cukhalābhidhānaḥ &#124;&#124;*

**Literal translation:**

“Born from Varāhagupta, who had arisen in that distinguished lineage, was the man called Cukhala, who had turned away from the affairs of worldly existence and whose mind was exclusively fixed on Śiva.”

Now we have the genealogy.

Atrigupta.

His distinguished line.

Varāhagupta.

And Cukhala.

The next verse will identify Abhinavagupta as born from Cukhala.

This gives us a much more precise family structure than the usual vague statement that Abhinavagupta came from a family of Kashmiri Brahmins.

### Tasya anvavāye

**तस्यान्ववाये - tasyānvavāye**

“In his lineage.”

**tasya** - of him, genitive singular.

The referent is Atrigupta.

**anvavāya** - lineage, descent, family succession.

The locative singular is **anvavāye**.

And here is the crucial point.

This does not mean Varāhagupta was Atrigupta's immediate son.

It means Varāhagupta belonged to his lineage.

The generations between are not enumerated.

That is why it would be historically irresponsible to draw a direct father-to-son arrow from Atrigupta to Varāhagupta without qualification.

### Mahati

**महति - mahati**

Locative singular of *mahat*, great.

It agrees with *anvavāye*.

The great or distinguished lineage.

This is social and genealogical praise.

Not evidence that every ancestor was a historically documented philosopher.

### Prasūtāt

**प्रसूतात् - prasūtāt**

From one born or arisen.

The ablative singular of the participle *prasūta*, connected with *pra-* \+ √*sū*, to generate or give birth.

The form qualifies Varāhagupta.

It describes him as arising within the lineage.

### Varāhaguptāt

**वराहगुप्तात् - varāhaguptāt**

From Varāhagupta.

The ablative singular identifies the immediate source of Cukhala's birth.

Thus **Varāhagupta is Cukhala's father** according to the grammatical construction of the verse.

This is different from the more distant ancestral relationship with Atrigupta.

### Pratilabdhajanmā

**प्रतिलब्धजन्मा - pratilabdhajanmā**

One who has obtained birth.

**prati-** - toward, in return, or an intensifying prefix in the verbal formation.

**labdha** - obtained, past passive participle of √*labh*.

**janman** - birth.

The compound is masculine nominative singular.

It describes Cukhala.

So the structure is:

“Born from Varāhagupta… the one named Cukhala.”

The relative pronoun **yaḥ** binds the description together.

### Saṃsāravṛttānta

**संसारवृत्तान्त - saṃsāravṛttānta**

The affairs, events, or course of worldly existence.

**saṃsāra** - conditioned existence, wandering, worldly becoming.

**vṛttānta** - occurrence, account, circumstance, course of events.

The compound evokes the ongoing affairs of saṃsāric life.

And Cukhala is described as **parāṅmukha**, turned away from them.

### Parāṅmukhaḥ

**पराङ्मुखः - parāṅmukhaḥ**

Turned away, averse, facing elsewhere.

From *parāñc / parāṅ-* in the semantic field of outward or away-directed movement, and *mukha*, face.

The word does not necessarily establish that Cukhala abandoned every ordinary social obligation or physically withdrew into a monastery.

It describes his spiritual orientation.

He had turned away from worldly entanglement.

That's the claim.

### Śivaikacittaḥ

**शिवैकचित्तः - śivaikacittaḥ**

Having one's mind directed exclusively toward Śiva.

**śiva** - Śiva.

**eka** - one, sole.

**citta** - mind, thought, inner disposition.

The sandhi:

*śiva + eka → śivaika.*

The compound describes a mind whose dominant or exclusive orientation is Śiva.

This is not yet the full metaphysical claim that Cukhala possessed perfect liberation.

It is a description of devotion or contemplative orientation.

The verse doesn't say that Abhinavagupta's father was omniscient.

It says his mind was devoted to Śiva and he had turned away from worldly affairs.

We should preserve that distinction.

### Cukhala - चुखल

The name appears as:

**चुखलाभिधानः - cukhalābhidhānaḥ**

“Called Cukhala.”

The form is grammatically straightforward.

The name *Cukhala* appears in other variants in the historical and textual tradition, including *Cukhalaka*.

And, importantly, the wider evidence identifies the same man as **Narasiṃhagupta**.

The 1918 editor explicitly states this identification in a note to the opening invocation, where Abhinavagupta's mother Vimala and father Narasiṃhagupta are mentioned in connection with the names of his parents.

Thus the more complete account is:

Cukhala, also called Cukhalaka, is identified with Narasiṃhagupta.

He is Abhinavagupta's father.

Varāhagupta is his father.

Atrigupta is an earlier ancestor of the line.

That's the genealogy.

And we have now reached the exact point where the philosopher himself enters the family narrative.

### The metre: Upajāti

Verse 12 belongs to the **Upajāti** family, using the eleven-syllable Indravajrā/Upendravajrā patterns.

The pādas are eleven syllables long.

The first begins:

*tas-yān-va-vā-ye-ma-ha-ti-pra-sū-tād.*

The consonant cluster *pr* makes the preceding short vowel heavy by position, an important detail in recognising the metrical pattern.

The second:

*va-rā-ha-gup-tāt-pra-ti-lab-dha-jan-mā.*

The remaining pādas follow the Upajāti family of eleven-syllable arrangements.

This is a different metrical structure from verse 11's Śālinī.

The verse lengths are similar, but the heavy-light sequences differ.

A proper metrical audit therefore cannot stop at saying “eleven syllables.”

We must identify the pattern.

### And now the human significance

Abhinavagupta has given us a lineage of learning.

But he's also presented his father as a person oriented away from saṃsāra and toward Śiva.

That gives the later self-description its force.

The son was born within this family.

He inherited a world in which religious devotion and learning were already important.

But his intellectual achievement cannot simply be explained as a mechanical family inheritance.

The epilogue will immediately turn to his own discernment, fearlessness, and work of explaining the hidden truth of Trika.

That is verse 13.

And it is one of the most revealing self-descriptions he ever wrote.


## III. Verse 13 - Abhinavagupta Finally Identifies Himself, and the Hidden Trika Is Brought into the Open

KSTS p. 280-281 · Gnoli p. 285 · Vasantatilakā

**Devanāgarī**

> तस्माद्विवेचितसमस्तपदार्थजाता-
> ल्लब्ध्वापि देहपदवीं परमेशपूताम् ।
> प्राप्ताभयोऽभिनवगुप्तपदाभिधानः
> प्रावेशयत्त्रिकसतत्त्वमिदं निगूढम् ॥१३॥

**IAST**

> *tasmād vivecitasamastapadārthajātāl*
> *labdhvāpi dehapadavīṃ parameśapūtām &#124;*
> *prāptābhayo 'bhinavaguptapadābhidhānaḥ*
> *prāveśayat trikasatattvam idaṃ nigūḍham &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“Having received from that father-who had discriminated the entire multitude of realities-an embodied existence sanctified by the Supreme Lord, the one who had attained fearlessness and was known by the designation Abhinavagupta brought this hidden truth of Trika into understanding.”

The final verb *prāveśayat* literally expresses causing something to enter. “Brought into understanding” conveys its explanatory force here, rather than pretending the Sanskrit directly says “published” or “revealed to the world.”

And there he is.

**Abhinavagupta.**

At last, the author of our entire thirty-six-part philosophical ordeal has named himself in the family narrative.

And look at the two things he emphasises.

The discernment inherited through his father.

And **fearlessness**.

Not supernatural powers.

Not royal patronage.

Not a list of his academic accomplishments.

Fearlessness.

The man who has spent hundreds of pages investigating the nature of consciousness describes himself as having attained a condition in which fear has been overcome.

Now that's a much more interesting self-portrait than an ordinary claim to intellectual brilliance.

### Tasmāt - तस्मात्

“From him.”

An ablative singular pronoun.

The antecedent is **Cukhala**, his father, introduced in verse 12.

This gives us a completely explicit connection:

Cukhala → Abhinavagupta.

The Sanskrit is not ambiguous about the immediate source of birth.

### Vivecitasamastapadārthajātāt

The long compound is:

**विवेचितसमस्तपदार्थजातात्**

*vivecita-samasta-padārtha-jātāt.*

**vivecita** - distinguished, examined, analysed, from *vi-* \+ √*vic*, to discriminate.

**samasta** - entire, complete.

**padārtha** - object or thing, literally something expressed by a word, and in philosophical settings an object or category of knowledge.

**jāta** - collection, multitude, aggregate; also “born” in other contexts.

The compound describes someone who has examined the entire range of meaningful realities or categories.

The ablative ending **-āt** agrees with the father.

This is an important description of Cukhala.

He is not merely a religious devotee.

He is praised as someone who possessed discriminative understanding of the whole field of objects.

In other words, Abhinavagupta is presenting a lineage of **intellectual discernment** as well as devotion.

### The sandhi of the first two pādas

At the boundary we have:

*vivecitasamastapadārthajātāt + labdhvā.*

The dental *t* assimilates before *l*:

*-āt + l- → -āl l-.*

Hence:

**जाताल्लब्ध्वा - jātāl labdhvā.**

The two *l* sounds straddle the morphological boundary.

This is not a new noun ending in *-āl*.

It is external sandhi.

That little distinction is worth learning because the full compound looks almost comically impenetrable until you separate its components.

### Dehapadavīm

**देहपदवीम् - dehapadavīm**

The condition or station of embodiment.

**deha** - body.

**padavī** - path, position, condition, station.

The accusative feminine singular **-īm** is governed by *labdhvā*, having obtained.

The expression describes receiving bodily existence.

And it is qualified by:

**param eśapūtām**, in continuous compound form **parameśapūtām**.

Purified or sanctified by the Supreme Lord.

### Parameśapūtām

**परमेशपूताम् - parameśapūtām**

**Parameśa** - the Supreme Lord.

**pūta** - purified, sanctified, a past passive participle related to √*pū*, to purify.

The feminine accusative agrees with *dehapadavīm*.

This is a religious evaluation of Abhinavagupta's embodiment.

It should not be turned into an independently verifiable claim that his physical body possessed extraordinary measurable properties.

The point is how he understands the divine significance of his own birth.

### Prāptābhayaḥ

**प्राप्ताभयः - prāptābhayaḥ**

Having attained fearlessness.

**prāpta** - attained, reached, obtained.

**abhaya** - fearlessness, absence of fear.

The compound is masculine nominative singular, describing Abhinavagupta.

And this word deserves particular attention.

What kind of fearlessness?

The verse does not define it through an explicit psychological inventory.

But in the broader Trika system, fear is closely connected with limitation and the experience of something fundamentally other than oneself.

If the subject recognises the universal consciousness as its ultimate nature, the metaphysical ground of absolute alienation is overcome.

That provides a philosophical interpretation of his claim.

But the historical statement remains a self-description: Abhinavagupta presents himself as having attained fearlessness.

It does not prove that he never experienced a startle response, anxiety, or ordinary concern.

### Abhinavaguptapadābhidhānaḥ

**अभिनवगुप्तपदाभिधानः**

*abhinavagupta-pada-abhidhānaḥ.*

“Known by the designation Abhinavagupta.”

**abhinava** - new, fresh, renewed, unprecedented.

**gupta** - protected, concealed, guarded.

**pada** - word, designation, expression.

**abhidhāna** - name or appellation.

The compound explicitly identifies the author's name.

It does not by itself prove precisely when or how he acquired that name.

Some later traditions interpret the name as an honorific reflecting mastery, but the verse's immediate content is simply that he is known by it.

We must distinguish the name's lexical components from a secure biography of its conferral.

### Trikasatattvam idaṃ nigūḍham

Now the object of his writing.

**त्रिकसतत्त्वमिदं निगूढम्**

*trika-satattvam idaṃ nigūḍham.*

“This hidden essential truth of Trika.”

The form **satattva** belongs to the semantic field of true reality or genuine principle.

**nigūḍha** - concealed, hidden, deeply embedded, from *ni-* \+ √*guh*, to conceal.

The same root underlies *guhya*, secret, a term we encountered repeatedly in the tantra.

The point is that the truth is difficult to apprehend or concealed by ordinary limitation.

The commentary brings it into intelligible expression.

### Prāveśayat

**प्रावेशयत् - prāveśayat**

A causative past formation associated with *pra-* \+ √*viś*, to enter.

Literally, “caused to enter.”

The object is the hidden truth of Trika.

The exact implied destination is not explicitly supplied as a separate noun in the verse.

Within the composition's context, the natural interpretation is that Abhinavagupta has introduced the doctrine into his readers' understanding through the commentary.

But we should not silently transform the causative into a verb that the Sanskrit doesn't contain.

This is the distinction between literal translation and contextual interpretation.

### The metre

**Vasantatilakā**, fourteen syllables in each pāda.

The long first compound is metrically disciplined.

The final pāda:

*prā-ve-śa-yat-tri-ka-sa-tat-tvam-i-daṃ-ni-gū-ḍham.*

Fourteen syllables, with the expected heavy and light positions.

The conjuncts matter.

**त्त्र - ttr** at the meeting of *prāveśayat* and *trika*.

**त्त्व - ttva** in *satattva*.

**गूढ - gūḍha**, with retroflex aspirated **ḍh** and long ū.

One cannot recover the correct metre by pronouncing every visible consonant as a separate syllable.

### The historical reconstruction so far

We now have the securely recoverable genealogical framework.

**Atrigupta**

Earlier ancestor from Antarvedī who came to Kashmir

Unspecified intervening generations

**Varāhagupta**

Member of Atrigupta's distinguished lineage

**Cukhala / Cukhalaka**

Also identified as Narasiṃhagupta

**Abhinavagupta**

The commentator, and brother of Manorathagupta

This distinction between a named ancestor and the immediate father-to-son links is important.

Atrigupta belongs to the earlier history of the family.

Varāhagupta and Cukhala belong to its later generations.

Abhinavagupta is explicitly identified as Cukhala's son.

And he now turns from genealogy to the people he is actually trying to teach.

The mood changes rather dramatically.

Because apparently the greatest problem with a work of profound metaphysics is that many people either cannot understand it or cannot be bothered to try.


## IV. Verse 14 - “Some People Cannot Think. Some People Think Badly. Perhaps One in a Hundred Thousand Will Make This Worthwhile.”

KSTS p. 281 · Gnoli p. 285 · Śārdūlavikrīḍita

**Devanāgarī**

> ये तावत्प्रविवेकवन्ध्यहृदयास्तेभ्यः प्रणामो वरः
> केऽप्यन्ये प्रविविञ्चते न च गताः पारं धिगेताञ्जडान् ।
> यस्त्वन्यः प्रविमर्शसारपदवीसंभावनासुस्थितो
> लक्षैकोऽपि स कश्चिदेव सफलीकुर्वीत यत्नं मम ॥१४॥

**IAST**

> *ye tāvat pravivekavandhyahṛdayās tebhyaḥ praṇāmo varaḥ*
> *ke 'py anye praviviñcate na ca gatāḥ pāraṃ dhig etāñ jaḍān &#124;*
> *yas tv anyaḥ pravimarśasārapadavīsaṃbhāvanāsusthito*
> *lakṣaiko 'pi sa kaścid eva saphalīkurvīta yatnaṃ mama &#124;&#124;*

**My literal translation:**

“As for those whose hearts are barren of genuine discrimination, it is better simply to offer them a salutation. There are others who attempt to discriminate but have not reached the far shore-damn those dullards! But if there is even one person in a hundred thousand, firmly established in the contemplation of the essential path of reflective awareness, let that person make my effort fruitful.”

There.

The gloves have come off.

The man who spent four verses explaining that he wants to help everyone has apparently encountered the actual reading public.

And his expectations have become somewhat more realistic.

**One in a hundred thousand.**

He will accept one.

One person who truly engages with the argument.

Everyone else can apparently enjoy a polite greeting and leave the serious enquiry alone.

Now, this is a hilarious passage.

But there's something much more interesting underneath it.

Abhinavagupta is distinguishing different relationships to knowledge.

The person incapable of discrimination.

The person who attempts enquiry but fails to understand.

And the rare person genuinely established in reflective insight.

These are not merely differences in intelligence.

They concern the capacity and willingness to enter into the enquiry required by the work.

### Pravivekavandhyahṛdaya

**प्रविवेकवन्ध्यहृदय - pravivekavandhyahṛdaya**

**praviveka** - thorough discrimination, careful distinction.

**vandhya** - barren, unproductive, incapable of yielding fruit.

**hṛdaya** - heart.

Thus:

“Those whose hearts are barren of discriminative understanding.”

This is rhetorically harsh.

The author is not offering a neutral psychometric classification.

He's criticising people who lack the capacity or disposition required to engage with the argument.

The image of barrenness is deliberate.

An intellectual heart that produces no genuine discernment.

And remember the banyan seed.

The Heart contains the potential of the entire universe.

But here a particular person's understanding has failed to produce the requisite fruit.

The botanical contrast is rather wonderful.

### Praṇāmo varaḥ

**प्रणामो वरः - praṇāmo varaḥ**

“A salutation is preferable.”

Why would Abhinavagupta prefer a salutation?

Because arguing with someone who cannot or will not engage the necessary distinctions is fruitless.

He is not saying such people lack the ultimate reality of consciousness.

That would contradict the metaphysics.

He is saying they are not presently suitable recipients of this particular kind of philosophical explanation.

The distinction is pedagogical.

Not ontological.

Someone may be fundamentally Śiva-consciousness while remaining entirely unable to understand a difficult Sanskrit commentary.

Indeed, the entire theory of contraction depends upon precisely that possibility.

### Praviviñcate

**प्रविविञ्चते - praviviñcate**

“They discriminate or attempt to distinguish.”

A present middle form associated with *pra-* \+ *vi-* \+ √*vic*, to discriminate.

This describes the second class.

They are at least trying.

But they have not reached the far shore.

### Pāram

**पारम् - pāram**

The far side, opposite shore, limit, completion.

The accusative singular appears with **gatāḥ**, gone or arrived.

**na ca gatāḥ pāram** -

“And they have not reached the far shore.”

The metaphor is conventional in Indian philosophical and religious language.

The far shore is completion.

The end of confusion.

The successful crossing of an intellectual or spiritual difficulty.

But Abhinavagupta's reaction is not a patient encouragement to keep trying.

**धिगेताञ्जडान् - dhig etāñ jaḍān.**

“Fie upon these dullards!”

And yes, **dhik** is a genuine Sanskrit exclamation of reproach or contempt.

The continuous form *dhig* reflects sandhi before the following voiced sound.

**jaḍa** means dull, inert, uncomprehending.

Abhinavagupta is being rude.

Not ambiguously.

Not in some secret metaphysical code.

He's directly expressing disdain.

It appears that the man who saw everything as Śiva could still become extremely frustrated with bad arguments.

Nonduality has not abolished the Sanskrit tradition of devastating intellectual insults.

### Pravimarśasārapadavī

Now the third kind of reader.

**प्रविमर्शसारपदवी - pravimarśasārapadavī**

The path of essential reflective insight.

**pra-vimarśa** - deep or thorough reflective apprehension.

**sāra** - essence, substance, central reality.

**padavī** - way, path, course.

This is not merely someone who knows the vocabulary.

It is someone capable of engaging the essential nature of the reflective enquiry.

The distinction matters enormously.

A person can memorise a philosophical system without understanding its reasoning.

Another can understand the reasoning without assimilating its implications.

The rare reader Abhinavagupta praises is established in the path of essential *vimarśa*.

### Saṃbhāvanāsusthitaḥ

**संभावनासुस्थितः - saṃbhāvanāsusthitaḥ**

Firmly situated in contemplation, consideration, or the relevant apprehensive possibility.

**saṃbhāvanā** can mean conception, possibility, consideration, or contemplative cultivation depending on context.

**susthita** - well established, firmly situated.

The compound indicates a reader who has developed a stable relation to the essential enquiry.

### Lakṣaikaḥ

**लक्षैकः - lakṣaikaḥ**

One in a hundred thousand.

**lakṣa** - one hundred thousand, a lakh.

**eka** - one.

The compound expresses extreme rarity.

And Abhinavagupta says:

**सफलीकुर्वीत यत्नं मम**

*saphalīkurvīta yatnaṃ mama.*

“Let that person make my effort fruitful.”

**saphala** - bearing fruit, successful.

**kurvīta** - optative middle of √*kṛ*, to make.

**yatna** - effort, exertion, endeavour.

**mama** - my.

This is an extraordinary statement.

He has written an enormous commentary.

And he would regard the work as worthwhile if one genuinely receptive person understood it.

That is not the logic of maximising readership.

It is the logic of genuine transmission.

### The metre

**Śārdūlavikrīḍita**, nineteen syllables per pāda.

All four pādas have nineteen syllables in the GRETIL reading, and their heavy-light pattern is consistent with the metre.

The opening scans:

*ye-tā-vat-pra-vi-ve-ka-van-dhya-hṛ-da-yās-te-bhyaḥ-pra-ṇā-mo-va-raḥ.*

Nineteen syllables.

The consonant cluster *ndhy* makes the preceding syllable heavy.

The long vowel ā in *hṛdayās* is also metrically significant.

And the final pāda:

*lakṣaiko 'pi sa kaścid eva saphalīkurvīta yatnaṃ mama*

likewise maintains the nineteen-syllable structure.

### A critical variant worth preserving

The KSTS electronic text reads a form corresponding to **saṃbhāvanāsusthitaḥ**, agreeing with GRETIL, but the same line includes small differences in the immediately adjacent wording.

The real caution is the last word of the verse.

GRETIL correctly reads **yatnaṃ mama**, “my effort.”

The digitised KSTS transcription displays a defective form resembling **yantraṃ mama**, “my instrument.”

The philosophical context strongly favours *yatnam*: Abhinavagupta is speaking about making his effort fruitful. But without the facsimile, the exact status of the electronic discrepancy cannot be established.

We should not turn a possible transcription error into a claim that Abhinavagupta regarded his commentary as a magical machine.

Although, considering the size of the work, I can understand why somebody might have thought that.

### The deeper point

The verse is not merely contemptuous.

It reveals the pedagogical purpose of the work.

Abhinavagupta is not trying to produce passive agreement.

He wants readers capable of genuine discrimination.

And he knows that this capacity is rare.

That makes the next verse even more interesting.

Because now he distinguishes people too lazy to examine themselves from those sincerely engaged in understanding the universe.

And he reserves his deepest respect for the latter.


## V. Verse 15 - “If You Can't Be Bothered to Examine Yourself, What Exactly Do You Expect Me to Do?”

KSTS p. 281 · Gnoli p. 285 · Śārdūlavikrīḍita

**Devanāgarī - close to the KSTS transmission**

> स्वात्मानं प्रविवेक्तुमप्यलसतां ये बिभ्रति प्रार्थना
> तान्प्रत्यात्मकदर्थनान्न परतः किंचित्फलं सोष्यते ।
> विश्वस्यास्य विविक्तये स्थिरधियो ये संरभन्ते पुनः
> तानभ्यर्थयितुं मयैष विहितो मूर्ध्ना प्रणामादरः ॥१५॥

**IAST - with the same unresolved construction**

> *svātmānaṃ pravivektum apy alasatāṃ ye bibhrati prārthanā*
> *tān praty ātmakadarthanān na parataḥ kiṃcit phalaṃ soṣyate &#124;*
> *viśvasyāsya viviktaye sthiradhiyo ye saṃrabhante punaḥ*
> *tān abhyarthayituṃ mayaiṣa vihito mūrdhnā praṇāmādaraḥ &#124;&#124;*

**Contextual translation:**

“Those who remain indolent even about discriminating their own Self-despite their appeal for instruction-can expect no fruit beyond self-torment. But those resolute people who earnestly undertake to discern the nature of this universe: to honour them, I have offered this respectful bow with my head.”

There is a textual qualification here. GRETIL transmits *svātmānāṃ*, where KSTS has the grammatically expected accusative *svātmānaṃ*. The word *prārthanā* is also syntactically awkward as transmitted; an accusative *prārthanām* would be natural with *bibhrati*, but that correction is not established from the consulted witnesses. The contextual meaning is reasonably clear, while the exact grammar of the first pāda remains unresolved. ; \]

And now we have the essential contrast.

A person who wants spiritual results without genuine enquiry.

And a person who is prepared to examine reality.

Abhinavagupta's respect is directed toward the second.

Not necessarily the person with the most elaborate ritual qualifications.

Not necessarily the person with the greatest reputation.

Not necessarily the person claiming the highest mystical experiences.

**The person who actually undertakes the work of discernment.**

### Svātmānam pravivektum

**स्वात्मानं प्रविवेक्तुम्**

*svātmānaṃ pravivektum.*

“To discern one's own Self.”

**sva** - own.

**ātman** - Self.

The accusative singular **ātmānam** is the object of the infinitive.

**pravivektum** - to examine thoroughly, distinguish, discern.

From *pra-* \+ *vi-* \+ √*vic*.

Notice the infinitive **-tum**.

The construction identifies the action in which the people are lazy.

Not simply ordinary reading.

Not simply recitation.

Discerning the Self.

The very reality that has been the subject of the whole commentary.

### Alasatām

**अलसताम् - alasatām**

Laziness, indolence, unwillingness to exert oneself.

The form here is accusative singular of the abstract noun *alasatā*.

It is governed by **bibhrati**, “they bear” or “they maintain.”

The subject is the group of people being criticised.

The verse is not saying that every difficult philosophical concept should be immediately understandable to everyone.

It is attacking unwillingness to engage.

A person may struggle sincerely with a complex argument.

That is different from refusing to examine anything while still expecting the result.

### Ātmakadarthana

**आत्मकदर्थन - ātmakadarthana**

Self-distress, self-torment, self-mortification, making oneself miserable.

**ātman** - self.

**kadarthana** - distress, degradation, torment, ill-treatment.

The phrase suggests that the expected result of such an approach is not liberation but wasted effort and self-inflicted difficulty.

The verb **soṣyate** is associated with the extracting or drawing out of a result, though the exact verbal analysis in this difficult line should not be asserted beyond the attested wording.

The broad argument is clear.

If someone refuses the very enquiry required by the teaching, the work cannot magically substitute for their own understanding.

### Viśvasyāsya viviktaye

**विश्वस्यास्य विविक्तये**

*viśvasyāsya viviktaye.*

“For the discernment of this universe.”

**viśvasya** - of the universe, genitive singular.

**asya** - of this, genitive singular.

**vivikti** - discrimination, separation, discernment.

The dative **-aye** marks purpose.

And here the scope expands.

The sincere reader is not simply concerned with a small private psychological state.

They want to understand the reality of the entire manifest universe.

Exactly what Abhinavagupta has been investigating.

### Sthiradhiyaḥ

**स्थिरधियः - sthiradhiyaḥ**

Those of firm understanding or resolute intelligence.

**sthira** - firm, stable, steady.

**dhī** - intelligence, understanding.

The nominative plural refers to those undertaking the enquiry.

And their defining characteristic is stability.

Not merely temporary enthusiasm.

Not merely excitement after a remarkable meditation.

The willingness to examine the subject carefully and persistently.

### Saṃrabhante

**संरभन्ते - saṃrabhante**

They undertake energetically, engage themselves, exert themselves.

A present middle form associated with *sam-* \+ √*rabh*, to seize or undertake.

The word has an energetic quality.

These are not merely passive recipients of a doctrine.

They are actively engaged in the investigation.

### Mūrdhnā praṇāmādaraḥ

**मूर्ध्ना प्रणामादरः**

*mūrdhnā praṇāmādaraḥ.*

A respectful salutation made with the head.

**mūrdhan** - head.

The instrumental singular is **mūrdhnā**.

**praṇāma** - bowing, respectful salutation.

**ādara** - respect, consideration, reverence.

Abhinavagupta says he has shown them honour through this gesture.

Now that is a remarkable reversal of spiritual hierarchy.

The great master bows.

To whom?

To the sincere investigators of reality.

The people who are willing to understand.

And the gesture is not merely decorative.

It expresses the seriousness with which he regards genuine enquiry.

### The metre

**Śārdūlavikrīḍita**, nineteen syllables per pāda.

The long compounds fit the nineteen-syllable pattern.

But the textual difficulties in the first half should remain visible.

Metrical regularity does not guarantee grammatical correctness in a damaged electronic witness.

A missing nasal can leave the syllable count unchanged.

The written forms must therefore be checked against reliable textual evidence.

### The real philosophical lesson

A book can help.

A teacher can help.

A mantra can help.

A ritual can help.

But none of these automatically makes someone understand a philosophical argument.

Abhinavagupta has already explained that supreme recognition is not produced by external action.

Now he makes the pedagogical counterpart explicit.

The student must actually engage the enquiry.

And the teacher's highest function is to help make that engagement possible.

This leads directly into the most savage passage of the entire epilogue.

Because Abhinavagupta has apparently encountered teachers who did the opposite.

Teachers who led their students into more confusion.

Teachers who bound people under the guise of liberating them.

And he is about to bring out a weapon.

A trident.

Not made of metal.

Made of knowledge.


## VI. Verse 16 - The Gurus Are Binding Everyone, So Abhinavagupta Builds a Trident to Cut the Ropes

KSTS p. 281 · Gnoli p. 285 · Śārdūlavikrīḍita

**Devanāgarī**

> भ्राम्यन्तो भ्रमयन्ति मन्दधिषणास्ते जन्तुचक्रं जडं
> स्वात्मीकृत्य गुणाभिधानवशतो बद्ध्वा दृढं बन्धनैः ।
> दृष्ट्वेत्थं गुरुभारवाहविधये यातानुयातान्पशून्
> तत्पाशप्रविकर्तनाय घटितं ज्ञानत्रिशूलं मया ॥१६॥

**IAST**

> *bhrāmyanto bhramayanti mandadhiṣaṇās te jantucakraṃ jaḍaṃ*
> *svātmīkṛtya guṇābhidhānavasato baddhvā dṛḍhaṃ bandhanaiḥ &#124;*
> *dṛṣṭvetthaṃ gurubhāravāhavidhaye yātānuyātān paśūn*
> *tatpāśapravikartanāya ghaṭitaṃ jñānatriśūlaṃ mayā &#124;&#124;*

**My literal translation:**

“Wandering in confusion themselves, those of feeble understanding lead the dull multitude of creatures astray. Making them their own, through the force of claims to virtue or attributed qualities, they bind them tightly with bonds. Seeing these bound creatures follow one another merely to carry the burdens of gurus, I fashioned the trident of knowledge to cut their fetters.”

There.

**The trident of knowledge.**

And suddenly the entire commentary acquires a new purpose.

Not simply explaining the secrets of Trika.

Not simply preserving a tantric scripture.

Not merely providing a better philosophical theory than the competing schools.

**Cutting people free from bad teachers.**

Now that's extraordinary.

Because Abhinavagupta has just spent hundreds of pages explaining the importance of the guru.

We discussed the guru's grace.

The transmission of mantra.

The role of initiation.

The way a teacher may disclose the supreme Heart.

And now he tells us that teachers can also be a principal source of bondage.

Not merely ineffective.

Actually binding.

They can take students who are already confused, appropriate them, and lead them into still greater confusion.

And Abhinavagupta's response is to create a work of discriminative knowledge.

The irony is magnificent.

The teacher writes a commentary to protect students from teachers.

### Bhrāmyantaḥ bhramayanti

**भ्राम्यन्तो भ्रमयन्ति**

*bhrāmyanto bhramayanti.*

This is one of the most pointed verbal constructions in the entire poem.

**bhrāmyantaḥ** - wandering, roaming, going astray; a present participial form related to √*bhram*.

**bhramayanti** - they cause to wander, lead astray; a causative present form of the same root.

Thus:

“They themselves are wandering, and they make others wander.”

The repetition of the root creates the argument.

Their misdirection is not necessarily an elaborate conspiracy undertaken with perfect knowledge.

They are themselves confused.

And because they possess the social role of teachers, they reproduce their confusion in others.

This is a much more sophisticated account of bad teaching than the simple distinction between honest and dishonest people.

A teacher may sincerely believe they understand something.

They may have a reputation.

They may possess disciples.

They may perform the appropriate rituals.

And still misunderstand the very subject they claim to teach.

That is why **competence matters**.

### Mandadhiṣaṇāḥ

**मन्दधिषणाः - mandadhiṣaṇāḥ**

Those of sluggish or deficient understanding.

**manda** - slow, dull, weak.

**dhiṣaṇā** - intelligence, understanding, intellectual capacity.

The nominative plural agrees with the wandering teachers.

Again Abhinavagupta is not offering a modern intelligence assessment.

He's condemning intellectual incompetence in people exercising religious authority.

### Jantucakram jaḍam

**जन्तुचक्रं जडम्**

*jantucakraṃ jaḍam.*

The dull multitude or circle of living beings.

**jantu** - creature, living being.

**cakra** - wheel, circle, group, assemblage.

**jaḍa** - inert, dull, uncomprehending.

The group is the object of *bhramayanti*.

The teachers lead it astray.

### Svātmīkṛtya

**स्वात्मीकृत्य - svātmīkṛtya**

Having made one's own.

From **sva**, own, and *ātman*, self, with the verbal formation expressing appropriation or making something belong to oneself.

The absolutive **-kṛtya** marks a prior action.

The teachers have made the creatures their own.

This is a powerful word in the context of spiritual authority.

A genuine teacher may help a student become independent in understanding.

An incompetent or exploitative teacher may instead make the student dependent upon the teacher's identity, reputation, or approval.

The Sanskrit here uses the language of appropriation.

Not liberation.

And the result is bondage.

### Guṇābhidhānavasataḥ

**गुणाभिधानवशतः - guṇābhidhānavasataḥ**

“Through the force of designations of qualities,” or “because of attributed virtues or reputations.”

**guṇa** - quality, excellence, virtue, attribute.

**abhidhāna** - naming, designation, appellation.

**vaśataḥ** - through the influence, power, or control of something.

The precise referent of the qualities being named is not made explicit in a separate clause.

The context suggests reputation, qualities ascribed to teachers, or labels that help them command adherents.

But it would be too much to translate the compound as a fully articulated theory of fraudulent certification.

The literal wording is narrower.

The teachers gain or exercise control through attributed qualities.

The result is that the creatures are bound.

### Baddhvā dṛḍhaṃ bandhanaiḥ

**बद्ध्वा दृढं बन्धनैः**

*baddhvā dṛḍhaṃ bandhanaiḥ.*

“Having bound them firmly with bonds.”

The repetition is forceful.

**baddhvā** - having bound, absolutive from √*bandh*.

**dṛḍham** - firmly, tightly.

**bandhanaiḥ** - by bonds, instrumental plural.

The participial structure gives an image of tightening.

The students are not being set free.

They are being tied more tightly.

And now the irony becomes devastating.

Because tantric initiation is frequently described as a means of severing bonds.

Here the supposed initiators are creating or reinforcing bonds.

The institution has inverted its own purpose.

### Gurubhāravāhavidhaye

**गुरुभारवाहविधये**

*gurubhāravāhavidhaye.*

For the condition or activity of carrying the burden of gurus.

**guru** - teacher; also heavy in ordinary Sanskrit, an additional semantic resonance.

**bhāra** - weight, burden, load.

**vāha** - bearing, carrying.

**vidhi** - mode, procedure, condition.

The compound is dative singular in the transmitted construction.

And the double resonance of *guru* is almost too perfect.

The guru is the teacher.

But *guru* also means heavy.

The creatures become burden-bearers.

An extraordinarily sharp image for a religious relationship that has ceased to be liberating.

### Yātānuyātān paśūn

**यातानुयातान्पशून्**

*yātānuyātān paśūn.*

Creatures following those who have gone before.

**yāta** - gone, from √*yā*, to go.

**anuyāta** - followed after, from *anu-* \+ √*yā*.

**paśu** - bound creature, animal, and in Śaiva technical vocabulary the limited soul.

The accusative plural **paśūn** is the object of **dṛṣṭvā**, having seen.

The image is of a procession.

One follows another.

Then another follows them.

They are directed by the apparent authority of the group.

And the whole procession may be carrying an entirely unnecessary burden.

The Śaiva technical sense of *paśu* makes the metaphor even sharper.

The people seeking liberation from bondage have become bound through the very system that claimed to liberate them.

### Tatpāśapravikartanāya

Now the purpose of the work.

**तत्पाशप्रविकर्तनाय**

*tat-pāśa-pravikartanāya.*

“To cut their fetters completely.”

**tat** - their, referring to the creatures just described.

**pāśa** - rope, bond, fetter.

**pravikartana** - thorough cutting, severing, cutting apart.

Connected with *pra-* \+ *vi-* \+ √*kṛt*, to cut.

The dative ending **-āya** marks purpose.

The purpose is liberation from the bonds.

Not establishing another kind of dependency upon Abhinavagupta himself.

That distinction is crucial.

### Jñānatriśūlam

**ज्ञानत्रिशूलम् - jñānatriśūlam**

The trident of knowledge.

**jñāna** - knowledge.

**tri** - three.

**śūla** - trident, spear, pointed weapon.

The accusative singular is the object of **ghaṭitam**, fashioned or constructed.

And the agent is explicit:

**mayā** - by me.

Abhinavagupta says he fashioned the trident.

Now what exactly are its three prongs?

The verse does **not** explicitly enumerate them.

We may reasonably connect the image with the Trika's triadic powers, its three goddesses, or the broader system of differentiated knowledge and action.

But we should not manufacture a single definitive mapping and claim that verse 16 itself supplies it.

It does not.

The secure claim is that the commentary is figured as a trident of knowledge designed to cut bondage.

The triadic symbolism is appropriate to the work.

Its precise specification would require further evidence.

### The metre

**Śārdūlavikrīḍita**, nineteen syllables per pāda.

All four pādas have nineteen syllables, and their weight patterns conform to the metre.

For example:

*bhrām-yan-to-bhra-ma-yan-ti-man-da-dhi-ṣa-ṇās-te-jan-tu-ca-kraṃ-ja-ḍaṃ.*

The initial *bhrām* is heavy.

The cluster *nt* affects the following syllabic division.

And the long compound remains metrically regular.

### The philosophical importance

This verse is a direct statement about the ethical purpose of scholarship.

Correct understanding can protect people from confusion transmitted through authority.

A teacher who possesses an impressive reputation but lacks knowledge may reproduce bondage.

A text that teaches genuine discrimination can help sever it.

That is one of the strongest arguments in the entire epilogue for the importance of rigorous interpretation.

And it explains why Abhinavagupta is so unwilling to let readers accept mere verbal claims.

The supreme Heart must be understood.

Not merely advertised.

Not merely invoked as a credential.

Not merely claimed through an initiatory title.

The metaphysical doctrine requires actual discernment.

And then the next verse becomes almost painfully personal.

Because Abhinavagupta admits that the problem isn't merely something he has observed in other people's lives.

**He has experienced it himself.**


## VII. Verse 17 - “I've Been Bullshitted by Gurus Who Couldn't Even Pronounce the Word ‘Reality.’”

KSTS p. 281 · Gnoli p. 285 · Āryā

**Devanāgarī**

> बहुभिरपि सोऽहमेव
> भ्रमितस्तत्त्वोपदेशकंमन्यैः ।
> तत्त्वमिति वर्णयुगमपि
> येषां रसना न पस्पर्श ॥१७॥

**IAST**

> *bahubhir api so 'ham eva*
> *bhramitas tattvopadeśakaṃmanyaiḥ &#124;*
> *tattvam iti varṇayugam api*
> *yeṣāṃ rasanā na pasparśa &#124;&#124;*

**Literal translation:**

“I myself have been led astray by many who imagined themselves teachers of reality-people whose tongues had not even touched the pair of syllabic units in the word *tattva*.”

There.

The confession.

**So 'ham eva.**

“I myself.”

Not some anonymous unfortunate student.

Not merely the confused multitude of the previous verse.

**Abhinavagupta himself.**

The man whose works became among the greatest achievements of Sanskrit philosophy says that he too was misled by self-styled teachers.

And the insult is absolutely magnificent.

These people claim to teach *tattva*.

Reality.

Truth.

The essential nature of things.

But their tongues, he says, have not even touched the elementary expression of the word.

Not even **tattva**.

The intellectual equivalent of a person claiming to teach Sanskrit phonology who cannot distinguish dental *t* from retroflex *ṭ*.

Except here the consequences are potentially much more serious.

People are looking for liberation.

And the teacher may not understand the subject.

### Bahubhiḥ api

**बहुभिरपि - bahubhir api**

“Even by many.”

**bahu** - many.

The instrumental plural **bahubhiḥ** identifies the agents.

Before *api*, sandhi produces **bahubhir api**.

The number is not specified.

He does not give us a list of the supposedly incompetent teachers.

It would be irresponsible to identify them with particular historical figures without independent evidence.

The verse is a general confession of repeated misdirection.

### So 'ham eva

**सोऽहमेव - so 'ham eva**

“That very I.”

The underlying forms are:

**saḥ** - that one, he.

**aham** - I.

**eva** - indeed, precisely.

The continuous expression is emphatic.

The person being deceived was Abhinavagupta himself.

The *so 'ham* combination has a broader history in Sanskrit religious language, but here its function is a direct first-person self-identification.

We should not automatically treat it as an inserted *so 'ham* meditation-mantra.

The context is autobiographical.

### Bhramitaḥ

**भ्रमितः - bhramitaḥ**

Caused to wander, confused, led astray.

A past participial form connected with the causative of √*bhram*, to wander.

This deliberately echoes verse 16.

**bhrāmyantaḥ** - wandering themselves.

**bhramayanti** - they make others wander.

Now:

**bhramitaḥ** - I was made to wander.

The sequence is rhetorically brilliant.

First the general problem.

Then the causal mechanism.

Then the confession.

The author is not standing outside the issue.

He has been one of its victims.

### Tattvopadeśakaṃmanya

**तत्त्वोपदेशकंमन्य - tattvopadeśakaṃmanya**

Someone who imagines themselves a teacher of truth.

**tattva** - reality, principle, essential truth.

**upadeśaka** - one who instructs, teacher.

**-manya** - thinking oneself to be, considering oneself to have a certain status.

The compound carries a distinctly disparaging implication.

A self-styled teacher.

Someone who claims a role they may not actually deserve.

The instrumental plural **-manyaiḥ** identifies the people who misled him.

And now the punchline.

### Tattvam iti varṇayugam

**तत्त्वमिति वर्णयुगम्**

*tattvam iti varṇayugam.*

“The pair of sound-units expressed as *tattva*.”

The verse itself calls it a **varṇayuga**, a pair of *varṇas*.

But from the standpoint of strict Sanskrit phonemic analysis, *tattva* is not literally composed of only two individual phonemes.

It contains several consonantal and vocalic elements.

The poetic expression is therefore best understood as referring to its two syllabic units, **tat** and **tva**, or as an idiomatic diminutive description of the very short word.

We should not convert the poetic insult into a false assertion that Sanskrit phonology recognises only two phonemes in the entire word.

The distinction is philologically important.

The insult is that the teacher doesn't grasp even the elementary expression of the reality they claim to teach.

### Rasanā

**रसना - rasanā**

Tongue.

The feminine noun is nominative singular here.

The tongue is the subject of the final verb.

This is a wonderfully concrete image.

The teacher's tongue has never touched even the word *tattva*.

What a way to expose the gulf between a title and real competence.

### Pasparśa

**पस्पर्श - pasparśa**

A perfect active form of √*spṛś*, to touch.

Third-person singular.

“She or it did not touch.”

The feminine tongue is the grammatical subject.

And **na** negates the action.

Thus:

**yeṣāṃ rasanā na pasparśa**

“Whose tongue did not touch…”

The relative genitive plural **yeṣām** refers back to the self-styled teachers.

The syntax is compact, pointed, and devastating.

### The metre

The verse is in the **Āryā** quantitative tradition.

Its four printed segments have the standard distribution of **12 + 18 / 12 + 15 mātrās**, counting the third segment's final short syllable according to the internal continuation rather than forcing an artificial pāda-final heaviness.

This is not a fixed eight-syllable anuṣṭubh.

It is a mātrā-based metre.

The shift from the huge nineteen-syllable Śārdūlavikrīḍita of verse 16 to the compact rhythm of Āryā is striking.

Verse 16 constructs the trident.

Verse 17 delivers the jab.

### The deeper admission

This is one of the most important moments in the entire work.

Abhinavagupta is not claiming that he was born with complete, effortless, perfectly articulated knowledge.

He acknowledges having been misled.

That matters because it places his final authority not simply in inherited social status or an unexamined claim of enlightenment.

It places the subsequent work of philosophical discrimination at the centre.

He encountered confusion.

He examined the teaching.

He learned to distinguish genuine understanding from false authority.

And he composed the commentary to help others do the same.

It is an unusually strong account of why a religious scholar might value rigorous criticism.

And the next verse explains what he believed enabled him to undertake that task.

**Śiva's grace, operating through the heart of a guru.**


## VIII. Verse 18 - Śiva Has Entered the Heart of the Guru, and Apparently Assigned Abhinavagupta an Extremely Difficult Job

KSTS p. 281 · Gnoli p. 285 · Āryā

**Devanāgarī**

> परमेश्वरः प्रपन्न-
> प्रोद्धरणकृपाप्रयुक्तगुरुहृदयः ।
> श्रीमान्देवः शम्भु-
> र्मामियति नियुक्तवांस्तत्त्वे ॥१८॥

**IAST**

> *parameśvaraḥ prapanna-*
> *proddharaṇakṛpāprayuktaguruhṛdayaḥ &#124;*
> *śrīmān devaḥ śaṃbhur*
> *mām iyati niyuktavāṃs tattve &#124;&#124;*

**My literal translation:**

“The Supreme Lord, the glorious god Śambhu, whose guru-heart is moved by compassion to lift up those who seek refuge, has appointed me to this much of the teaching concerning reality.”

Now consider the extraordinary movement.

In verse 16, Abhinavagupta denounces incompetent gurus.

In verse 17, he admits that he himself was misled by them.

In verse 18, he identifies the ultimate source of his genuine instruction.

**Śambhu.**

But the important expression is not merely the name of the deity.

It is the compound describing the **guru-heart**.

The heart moved by compassion toward those seeking refuge.

This connects directly with the opening verse of the epilogue.

There, Maheśvara was engaged in raising up those who surrendered.

Here, Śambhu's compassionate guru-heart is the means through which Abhinavagupta understands his own commission.

The text has come full circle.

### Parameśvara - परमेश्वर

**parama** - supreme, highest.

**īśvara** - Lord, ruler, sovereign.

The compound means Supreme Lord.

The nominative singular **parameśvaraḥ** is the subject of the sentence.

And the subject is further identified as:

**śrīmān devaḥ śambhuḥ.**

The glorious god Śambhu.

The verse is not introducing three independent gods.

The names and adjectives refer to the same supreme divine agent.

### Prapanna - प्रपन्न

One who has resorted to refuge or surrendered.

From *pra-* \+ √*pad*, to approach, go toward, resort to.

The word appears in verse 1 as well.

That repetition is deliberate and thematically important.

The people seeking help are not represented as already possessing complete understanding.

They are those turning toward the divine for liberation.

And the Lord's response is described through compassion.

### Proddharaṇa - प्रोद्धरण

Thorough uplifting, raising up, rescue.

The form can be understood through *pra-* \+ *ud-* \+ √*hṛ*, to lift or carry away.

The vowel combination *pra + ud-* contributes to the surface **pro-**.

In the compound, the meaning concerns rescuing or liberating those who seek refuge.

### Kṛpā - कृपा

Compassion, mercy, pity.

This is a particularly important word.

After two verses of devastating criticism, Abhinavagupta now identifies the ultimate teacher's motive as **compassion**.

Not reputation.

Not collecting disciples.

Not exerting social control.

Not maintaining an institution for its own sake.

Compassion for those who need help.

The contrast with the fraudulent teachers of verse 16 could hardly be stronger.

### Prayukta - प्रयुक्त

Set in motion, engaged, impelled, applied.

A past passive participle from *pra-* \+ √*yuj*, to join or employ.

Thus the guru-heart is described as impelled by compassion.

This is not a passive, indifferent awareness.

It is a conscious movement toward liberation.

Within Abhinavagupta's theology, that movement is a manifestation of **anugraha**, grace.

### Guruhṛdaya - गुरुहृदय

The heart of the guru, or the heart assuming the function of the teacher.

**guru** - teacher.

**hṛdaya** - heart.

Here the expression binds the divine Lord to the function of instruction.

We should distinguish the ordinary human teacher from the supreme divine ground.

The verse does not identify every person claiming to be a guru as automatically omniscient.

Indeed, the previous two verses would make that interpretation absurd.

The genuine teacher manifests the liberating function of Śiva-consciousness.

The false teacher may obscure it.

The criterion remains understanding and liberation, not the title.

### Niyuktavān

**नियुक्तवान् - niyuktavān**

Having appointed, assigned, commissioned.

An active possessive participial formation from **niyukta**, appointed or engaged, connected with *ni-* \+ √*yuj*.

The masculine nominative singular agrees with Śambhu.

The object is:

**mām** - me, accusative singular.

Thus:

**mām ... niyuktavān**

“He appointed me.”

### Iyati tattve

**इयति तत्त्वे - iyati tattve**

“In this much of the matter concerning reality.”

**iyat** - this much, of this extent.

**tattva** - reality, essential principle, true nature.

The locative singular **tattve** indicates the domain of the commission.

This does not necessarily imply that Abhinavagupta believes he has exhaustively explained the whole of ultimate reality.

Recall verse 2.

Who can impose a final limit on Śambhu's teaching?

The author's commission is particular.

The supreme reality is not exhausted by that particular exposition.

That is the subtle balance of these verses.

He claims divine encouragement.

But he also acknowledges the finite scope of his own work.

### The metre

This is **Āryā**, with the familiar quantitative pattern:

| Quarter | Mātrās |
|--------|--------|
| *parameśvaraḥ prapanna-* | 12 |
| *proddharaṇakṛpāprayuktaguruhṛdayaḥ* | 18 |
| *śrīmān devaḥ śaṃbhur* | 12 |
| *mām iyati niyuktavāṃs tattve* | 15 |

The fourth quarter's short syllables and heavy final syllable are important to its quantitative structure.

The metre is regular in the GRETIL reading.

### What does the verse actually establish?

It establishes Abhinavagupta's understanding of his own authorship.

He sees the work as commissioned through Śiva's compassion, operating through the guru-heart.

That is a religious self-description.

It does not independently prove a supernatural communication.

But it is a fundamental part of how Abhinavagupta understands the origin and purpose of his work.

And the following verse identifies the human philosophical tradition through which that work was shaped.

**Somānanda.**

Not a random divine revelation floating free of earlier scholarship.

A historical philosophical lineage.

This is where the spiritual and intellectual histories meet.


## IX. Verse 19 - Somānanda's Teaching Enters the Heart

KSTS p. 281-282 · Gnoli p. 285-286 · Quantitative verse; metrical reading uncertain

**Devanāgarī**

> तत्तत्त्वनिर्मलस्थिति-
> विभागिहृदये स्वयं प्रविष्टमिव ।
> श्रीसोमानन्दमतं
> विमृश्य मया निबद्धमिदम् ॥१९॥

**IAST**

> *tattattvanirmalasthiti-*
> *vibhāgihṛdaye svayaṃ praviṣṭam iva &#124;*
> *śrīsomānandamataṃ*
> *vimṛśya mayā nibaddham idam &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“Having reflected upon the venerable Somānanda's teaching-as though it had itself entered a heart able to discern the pure condition of the respective principles-I composed this work.”

This verse requires some grammatical care. The relationship among *tattattvanirmalasthiti*, *vibhāgihṛdaye*, and *svayaṃ praviṣṭam iva* is compressed; the translation expresses the natural sense without pretending that the compound permits only one possible segmentation.

And now consider the importance of this statement.

Abhinavagupta has just claimed that Śiva's compassion moved him to compose the work.

But then he immediately identifies **Somānanda's doctrine** as something he reflected upon in the process.

That's a crucial piece of intellectual history.

The philosophy did not simply materialise without antecedents.

It developed through a lineage of argument, interpretation, criticism, and refinement.

Somānanda's *Śivadṛṣṭi* is one of the foundational works of philosophical Kashmir Śaivism.

Utpaladeva developed the Pratyabhijñā system further.

Abhinavagupta inherited and transformed that intellectual tradition.

The verse explicitly acknowledges Somānanda.

### The historical lineage

The relevant line of philosophical instruction is:

**Somānanda → Utpaladeva → Lakṣmaṇagupta → Abhinavagupta.**

This is supported independently by Abhinavagupta's introductory verses to the *Īśvarapratyabhijñāvimarśinī*, in which he describes Utpaladeva as transmitting Somānanda's teaching and identifies his own understanding as received through Lakṣmaṇagupta. ; ; \]

The lineage matters because Abhinavagupta's philosophical work is not merely an appeal to scriptural authority.

It involves sustained engagement with rival theories of cognition, reality, and subjectivity.

The tradition's central claim-that the limited knower is ultimately grounded in one universal consciousness-was developed through careful argument.

And Abhinavagupta's epilogue places his commentary within that history.

### Tattattva - तत्तत्त्व

The repeated *tat* deserves attention.

Depending on segmentation, the expression can evoke “each respective principle” or “that true principle.”

The surrounding compound concerns the pure condition of the principles and their discrimination.

The contextual translation therefore refers to discerning the respective realities.

But we should not pretend that the compound is a simple one-word dictionary entry.

It is a poetic construction.

### Nirmalasthiti - निर्मलस्थिति

**nirmala** - stainless, pure, free from impurity.

**sthiti** - condition, abiding, standing, state.

The compound means a pure or unobscured condition.

In Trika, *mala*, impurity or limitation, plays an important role in explaining contraction.

Thus *nirmala* possesses a clear doctrinal resonance.

The truth is not merely a collection of externally juxtaposed philosophical categories.

It concerns the real condition of consciousness and its manifestation.

### Vibhāgi - विभागि

Distinguishing, discerning, dividing, or able to make distinctions.

The adjective belongs to the family of *vibhāga*, division or differentiation.

And in this context the relevant distinction is intellectual.

A mind that can discriminate the meanings of different principles.

One that can distinguish them without mistaking their differentiated character for ultimate separation.

This is exactly the skill Abhinavagupta has praised throughout the epilogue.

### Svayaṃ praviṣṭam iva

**स्वयं प्रविष्टमिव**

*svayaṃ praviṣṭam iva.*

“As though it had entered by itself.”

**svayam** - oneself, spontaneously, of its own accord.

**praviṣṭa** - entered, from *pra-* \+ √*viś*.

**iva** - as if, like.

The comparison is important.

The doctrine is described as though it had spontaneously entered the heart.

The expression is not a straightforward account of a physical object entering the chest.

It concerns the way the teaching has become internalised or present within understanding.

And the word **iva** explicitly marks the comparison.

As though.

We should not erase that qualification.

### Śrīsomānandamatam

**श्रीसोमानन्दमतम्**

*śrī-Somānanda-matam.*

The venerable doctrine of Somānanda.

**Śrī** - an honorific.

**Somānanda** - the philosopher's name.

**mata** - doctrine, view, position, what is understood or accepted.

The accusative singular **matam** is the object of the following absolutive:

**vimṛśya**.

### Vimṛśya

**विमृश्य - vimṛśya**

Having examined or reflected upon.

An absolutive from *vi-* \+ √*mṛś*.

And this is one of the most important verbal choices in the epilogue.

Abhinavagupta does not merely say:

“I copied Somānanda.”

Nor:

“I received a formula and repeated it without understanding.”

He says he **examined** the doctrine.

Reflected upon it.

Considered it.

And then composed the work.

The verb belongs to the same family as *vimarśa*, the term that has structured the entire metaphysics of self-awareness.

So we have a beautiful connection.

The philosophical *vimarśa* of consciousness.

The intellectual *vimarśa* of the commentator.

Both are forms of reflective activity, though they should not be equated without qualification.

### Mayā nibaddham idam

**मया निबद्धमिदम्**

*mayā nibaddham idam.*

“This was composed by me.”

**mayā** - by me, instrumental singular.

**nibaddha** - composed, arranged, bound together, from *ni-* \+ √*bandh*.

**idam** - this.

The sentence explicitly identifies Abhinavagupta's act of composition.

The verb of writing is not a mere mechanical transcription.

It is the binding together of meaning through reflection upon the inherited teaching.

### The metrical difficulty

This verse is arranged like an **Āryā-family** quantitative stanza, but the consulted GRETIL transcription does not yield an entirely secure regular Āryā scansion.

With normal internal syllable weights, the four printed segments are approximately **12 + 17 / 12 + 14 mātrās**, rather than the standard 12 + 18 / 12 + 15.

That may reflect textual loss, differences in permissible segmentation, or a nonstandard metrical construction. The digitised KSTS witness does not provide an unambiguous resolution.

It would be dishonest to label the metre “verified regular Āryā” merely because the layout resembles one.

So the defensible conclusion is narrower:

**A quantitative Āryā-like verse whose exact metrical reading requires further collation.**

This is precisely the kind of uncertainty that a philologically rigorous edition must preserve.

### The philosophical significance

Now we see the complementarity of grace and scholarship.

Verse 18 describes Śiva's compassionate commission.

Verse 19 describes reflective engagement with Somānanda's doctrine.

These are not presented as contradictory sources.

Within Abhinavagupta's worldview, the inherited teaching and its philosophical examination can be manifestations of divine grace.

But in historical analysis, we can distinguish them.

The theological interpretation is one level.

The documented intellectual lineage is another.

And both matter.

Now the author is ready to conclude.

But not with an ordinary professional acknowledgement.

Not with a final claim to mastery.

Not even with a simple statement that the work is complete.

He turns directly to the goddesses.

And the Sanskrit suddenly becomes magnificent.


## X. Verse 20 - The Goddesses Return, Carrying a Trident That Cuts Through Millions of Bonds

KSTS p. 282 · Gnoli p. 286 · Sragdharā

**Devanāgarī**

> हंहो हृच्चक्रचारप्रविचरणलसन्निर्भरानन्दपूर्णा
> देव्योऽस्मत्पाशकोटिप्रविघटनपटुज्ञानशूलोर्ध्वधाराः ।
> चेतोवाक्कायमेतद्विगतभवभयोत्पत्ति युष्मासु सम्यक्
> प्रोतं यत्तेन मह्यं व्रजत किल हृदि द्राक्प्रसादं प्रसह्य ॥२०॥

**IAST**

> *haṃho hṛccakracārapravicaraṇalasannirbharānandapūrṇā*
> *devyo 'smatpāśakoṭipravighaṭanapaṭujñānaśūlordhvadhārāḥ &#124;*
> *cetovākkāyam etad vigatabhavabhayotpatti yuṣmāsu samyak*
> *protaṃ yat tena mahyaṃ vrajata kila hṛdi drāk prasādaṃ prasahya &#124;&#124;*

**My translation:**

“Ah! O goddesses, filled with overflowing bliss shining through the movements of the Heart's circle, you who are the keen upper edges of the trident of knowledge, skilled at breaking apart my countless bonds! Because this mind, speech, and body of mine-freed from the arising of fear of worldly becoming-have been properly threaded into you, come swiftly and powerfully into my heart with your grace!”

This is an interpretive translation of a dense, highly compounded verse. In particular, the relation between *vigatabhavabhayotpatti*, *cetovākkāyam*, and *protam* permits some variation in English phrasing. The central prayer, however, is unmistakable.

**Come into my heart with grace.**

And now we have reached one of the most magnificent reversals in the entire work.

Abhinavagupta has spent hundreds of pages analysing the powers of the divine Mother.

The alphabet.

The Mātṛkā.

The three goddesses.

The stages of speech.

The movement of Śakti.

The powers of cognition.

The cosmic processes of manifestation and withdrawal.

And now, after all that analysis, he addresses the goddesses directly.

No longer merely as subjects of philosophical description.

As the recipients of prayer.

The intellectual investigation has become devotion.

And the imagery is spectacular.

The goddesses are the edges of a trident.

Not just any trident.

**The trident of knowledge that breaks the bonds of the limited subject.**

Exactly the instrument Abhinavagupta described himself as fashioning in verse 16.

The weapon returns.

But now its power is identified with the goddesses.

And that's a crucial transformation.

The author's intellectual work is not understood as an independent achievement of a self-sufficient ego.

Its liberating power belongs to Śakti.

### Haṃho - हंहो

An exclamation.

An attention-command, invocation, or emotional opening.

It can convey urgency, wonder, or direct address.

Here it introduces the goddesses.

The tone is no longer discursive.

It's immediate.

**Haṃho!**

A sudden invocation.

The philosopher has stopped explaining.

He is speaking to the divine.

### Hṛccakra - हृच्चक्र

**hṛd** - heart.

**cakra** - wheel, circle, configuration, divine assemblage.

Through sandhi:

*hṛd + cakra → hṛccakra.*

The dental *d* changes before the palatal *c*, producing the doubled palatal articulation represented by **च्च**.

The compound means the circle of the Heart.

We have encountered this repeatedly.

The Heart is not simply the physical organ.

It is the supreme conscious ground.

The *cakra* expresses its differentiated powers.

And the goddesses are associated with the dynamic movement of those powers.

### Cāra - चार

Movement, going, activity, circulation.

Connected with √*car*, to move.

### Pravicaraṇa - प्रविचरण

Moving through, traversing, examining, passing throughout.

From *pra-* \+ *vi-* \+ √*car*.

The compound suggests the movement of divine powers through the Heart's circle.

The 1918 electronic transcription differs from GRETIL near this word, giving a damaged form resembling *aviracana*. The parallel *pravicaraṇa* is coherent with the surrounding vocabulary of movement and the Heart-circle, and preserves the metre. But without the original manuscript collation, the discrepancy should remain a textual caution rather than a confidently reconstructed manuscript variant. ; \]

### Lasat - लसत्

Shining, gleaming, playing, moving beautifully.

A present participial form from √*las*, to shine or sport.

The word gives the goddess-description its luminous quality.

The Heart-circle is not an inert diagram.

It is a dynamic manifestation of divine power.

### Nirbhara - निर्भर

Full, abundant, overflowing, intense.

The compound intensifies **ānanda**, bliss.

### Ānanda - आनन्द

Bliss, delight, fullness, joy.

Within Abhinavagupta's metaphysics, the highest *ānanda* is not merely a pleasant sensation.

It concerns the self-relish of consciousness's own freedom.

The goddesses are described as full of that bliss.

And their power is not external to the supreme Heart.

### Pūrṇāḥ - पूर्णाः

Full, complete.

Feminine nominative plural in the transmitted poetic construction.

It qualifies the goddesses.

The plural is important.

We are not simply looking at an isolated singular deity.

The verse addresses the divine powers as a plurality, while the surrounding metaphysics understands them as manifestations of one supreme Śakti.

The plurality is not abolished.

Its ultimate ground is recognised.

### Devyaḥ - देव्यः

Goddesses.

Feminine plural of *devī*.

The verse is in direct address.

The goddesses are not merely the topic of discussion.

They are being invoked.

And the crucial imagery comes next.

### Asmatpāśakoṭi

**अस्मत्पाशकोटि - asmatpāśakoṭi**

My or our countless bonds.

**asmat** - the first-person pronoun base, in compound.

**pāśa** - bond, rope, fetter.

**koṭi** - a large number, commonly ten million in numerical usage, but frequently used poetically for countless or innumerable multitudes.

The compound evokes an enormous number of limiting bonds.

Now remember the Śaiva concept of *paśu*.

The bound subject.

And *pāśa*.

The fetter.

The liberation of the *paśu* requires release from the *pāśa*.

That technical vocabulary is central to the entire religious system.

And here the goddesses are the power through which the bonds are broken.

### Pravighaṭana - प्रविघटन

Breaking apart, disintegration, disruption, destruction.

From *pra-* \+ *vi-* \+ √*ghaṭ*, in the relevant verbal formation.

The compound describes the dismantling of the bonds.

### Paṭu - पटु

Skilful, sharp, adept, capable.

The divine power is described as skilful at destroying bondage.

Not merely forceful.

Discriminating.

Effective.

The philosophical association with knowledge is immediate.

### Jñānaśūla - ज्ञानशूल

The spear or trident of knowledge.

**jñāna** - knowledge.

**śūla** - pointed weapon, spear, trident.

And now comes:

**ūrdhvadhārāḥ**.

The upper or upward-directed edges, blades, or cutting points.

**ūrdhva** - upward, elevated.

**dhārā** - edge, blade, stream, or current, depending on context.

Here the weapon imagery favours the sense of sharp cutting edges.

The goddesses are identified with the piercing, liberating edge of knowledge.

And this is exactly what Abhinavagupta's commentary is intended to do.

Cut through misrecognition.

Not merely add another layer of terminology.

Not merely generate another school of metaphysical allegiance.

Actually remove the bonds of confusion.

### Cetovākkāyam

**चेतोवाक्कायम् - cetovākkāyam**

Mind, speech, and body.

**cetas** - mind, thought, inner cognition.

**vāk** - speech.

**kāya** - body.

The compound brings together the three domains of human expression.

And this is especially appropriate.

The entire *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* has investigated consciousness through speech.

But now the philosopher offers not merely words.

He brings his mind, speech, and body into the relationship with the goddesses.

Everything through which the finite subject acts and expresses itself.

The whole person.

### Protam - प्रोतम्

Threaded, woven, strung through.

A past passive participle connected with *pra-* \+ √*ve*, to weave, in the relevant formation.

The word creates a beautiful image.

The mind, speech, and body are threaded into the goddesses.

Not transported through space in a literal mechanical sense.

But understood as belonging within their conscious power.

The imagery is one of inseparability.

The finite person's capacities are woven into the divine manifestation.

### Yuṣmāsu - युष्मासु

“In you.”

Locative plural of the second-person pronoun.

The goddesses are the referent.

The locative reinforces the image of being threaded within them.

The divine powers are not merely externally associated with the person.

They are the ground in which the person's modes of action become intelligible.

### Drāk - द्राक्

Immediately, swiftly, at once.

An adverb.

The urgency is unmistakable.

The poet is not requesting a vague future favour.

He asks for grace now.

### Prasāda - प्रसाद

Grace, favour, clarity, tranquillity, gracious disposition.

The word is associated with *pra-* \+ √*sad*, to settle or become clear, in the relevant lexical history.

In religious contexts, it frequently denotes divine favour.

Here the prayer concerns the goddesses' immediate grace.

### Prasahya - प्रसह्य

Forcefully, powerfully, irresistibly.

An adverbial absolutive associated with *pra-* \+ √*sah*.

It gives the closing request its unusual energy.

He doesn't merely ask politely for a little spiritual encouragement.

He asks the goddesses to come powerfully into his heart.

The metaphor is emphatic.

The divine powers that destroy bondage are being invoked with urgency.

### The metre: Sragdharā

Now we have a genuinely magnificent metrical form.

**Sragdharā** uses twenty-one syllables per pāda.

Its sequence of gaṇas is:

**ma - ra - bha - na - ya - ya - ya**.

In heavy-light notation:

**- - - &#124; - ⏑ - &#124; - ⏑ ⏑ &#124; ⏑ ⏑ ⏑ &#124; ⏑ - - &#124; ⏑ - - &#124; ⏑ - -**

All four pādas of verse 20 yield twenty-one syllables and match this pattern in the GRETIL reading.

The caesurae divide the pāda into three groups of seven syllables.

This is not merely a general identification by appearance.

The syllable count and gaṇa pattern have been checked.

And the choice of metre is extraordinarily fitting.

A long, rolling, elaborate devotional verse for the culmination of the commentary's prayer to the goddesses.

After the clipped insult of verse 17 and the compact reflective verses 18-19, the Sanskrit opens into a magnificent twenty-one-syllable rhythm.

The philosophical exposition has become a hymn.

And then comes the final verse.

Where Abhinavagupta apologises.


## XI. Verse 21 - “Dear Goddesses, Please Forgive My Mouth. It Has Been Running for Several Hundred Pages.”

KSTS p. 282 · Gnoli p. 286 · Vasantatilakā

**Devanāgarī**

> व्याख्यादिकर्मपरिपाटिपदे नियुक्तो
> युष्माभिरस्मि गुरुभावमनुप्रविश्य ।
> वाक्चित्तचापलमिदं मम तेन देव्यस्-
> तच्चक्रचारुचतुरस्थितयः क्षमध्वम् ॥२१॥

**IAST**

> *vyākhyādikarmaparipāṭipade niyukto*
> *yuṣmābhir asmi gurubhāvam anupraviśya &#124;*
> *vākcittacāpalam idaṃ mama tena devyas*
> *taccakracāracaturasthitayaḥ kṣamadhvam &#124;&#124;*

The GRETIL transcription writes *devyās* at the line boundary; the KSTS Devanāgarī points toward **devyas**, the form expected from *devyaḥ* before the following consonant. I have shown the grammatically expected short-vowel form rather than silently carrying over the electronic vowel difference.

**My translation:**

“Appointed by you to the station of performing commentarial and related work, having entered the role of a teacher, I have therefore displayed this restlessness of speech and mind. O goddesses, so skilfully established in the lovely movements of that divine circle, forgive me.”

There it is.

The final authentic numbered verse.

After all the extraordinary doctrines, Abhinavagupta asks forgiveness for the restlessness of his own words and mind.

And the irony is almost unbearably beautiful.

He has spent hundreds of pages explaining the divine nature of language.

The stages of speech.

The emergence of phonemes.

The relation between sound and meaning.

The ultimate reality of *vimarśa*.

The freedom of Śakti to manifest through differentiated expression.

And now he looks back at the intellectual and verbal activity involved in producing the work.

And calls it:

**वाक्चित्तचापलम् - vākcittacāpalam.**

The fickleness, agitation, or restlessness of speech and mind.

The greatest exponent of divine speech concludes by apologising for speaking.

What a remarkable literary gesture.

### Vyākhyādikarma

**व्याख्यादिकर्म - vyākhyādikarma**

The work of explanation and related activities.

**vyākhyā** - explanation, interpretation, commentary.

**ādi** - and so forth.

**karman** - action, work, task.

The compound refers to the work of composing and transmitting explanations.

It is exactly what Abhinavagupta has been doing.

### Paripāṭipada

**परिपाटिपद - paripāṭipada**

The station or position within a sequence of prescribed activities.

**paripāṭi** - order, arrangement, sequence, system of performance.

**pada** - place, position, station.

The commentator describes himself as appointed to a particular role.

Not as an independent creator of the supreme reality.

Not as a person whose philosophical skill somehow exists outside divine consciousness.

He is performing a function within the manifestation of Śakti.

### Niyuktaḥ

**नियुक्तः - niyuktaḥ**

Appointed, assigned, commissioned.

Past passive participle from *ni-* \+ √*yuj*.

The masculine nominative singular describes Abhinavagupta.

The agent appears in the instrumental plural:

**yuṣmābhiḥ** - by you.

The goddesses are the ones to whom the verse attributes his appointment.

### Gurubhāvam anupraviśya

**गुरुभावमनुप्रविश्य**

*gurubhāvam anupraviśya.*

“Having entered the condition or role of a guru.”

**guru** - teacher.

**bhāva** - state, condition, nature, role.

**anupraviśya** - having entered into, absolutive of *anu-* \+ *pra-* \+ √*viś*.

There is a small interpretive ambiguity in the relationship between the agent of appointment and the subject of the absolutive.

Grammatically, the natural reading is that Abhinavagupta has entered the role of teacher and has been appointed by the goddesses.

The theological context also allows the idea that the goddesses act through the manifested guru-function.

But the verse does not need to be forced into one elaborate account of divine possession.

Its immediate statement is that the author understands his teaching role as arising through their power.

### Vākcittacāpalam

**वाक्चित्तचापलम्**

*vāk-citta-cāpalam.*

This is the key compound.

**vāk** - speech.

**citta** - mind.

**cāpala** - restlessness, wavering, impulsiveness, activity lacking complete steadiness.

The neuter accusative singular is the object of the implied request for forgiveness.

And Abhinavagupta calls it **mama**.

Mine.

His own speech and mind.

This is astonishingly personal.

The man who has just criticised bad teachers is not pretending to stand outside every possible human limitation.

He ends by acknowledging the activity of his own mental and verbal faculties.

### Tena - तेन

“Therefore,” “because of that.”

Instrumental singular of the demonstrative pronoun, functioning causally.

Because he has been appointed to the task of explanation, the activity of speech and thought has unfolded.

And he asks the goddesses to forgive it.

### Taccakracāracaturasthitayaḥ

**तच्चक्रचारुचतुरस्थितयः**

*tac-cakra-cāru-catura-sthitayaḥ.*

This is another elaborate divine address.

**tat** - that.

**cakra** - circle, divine assemblage.

**cāru** - beautiful, pleasing, graceful.

**catura** - skilful, clever, adept.

**sthiti** - state, condition, standing, establishment.

The feminine plural form addresses the goddesses as those skilfully established in the beautiful movement or structure of that divine circle.

It evokes the **Hṛccakra**, the Heart-circle that has been central to the concluding meditation.

The goddesses are not merely external figures.

They are the powers through which consciousness's differentiated manifestation is understood.

### Kṣamadhvam - क्षमध्वम्

Now the final verb.

**क्षमध्वम् - kṣamadhvam.**

“Forgive!”

Second-person plural imperative middle, from √*kṣam*, to endure, bear, forgive, tolerate.

The final word of Abhinavagupta's twenty-one-verse authorial epilogue is therefore an imperative addressed to the goddesses.

**Forgive.**

And the grammar is beautiful.

He is not making a claim about his own supremacy.

He is requesting grace.

The final finite verb of the epilogue is a plea.

Not a declaration that his explanations are beyond criticism.

Not an assertion that the work is perfect.

A request for forgiveness.

### The metre

We return to **Vasantatilakā**, the fourteen-syllable metre used at the beginning of the authorial epilogue.

Each of the four pādas has fourteen syllables and the expected heavy-light sequence:

**- - ⏑ - ⏑ ⏑ ⏑ - ⏑ ⏑ - ⏑ - -**

For example:

*vyā-khyā-di-kar-ma-pa-ri-pā-ṭi-pa-de-ni-yuk-to.*

And the final:

*tac-ca-kra-cā-ru-ca-tu-ra-sthi-ta-yaḥ-kṣa-madh-vam.*

The last word **kṣamadhvam** contains the conjunct **क्ष - kṣ** and the cluster **ध्व - dhva**.

The consonants do not each form separate syllables.

The final syllable is heavy.

The metre closes cleanly.

After the expansive twenty-one-syllable Sragdharā of verse 20, the poem returns to the fourteen-syllable form with which Abhinavagupta began describing his own composition.

The structural return is fitting.

The epilogue began with the author as the bee at Śiva's feet.

It ends with the author asking the goddesses for forgiveness.

Both are devotional acts of humility.

And between them he has constructed one of the most remarkable pieces of autobiographical and philosophical reflection in Sanskrit literature.


## XII. What comes after verse 21? The colophon, the mysterious extra stanza, and the nineteenth-century editor's own verses

The twenty-first authorial verse ends with:

**क्षमध्वम् - kṣamadhvam.**

“Forgive.”

Then the printed text gives a completion formula.

### The completion statement

**समाप्तमिदं परात्रिंशिकातत्त्वविवरणम् ॥**

*samāptam idaṃ parātriṃśikātattvavivaraṇam.*

“This explanation of the reality taught in the Parātriṃśikā is complete.”

The grammar is straightforward.

**samāptam** - completed, finished.

**idam** - this.

**parātriṃśikā** - the root tantra's title.

**tattva** - reality, essential principle.

**vivaraṇa** - explanation, exposition, elucidation.

The final two words are neuter singular and agree with *idam*.

This marks the end of the work.

But immediately afterward comes another Sanskrit verse.

And its status deserves considerable attention.

### The additional summative stanza

**शतैरेकोनविंशत्या त्रिंशिकेयं विवेचिता ।**

*śatair ekonaviṃśatyā triṃśikeyaṃ vivecitā.*

**सर्वेषु त्रिकशास्त्रेषु ग्रन्थीर्निर्दलयिष्यति ॥**

*sarveṣu trikaśāstreṣu granthīr nirdalayiṣyati.*

**My translation:**

“This Triṃśikā has been explained in nineteen hundreds; it will break apart the knots in all the Trika scriptures.”

Now there are several important questions.

Who composed this stanza?

What exactly does “nineteen hundreds” count?

And does it belong to Abhinavagupta's own closing poetry?

The first thing we can say securely is that it appears **after the completion statement** in the KSTS and GRETIL presentations.

It is not numbered 22 within the sequence of twenty-one authorial epilogue verses.

But its transmission is more complicated than merely calling it a modern editorial addition.

### A manuscript witness preserves the stanza

A modern manuscript-catalogue discussion by Mrinal Kaul, published in *Verità e bellezza: Essays in Honour of Raffaele Torella* \(2022), transcribes the ending of a composite manuscript containing the *Parātriṃśikāvivaraṇa*, another short text, and the *Tantrālokaviveka*.

In that manuscript's colophon, the additional stanza appears after the completion formula and an attribution to Abhinavagupta.

So the stanza is **not simply a verse invented by the 1918 editor**. It has manuscript transmission.

That is an important correction to the easy assumption that anything after *samāptam* must be modern.

But the manuscript's inclusion also does not conclusively establish Abhinavagupta's authorship of the stanza.

A colophon may contain scribal, transmissional, or earlier editorial material.

Its proximity to an authorial attribution does not settle the question by itself.

The responsible conclusion is therefore:

**The stanza belongs to the transmitted closing material of the work, but its authorial status remains less certain than that of the preceding twenty-one numbered verses.**

The manuscript evidence is relevant; the exact reading of that manuscript has been checked through the published catalogue transcription rather than a direct image of the folio.

### The strange number: nineteen hundreds

**शतैरेकोनविंशत्या**

*śatair ekonaviṃśatyā.*

Let's examine it.

**śata** - hundred.

**śataiḥ** - by hundreds, instrumental plural.

**ekonaviṃśati** - nineteen, literally one less than twenty.

The instrumental singular is **ekonaviṃśatyā**.

Thus:

“Nineteen hundreds.”

That is **1,900**.

But the verse doesn't explicitly identify the unit in the manner we might expect from a modern bibliographical statement.

It may refer to a traditional measure of the commentary's textual extent, rather than to 1,900 individually numbered verses.

We should not announce that the *Parātriṃśikāvivaraṇa* contains 1,900 root-tantra verses.

It plainly does not.

Nor does it contain 1,900 numbered authorial verses.

The numeral belongs to the transmitted description of the exposition's extent.

Its precise material counting convention should be investigated separately.

### Granthi - ग्रन्थि

Now the wonderful final claim.

**ग्रन्थीन् - granthīn**

Knots, accusative plural.

The printed sandhi before the following word yields **granthīr**.

The root noun *granthi* means knot, binding, or something tied together.

And in the context of Trika scripture it also evokes textual difficulties.

Knots in interpretation.

Difficult passages.

Conceptual obscurities.

Contradictory-seeming statements.

The commentary promises to untie or break through them.

### Nirdalayiṣyati

**निर्दलयिष्यति - nirdalayiṣyati**

A future form meaning “will crush, break, tear apart, split open.”

From the verbal formation associated with *nir-* \+ *dal*, to split or break.

This is a strong verb.

The text does not merely promise to make the scriptures somewhat easier to read.

It promises to break apart their knots.

And there is an obvious resonance with the **jñānatriśūla**.

The trident of knowledge cuts the bonds of the limited subject.

The explanatory work breaks the knots of difficult scripture.

Both metaphors describe the transformative power of understanding.

### The metre

The additional stanza is **anuṣṭubh**, with four eight-syllable pādas.

| Pāda | Syllables |
|--------|--------|
| *śatair ekonaviṃśatyā* | śa-tair-e-ko-na-viṃ-śa-tyā |
| *triṃśikeyaṃ vivecitā* | triṃ-śi-ke-yaṃ-vi-ve-ci-tā |
| *sarveṣu trikaśāstreṣu* | sar-ve-ṣu-tri-ka-śās-tre-ṣu |
| *granthīr nirdalayiṣyati* | gran-thīr-nir-da-la-yi-ṣya-ti |

Each has eight syllables.

The stanza is metrically coherent.

That supports its integrity as a verse, though it doesn't resolve its authorship.

### The concluding attribution

GRETIL then gives:

**कृतिरभिनवगुप्तस्य**

*kṛtir abhinavaguptasya.*

“A composition of Abhinavagupta.”

The 1918 KSTS presentation has a longer attribution identifying the work with the venerable Abhinavaguptācārya.

Those statements belong to the colophon tradition.

They identify the work's author.

They do not necessarily assign the additional unnumbered summarising stanza to the same author.

And this is why the boundary must be kept visible.

### The modern editorial verses

After the colophon, the printed 1918 edition contains **three further verses** concerning the preservation of learning, the Kashmiri royal administration's support for recovering important texts, and the scholarly editorial work undertaken under the direction of **Mukunda Rāma Śāstrī**.

These are part of the publication's modern scholarly apparatus.

The reference to the editor's own role makes the distinction unmistakable.

They are **not an additional three-verse continuation composed by Abhinavagupta in eleventh-century Kashmir**.

They should not be included in the count of twenty-one authorial epilogue verses.

Nor should they be presented as evidence of Abhinavagupta's historical experiences.

They are historically valuable for understanding the 1918 edition.

But they belong to a different period and authorial context.

And so the final textual structure is:

| Layer | Content | Status |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Root tantra | The verses of the *Parātriṃśikā*, with differing editorial numbering | Scriptural source |
| Running *Vivaraṇa* | Abhinavagupta's philosophical exposition, ending *iti śivam* | Authorial commentary |
| Epilogue verses 1-21 | The commentator's devotional, biographical, and authorial poetry | Numbered authorial epilogue |
| Completion formula and extra stanza | *Samāptam idaṃ…* and the nineteen-hundreds stanza | Transmitted colophon material; stanza's authorship not certain |
| Additional 1918 verses | Praise of the scholarly edition and its institutional setting | Modern editorial material |

This is the structure a reliable edition, translation, or audiobook should preserve.

Now the text is genuinely finished.

And we can finally ask what the entire work has taught us.


# XIII. The Grand Finale: What the Fuck Is Consciousness, and Why Did Abhinavagupta Spend Hundreds of Pages Explaining It?

We began with a question from the Goddess.

What is the unsurpassable reality?

How can it be recognised?

How does its recognition confer immediate liberation?

What is the supreme Heart?

And what is the extraordinary power through which the whole universe appears?

Abhinavagupta's answer was not a simple exhortation to stop thinking.

He constructed a vast philosophical explanation.

A theory of the Self.

A theory of manifestation.

A theory of language.

A theory of agency.

A theory of cognition.

A theory of bondage.

A theory of liberation.

And a way of interpreting tantric ritual through the universal consciousness of Śiva.

Let's now assemble the entire argument without losing the distinctions that make it rigorous.

## 1. The supreme reality isn't an object

The central claim is that the ultimate conscious reality cannot be known in the same manner as an ordinary external object.

A pot is seen.

A sound is heard.

A thought is recognised.

A memory appears.

Each has a determinate form.

But the awareness in which these forms are manifest cannot simply be treated as another external item standing beside them.

Why?

Because any object presented to consciousness already presupposes its manifestness.

If the ultimate subject were simply another object, we could ask what makes that object manifest.

And if another subject were required, the explanatory process would continue.

Pratyabhijñā's answer is that consciousness is **intrinsically self-manifesting**.

It does not need a second conscious subject to illuminate it from outside.

This is the significance of *prakāśa*.

Luminosity.

Manifestness.

The fact that experience is revealed.

But Abhinavagupta refuses to reduce the supreme to a merely passive light.

Because a passive light would not account for its own awareness.

Thus he places enormous emphasis upon *vimarśa*.

Reflexive self-apprehension.

Consciousness's awareness of itself.

And the ultimate reality is their inseparable unity.

Now, that is a philosophical theory.

It is not a conclusion automatically established by contemporary neuroscience.

Nor does the mere occurrence of experience prove every part of the Trika metaphysics.

But the theory addresses a genuine philosophical problem.

What is the relation between the conscious subject and the objects it knows?

And can the ultimate subject be reduced to the status of one more object?

Abhinavagupta's answer is no.

## 2. Consciousness is free

Now the next problem.

How can one consciousness manifest an entire differentiated universe?

If the supreme were simply an inert, undifferentiated substance, it would be difficult to explain the emergence of variety without introducing something external to it.

But if an external second principle were ultimately independent, the supreme would no longer be absolutely universal.

Abhinavagupta's solution is **svātantrya**.

Freedom.

The power of consciousness to manifest.

And this freedom is inseparable from Śakti.

The divine power of manifestation.

This is why the system is not simply a philosophy of passive awareness.

It is a philosophy of conscious agency.

The supreme is not an observer trapped before a pre-existing external world.

It is the ground through which manifestation becomes possible.

And the entire field of differentiated experience belongs to its expressive power.

### Does that make every human ego omnipotent?

No.

This is one of the most important distinctions we've made throughout the series.

The empirical personality is a contracted manifestation.

It possesses restricted knowledge and agency.

The supreme consciousness is universal.

The two are not ultimate independent substances.

But the finite ego cannot simply appropriate the unrestricted capacities of the supreme by declaring itself divine.

That would confuse the differentiated person with their ultimate ground.

The doctrine is not a licence for delusions of personal omnipotence.

It is an account of what the limited subject ultimately is.

## 3. The alphabet is not just a collection of sounds

And now the extraordinary linguistic turn.

Abhinavagupta investigates how the powers of consciousness become articulated through the phonemic structure of Sanskrit.

The vowel-powers.

The consonants.

The Mātṛkā.

The Mālinī.

The stages of speech.

The relation between word and meaning.

The movement from undifferentiated expression toward articulated language.

He treats phonemes as manifestations of divine powers.

This is not the ordinary historical-linguistic claim that Sanskrit letters physically generated the cosmos.

It is a tantric metaphysical interpretation of language.

The phonemic order becomes a map of the differentiating power of consciousness.

And this is why the commentary pays such close attention to the alphabet.

The sounds are not merely decorative syllables attached to a philosophical system.

They are part of its explanation of manifestation.

### Why the grammar matters

Because the actual Sanskrit words carry the argument.

A long vowel can change a form.

A case ending can change the syntactic relation.

A negative prefix can reverse a metaphysical proposition.

A compound can contain multiple interpretive possibilities.

And the difference between a quoted root verse and a commentator's explanation can be decisive.

If we misread the Sanskrit, we can misrepresent the philosophy.

That's why a rigorous reading requires both linguistic precision and philosophical interpretation.

One without the other is insufficient.

## 4. The Heart is not simply an organ

The *hṛdaya* is one of the work's central terms.

It can refer to the heart in ordinary language.

But in the theological context, the supreme Heart signifies the essential centre or ground of consciousness.

Not a little physical object somewhere inside the chest.

Not a region of measurable space.

Not merely an emotional state.

The Heart is the conscious reality in which the entire differentiated field becomes manifest.

And the supreme Heart-mantra **सौः - sauḥ** expresses that reality through its phonemic structure.

*Sa.*

*Au.*

*Visarga.*

The world of manifestation.

The powers of differentiation.

The supreme emitting power.

And the return of differentiation to its undivided ground.

This is why the mantra is treated as extraordinarily important.

But the real potency of the mantra is not simply the physical utterance.

It is the conscious reality being expressed.

Which brings us to the next decisive distinction.

## 5. Reciting a mantra and recognising its reality are not identical

A person can pronounce a mantra correctly.

They can memorise its syllables.

They can understand its traditional associations.

They can analyse its grammar.

They can count the prescribed repetitions.

But none of those actions alone proves the complete realisation described by Abhinavagupta.

The distinction is between the external or conceptual possession of a formula and the recognition of the reality it expresses.

This is why the text repeatedly returns to *mantravīrya*.

The potency of the mantra.

Not merely its phonetic existence.

The highest form of mantra is the consciousness that makes the entire act of utterance and understanding possible.

And so the culminating statement of Part XXXIII was:

**स्वस्वरूपपरिज्ञानं मन्त्रोऽयं पारमार्थिकः ।**

*svasvarūpaparijñānaṃ mantro 'yaṃ pāramārthikaḥ.*

“Complete recognition of one's own nature is the ultimate mantra.”

That is the essence of the entire theory.

The audible mantra can be a means.

The actual recognition is its highest meaning.

## 6. Liberation is recognition, not the production of a new Self

Now we reach *pratyabhijñā*.

Recognition.

A person can fail to recognise something already present.

The event of recognition occurs in time.

But it does not create the identity being recognised.

That analogy illuminates Abhinavagupta's account of liberation.

The supreme consciousness is not absent before recognition.

The limited subject misrecognises their own ultimate nature.

And liberation concerns the overcoming of that misrecognition.

This is why the highest initiation can be identified with knowledge itself.

Not because the ritual is automatically useless.

But because the ritual cannot manufacture the supreme consciousness.

It may help disclose it.

The supreme reality is not produced by the disclosure.

### The astonishing consequence

Someone who has never seen the maṇḍala may nevertheless be a true initiate.

If they possess genuine recognition of the supreme Heart.

This is not a declaration that every private mystical claim is valid.

The condition is *tattvataḥ vetti*.

Knowing according to reality.

And Abhinavagupta relates that to *śaktipāta*, divine grace.

The highest recognition is not a social credential.

Nor a possession granted by institutional membership alone.

Its defining condition is the actual disclosure of the supreme Self.

## 7. The world need not disappear

Here is perhaps the most radical practical implication.

If the world is a manifestation of consciousness, then liberation need not require the annihilation of every differentiated appearance.

Thought can continue.

Sensation can continue.

Memory can continue.

Language can continue.

The body can continue.

The person can act.

And the underlying identity may remain recognised.

That is the meaning of **jīvanmukti**, liberation while living, in the relevant passage.

The liberated person is not required to become inert.

Nor to suppress all thought.

Nor to eliminate every sensory event.

The transformation concerns the understanding of their ultimate nature.

This is why Abhinavagupta can speak of continuous recognition amid ordinary activity.

And why the inserted Heart-verses include:

Meditating.

Remembering.

Reflecting.

Acting.

Anywhere.

The supreme Heart is not limited to a particular contemplative posture.

## 8. Ritual becomes an expression of recognition

And this was the great surprise of Part XXXIII.

After explaining that supreme knowledge can confer liberation without the conventional fire offering, Abhinavagupta interprets an elaborate ritual sequence.

The five bodily placements.

The twenty-sevenfold divine powers.

The ten directions.

The claps.

The water.

The flowers.

The seat.

The Goddess.

The fire.

And the offering of oneself.

Why?

Because the ritual can be understood through the same nondual framework.

The worshipper is a manifestation of consciousness.

The act of worship is a manifestation of Śakti.

The deity worshipped belongs to the same ultimate reality.

And the offering is not ultimately a foreign substance transported into an independent second god.

The highest offering is the relinquishment of the misrecognition that the subject is absolutely separate.

This is why **ātmanivedana**, self-offering, becomes so important.

The point is not destroying the body.

It is recognising that the whole structure of the act belongs to consciousness.

## 9. The guru is not beyond criticism

Now return to verses 16-17.

The epilogue's sharpest intellectual and ethical warning.

Teachers can mislead.

A title does not establish understanding.

A lineage does not guarantee competence.

A reputation can become a source of bondage.

And even Abhinavagupta admits having been deceived by self-styled teachers.

This is extraordinary because the wider system places great importance on initiation and the guru.

Yet the author does not simply make every claimant to spiritual authority immune from criticism.

He insists upon genuine knowledge.

The teacher's proper function is liberating.

A teacher who obscures rather than clarifies may invert that function.

And Abhinavagupta's response is **jñānatriśūla**.

The trident of knowledge.

A text intended to cut the bonds of confusion.

This may be the strongest statement in the entire work about why philological and philosophical precision matters.

Not merely to be correct.

But because misunderstanding can have real consequences for people seeking guidance.

## 10. The author writes out of compassion

And finally we come back to the human beings.

Abhinavagupta's father.

His family lineage.

His brother.

The scholars around him.

Śauri.

Vatsalikā.

Karṇa.

Rāmadeva.

The household and intellectual community.

He did not write the work as an entirely impersonal abstraction.

He understood it as a means of helping people.

He wanted the doubtful to achieve clarity.

He wanted the already convinced to become firmly established.

He wanted serious readers to continue investigating.

And he wished that the work might become a *mārgapradarśana*, a showing of the path, for everyone's attainment of Śiva.

That is an extraordinarily generous purpose.

And the closing verses identify it with the compassion of Śambhu.

The ultimate teacher's heart is moved to lift up those seeking refuge.

The human commentator participates in that function.

The authorial work is therefore both intellectual and devotional.

An act of explanation.

An act of service.

An act of reflection.

And, in his own theological understanding, an expression of grace.


# XIV. The final practical question: What does recognition actually change?

Strip away the historical vocabulary for a moment.

Not the philosophy.

Not its precision.

Just the unfamiliarity of the Sanskrit.

What is the question Abhinavagupta is asking?

You experience a world.

You perceive objects.

You have sensations.

You think.

You remember.

You desire.

You act.

You identify with a body and a psychological history.

And ordinarily, you understand yourself as a small conscious subject situated within a much larger world of things.

Now ask:

**What makes the entire field of experience manifest?**

A sound occurs.

It is known.

A sensation appears.

It is known.

A thought arises.

It is known.

The sense of being a particular person appears.

It too is known.

The ordinary subject-object distinction remains useful.

But Abhinavagupta asks whether the ultimate conscious reality can be reduced to one more object appearing within that distinction.

His answer is no.

The conscious ground is not one differentiated object alongside all the others.

And in the Trika metaphysics, that ground is universal Śiva-consciousness.

The world is its manifestation.

The limited subject is its contraction.

And recognition is the disclosure of that identity.

### Does this mean every experience is proof of spiritual attainment?

No.

A surge of energy.

A sense of light.

Unusual concentration.

Bliss.

Stillness.

A period with few thoughts.

A powerful feeling of unity.

These experiences can be meaningful, but their occurrence does not by itself establish the complete realisation described by Abhinavagupta.

His criterion is deeper.

The actual understanding of the nature of the subject.

The relation between awareness and its manifestations.

And the overcoming of the misrecognition that constituted bondage.

That is why he does not reduce liberation to the reproduction of an extraordinary state.

The Heart is not a state that appears and then disappears.

Its conscious reality is understood as the ground of the appearance and disappearance of states.

### The difference between an experience and recognition

Suppose a person has a profound meditation.

The sense of ordinary identity becomes quiet.

There is a feeling of great openness.

Then the experience ends.

The person attempts to reproduce it.

Again and again.

They become increasingly preoccupied with the state.

Was it as intense as before?

How long did it last?

Did the light appear?

Was the body as still?

Has the bliss returned?

Abhinavagupta's teaching invites a different enquiry.

What was the nature of the awareness in which both the extraordinary state and its absence were manifest?

Is consciousness fundamentally absent when the special state ends?

Or has the form of the experience changed while the fact of awareness remains?

The question is not simply how to preserve an unusual psychological configuration.

It is what the configuration reveals about the nature of experiencing.

That distinction can completely change the orientation of contemplative practice.

### What about ordinary life?

Now the final practical implication.

Suppose you are eating.

Working.

Walking.

Studying.

Speaking.

Listening.

Writing.

A memory appears.

A thought occurs.

An emotion arises.

The body moves.

Nothing particularly spectacular happens.

Can recognition be relevant here?

According to the final Heart-verses, yes.

Because the supreme consciousness is not confined to extraordinary states.

Ordinary experience remains within the scope of manifestation.

And the enquiry can be directed toward its conscious ground.

Not by denying what is happening.

Not by pretending practical consequences don't matter.

Not by treating every impulse as a divine command.

But by examining the nature of the aware subject and the field of experience.

That is the movement from merely knowing a philosophical doctrine to the possibility of living recognition.

### And what should we do with the difficult parts of the tradition?

The extraordinary claims.

The siddhis.

The esoteric initiations.

The ritual prescriptions.

The accounts of divine beings.

The promises of omniscience.

We should distinguish the levels.

Some are historical reports.

Some are theological assertions.

Some are philosophical arguments.

Some are contemplative instructions.

Some are ritual prescriptions.

Some are poetic metaphors.

And some are extraordinary empirical claims that would require evidence beyond a traditional text to establish as factual capacities.

A rigorous study does not need to flatten them into one category.

Nor does it need to dismiss the entire work because it contains claims modern science has not verified.

The philosophical arguments can be examined on their merits.

The Sanskrit can be translated accurately.

The historical tradition can be reconstructed critically.

The ritual systems can be studied as religious practices.

And the extraordinary assertions can be identified without pretending that scriptural authority alone verifies them.

That is the kind of scholarship Abhinavagupta's own demands for discernment make especially appropriate.


# XV. The final word: The bee, the trident, and the goddesses

We began with a philosopher.

We ended with a person.

A Kashmiri scholar from a family whose ancestors had come from elsewhere.

A son of Cukhala.

A brother of Manorathagupta.

An intellectual heir of Somānanda and Utpaladeva.

A member of a circle of devoted and learned people.

A teacher who had himself been misled by teachers.

A writer who wanted to protect others from the same confusion.

A thinker who believed that genuine knowledge could sever the bonds of conditioned existence.

And a devotee who concluded by asking the goddesses to forgive the activity of his own speech and mind.

Now consider the beauty of that ending.

He could have ended with an assertion of philosophical victory.

He could have declared that no one had ever understood the supreme doctrine as completely as he had.

He could have announced that the *Vivaraṇa* was the final and unsurpassable explanation of all reality.

But instead he asks for forgiveness.

Why?

Because, in his own metaphysics, every act of speech is a differentiated manifestation.

Language can express the supreme.

But no finite expression can exhaust the inexhaustible conscious ground.

A sentence ends.

The supreme Heart does not end with it.

A commentary can be completed.

The reality it investigates is not thereby brought into existence.

A philosopher can become silent.

Consciousness does not become absent because that philosopher has stopped speaking.

And so the final act is devotional.

The man who examined every phoneme offers his own words back to the goddesses.

The man who investigated the nature of the subject offers his own mind.

The man who forged the trident of knowledge acknowledges the divine powers that make knowledge possible.

And the man who tried to free others from false teachers understands his own work as an expression of grace rather than a claim to independent supremacy.

That is the extraordinary coherence of the ending.

The metaphysics is not abandoned.

It is lived within the authorial act.

### One last image

Imagine Abhinavagupta finishing the manuscript.

The final verse written.

The Sanskrit complete.

The difficult explanations over.

The names of his companions recorded.

His father's lineage preserved.

His criticism of ignorant teachers delivered.

The trident of knowledge forged.

The goddesses invoked.

And the final request made:

**क्षमध्वम्।**

*Kṣamadhvam.*

Forgive.

Now the room is quiet.

No more compounds.

No more phonemes.

No more metrical calculations.

No more arguments about the nature of the knowing subject.

The manuscript lies before him.

A finite composition.

Written with a particular hand.

In a particular place.

At a particular historical moment.

And yet its subject was the reality that Abhinavagupta believed to be beyond spatial and temporal limitation.

The supreme Heart.

The self-manifesting consciousness through which every form of experience appears.

The awareness in which the act of writing was known.

The awareness in which the words were understood.

The awareness in which the reader's questions would eventually arise.

And the awareness in which the entire philosophical enquiry would unfold.

The commentary has ended.

The questions can continue.

Because a finite explanation is not the same thing as the infinite reality it attempts to make intelligible.

And the most profound result of the work is not that the reader has acquired a collection of impressive Sanskrit doctrines.

It is the possibility of examining the very nature of the consciousness through which those doctrines are understood.

That is the point.

Not merely to know about Śiva.

Not merely to repeat a mantra.

Not merely to perform an initiation.

Not merely to attain a special state.

But to recognise what, in Abhinavagupta's system, is the ultimate reality of the subject and the world.

And if that recognition occurs, the entire universe is no longer understood as a second reality fundamentally alien to consciousness.

It becomes intelligible as the expression of Śiva's freedom.

The sound.

The thought.

The body.

The world.

The teacher.

The student.

The words.

The silence.

All manifest within the same conscious ground.

And then the greatest philosophical joke of all becomes apparent.

We have spent thirty-six instalments investigating the supreme consciousness.

We've travelled through the Sanskrit alphabet.

We've examined the thirty-six tattvas.

We've studied initiation, ritual, death, liberation, mantra, desire, and the cosmic powers of Śakti.

We've confronted the relation between the limited self and the universal subject.

We've watched Abhinavagupta dismantle the authority of incompetent teachers with an intellectual trident.

And now, finally, we reach the end.

Only to discover that the very reality we were studying was, according to the philosophy, already the condition of our studying it.

The question was manifested within consciousness.

The enquiry occurred within consciousness.

The understanding appeared within consciousness.

And the supreme consciousness was not manufactured by any of them.

So the last question is no longer:

“Where can I find Śiva?”

It is:

**What is the ultimate nature of the awareness through which the very question appears?**

That is the enquiry Abhinavagupta leaves us with.

And that is why the book ends not with a new object to acquire, but with the recognition of the reality in which all acquiring and seeking occur.

The universe has not become Śiva.

The ultimate nature of the universe has been recognised as Śiva-consciousness.

And the final Sanskrit word is no longer something we need to invent.

Abhinavagupta has already given it to us.

**क्षमध्वम् - kṣamadhvam.**

Forgive.

The book is finished.

The Heart, he says, is ever manifest.

**इति शिवम्। - iti śivam.**
