## 1. The instructions begin - पूर्वे परेषामपरे परे

The commentary reaches Kashmir Series p. 157 and announces that a method is being written down for those skilled in revealing the many distinctions among the *kula-puruṣas* and *kula-śaktis*.

Then comes the verse beginning:

## पूर्वे परेषामपरे परे पृष्ठवदेव च।

*Pūrve pareṣām apare pare pṛṣṭhavad eva ca.*

This is an attested instruction quoted within Abhinavagupta's commentary, not a new numbered verse of the root Parātrīśikā.

The following line identifies the procedure as a rule belonging to **Mātṛkā**. The next verse applies the corresponding principle to the differentiated generative matrix of **Mālinī**.

Now, what the hell does the opening mean?

The compact wording describes relations of placement.

Former sounds.

Subsequent sounds.

What goes before.

What goes behind.

And the relative order in which they're arranged.

The passage is interpreted in terms of the **kula-puruṣas**, the vowel or seed powers, and the **kula-śaktis**, the consonantal or matrix powers.

The ordinary Mātṛkā arrangement preserves a familiar organization.

Mālinī permits a differentiated configuration.

And the rule shows how arrangements can be generated by placing those powers in relation to one another.

### The grammar of pūrva

**पूर्व - pūrva**

Former, previous, earlier, eastern.

Now, look at:

**पूर्वे - pūrve**

This can have different grammatical analyses depending on the sentence, including a masculine nominative dual or plural-related uses in particular constructions, and a locative singular of an *-a* stem.

Here, in the verse's compressed arrangement language, we're dealing with relative positions among groups.

Then:

**परेषाम् - pareṣām**

Of the others, of the subsequent ones.

This is a genitive plural form of *para*.

The ending **-eṣām** is extraordinarily useful to recognize.

Compare:

**सर्वेषाम् - sarveṣām**

Of all.

**परेषाम् - pareṣām**

Of others.

The genitive relation is explicit.

Now:

**पृष्ठवत् - pṛṣṭhavat**

In the manner of the back, or as placed behind.

From:

**पृष्ठ - pṛṣṭha**

Back, rear.

And the suffix:

**-वत् - -vat**

Having the quality of, resembling, or in the manner of.

And finally:

**एव - eva**

Indeed, just, precisely.

So the instruction concerns positions of phonemic elements relative to others.

Now here's something I want to be careful about.

This is not a fully explicit modern pseudocode algorithm.

The Sanskrit is compressed and assumes familiarity with the system.

Its purpose is to describe a rule for generating or organizing phonemic configurations.

We shouldn't pretend that the line alone gives every parameter required for a computer program capable of reconstructing every historical Mālinī mantra.

Nevertheless, its essential operation is clear.

**The vowels and consonants can be arranged through controlled relationships of precedence and succession.**

And that's the beginning of the combinatorial system.

### The crucial distinction

This is not an ordinary Sanskrit sandhi rule.

Sandhi tells you what happens when sounds meet under specific phonological conditions.

For example:

**a + i → e**

But the present instruction concerns the **placement of whole phonemic powers within a sacred arrangement**.

That is a different kind of operation.

It belongs to the Tantric system of constructing *varṇa*-sequences.

And the metaphysical interpretation comes from the relation between Śiva, Śakti and their differentiated manifestation.

So we've got three distinct activities:

Ordinary phonetics.

Phonemic arrangement.

Tantric interpretation.

Don't conflate them.

Abhinavagupta isn't merely inventing complicated spelling rules to annoy future students.

He is describing how the powers of consciousness may be ordered and expressed.

Although future students are, unfortunately, very much being annoyed.


## 2. Mātṛkā and Mālinī - Two ways of arranging one divine family

The quoted instruction next invokes:

**मातृकाया विधिः - mātṛkāyā vidhiḥ**

The rule of Mātṛkā.

Now the grammar.

**मातृका - mātṛkā**

The alphabetic Mother-power.

**मातृकायाः - mātṛkāyāḥ**

Of Mātṛkā, genitive singular.

When followed by *vidhiḥ* in connected speech, the final visarga is subject to sandhi.

Then:

**विधिः - vidhiḥ**

Rule, method, procedure, ordinance.

The noun is masculine nominative singular.

Now the contrast.

The Mātṛkā arrangement is associated with the familiar ordering of phonemic powers.

The *kula-puruṣas*-understood here as vowel or seed powers-can be placed relative to the *kula-śaktis*, consonantal powers, while the regular precedence of the series is preserved.

Mālinī is different.

She is:

**भिन्नयोनि - bhinnayoni**

The differentiated generative matrix.

We've learned this word.

**भिन्न - bhinna**

Separated, divided, differently configured.

**योनि - yoni**

Womb, origin, matrix.

So *bhinnayoni* expresses the altered arrangement of the generative phonemic powers.

And this is precisely why Mālinī begins with:

**न - na**

Rather than:

**अ - a**.

The divine alphabet has been rearranged without requiring that its phonemic powers become wholly independent substances.

### The theological distinction

Think of Śiva and Śakti.

Śiva is the luminous ground of universal consciousness.

Śakti is its expressive freedom.

Their distinction is meaningful in the teaching, but not ultimately substantial.

Now the same principle applies to the sacred phonemes.

The vowels and consonants may be distinguished.

Their order can be changed.

Their relationship can be expressed through different arrangements.

Yet the ultimate reality of their manifestation is not divided.

Now here's the important thing.

Abhinavagupta's argument is **not**:

"Every random arrangement of letters must have the same sacred significance."

He has just spent several pages explaining that specific arrangements have particular traditional functions.

His argument is that the freedom of consciousness permits different valid configurations of its powers.

Those configurations remain subject to their own rules.

And this is why the distinction between Mātṛkā and Mālinī matters.

It's the difference between the ordinary ordered presentation of the alphabetic Mother and the differently configured manifestation of the Goddess.

### The beautiful name Uttaramālinī

The verse also names:

**उत्तरमालिनी - uttaramālinī**

Uttaramālinī, Mālinī in a further, higher or subsequent form.

Break it down:

**उत्तर - uttara**

Further, later, higher, subsequent.

**मालिनी - mālinī**

The garlanded Goddess.

Now remember the opening of the root Tantra.

**अनुत्तर - anuttara**

The unsurpassable.

The same *uttara* family is appearing again.

But don't assume every use of *uttara* has the full sixteenfold philosophical significance Abhinavagupta previously extracted from *anuttara*.

Here it functions within a name for a differentiated mode of Mālinī.

The context determines the intended meaning.

### An important variant

The Kashmir Series Devanāgarī has a reading equivalent to *śāktādyasaṅkhyā*, while the GRETIL transcription gives *śāktā hy asaṃkhyā*.

The latter can be understood as emphasizing that the Śākta configurations are innumerable.

Both witnesses connect the differentiated Mālinī power with a vast, effectively inexhaustible range of possible forms.

But the exact Sanskrit segmentation differs.

So we'll preserve the interpretive point without presenting a single unqualified reading as though no textual variation existed.

And now Abhinavagupta moves from the alphabet's order to the small dot that will generate an enormous amount of mathematical trouble.

**Bindu.**


## 3. The bindu instructions - Put the dot where it belongs

The next instruction begins with the placement of phonemic units above and below one another, in different numerical or differentiating positions.

The Devanāgarī witness reads:

**ऊर्ध्वाधो विनिविष्टेषु**

*Ūrdhvādho viniviṣṭeṣu.*

Placed above and below.

There is a textual variation worth keeping: a parallel presentation reads *adho 'dho*, "below, below," whereas the Kashmir Series and GRETIL transcriptions have *ūrdhvādho*, "above and below." That affects the literal spatial instruction, so it isn't something to silently harmonize.

The following lines bring in **bindu**, preceding and final positions, numerical placement, and the repeated formation of new orders. ; \]

### First: Ūrdhva and Adhas

**ऊर्ध्व - ūrdhva**

Above, upward, higher.

**अधस् - adhas**

Below, downward.

When combined in this expression, the terms describe vertical relationships within an arrangement.

And:

**विनिविष्ट - viniviṣṭa**

Placed, inserted, entered into.

Related to *vi-ni-√viś*, to enter or be placed.

The locative plural:

**विनिविष्टेषु - viniviṣṭeṣu**

In those which have been placed.

Now look at the ending:

**-ेषु - -eṣu**

Locative plural for masculine or neuter *-a* stems.

You've already learned *-eṣām* as a genitive plural.

Now learn the difference:

| Form | Case | Meaning |
|--------|--------|--------|
| **विनिविष्टेषाम्** - *viniviṣṭeṣām* | Genitive plural | Of the placed ones |
| **विनिविष्टेषु** - *viniviṣṭeṣu* | Locative plural | Among or in the placed ones |

This is a perfect example of why Sanskrit morphology matters.

One vowel difference changes the grammatical relationship.

### Second: Bhedasaṃkhyā

**भेदसङ्ख्या - bhedasaṃkhyā**

A number, enumeration or count associated with differentiation.

**भेद - bheda**

Difference, division.

From √*bhid*, to split.

**सङ्ख्या - saṃkhyā**

Number, reckoning, enumeration.

Related to *saṃ-√khyā*, to count or make known.

Now the passage is moving beyond the mere order of sounds.

It concerns a **reckoning of possible differentiated configurations**.

And that is why *saṃkhyā* matters.

Remember that the Sanskrit word **Sāṃkhya**, the philosophical school, belongs to this same vocabulary of enumeration.

But don't confuse the present combinatorial instructions with Sāṃkhya metaphysics merely because the word occurs.

Here the immediate concern is a method of arranging and counting sacred phonemic powers.

### Third: Bindu

# ं

bindu - point, dot

# अं

aṃ

Now we need to distinguish three things.

First, the ordinary Sanskrit word:

**बिन्दु - bindu**

A drop, point or dot.

Second, the written sign:

**ं**

The *anusvāra* mark in Devanāgarī.

Third, the Tantric interpretation of *bindu* as concentrated phonemic or divine power.

These aren't simply identical categories.

The sign has an orthographic and phonological function.

The Tantric doctrine interprets the point as a manifestation of power.

And the present instructions use the dot in the construction or notation of phonemic arrangements.

### The wording of the instruction

The original includes an expression meaning that a single *bindu* is involved in one case, while in other cases its placement is related to preceding and final positions.

And then comes:

**विनिवेश्यैकतः क्षिपेत्**

*Viniveśyaikataḥ kṣipet.*

Having placed it, one should cast or put it in one position.

Let's dismantle the verbs.

**विनिवेश्य - viniveśya**

Having placed or inserted.

An absolutive formation associated with *vi-ni-√viś* in its causative form.

And:

**क्षिपेत् - kṣipet**

One should throw, cast or place.

Third-person singular optative of √**kṣip**, to throw or cast.

Now look at the conjunct:

**क्ष = क् + ष**

Again.

That little conjunct is everywhere in Tantric Sanskrit.

The full expression:

*viniveśya + ekataḥ → viniveśyaikataḥ*

Uses:

**a + e → ai**

The final *a* combines with the following *e*.

Excellent.

Now we can see the grammatical sequence:

Having placed the relevant mark or numerical element, one should cast or arrange it according to the specified position.

This is a procedural instruction, not a purely descriptive philosophical sentence.

### Why the dot isn't just decoration

Within the passage's sacred combinatorics, *bindu* participates in distinguishing configurations.

The dot can modify how a phonemic unit or group is represented.

That produces further possibilities beyond simply choosing which letter comes first.

And when the same underlying phonemic powers can be arranged in different sequences, with additional differentiated markings, the number of possible expressions expands.

Now, here's where I want to be precise.

**The text does not provide a modern fully specified computational recurrence with a clearly defined alphabet size, every permitted positional constraint, and a final numerical output derived from these lines alone.**

We can identify the operations it names:

- Ordering and reordering sounds.
- Arranging them above or below.
- Distinguishing vowel and consonant groupings.
- Employing *bindu* in different positional relationships.
- Continuing the construction through further combinations.

But to turn these directions into a program that generates every historically authorized mantra, we would need to settle the variant readings and the ritual conventions assumed by the text.

Otherwise we'd be writing an invented algorithm and claiming it came from Abhinavagupta.

And the man is sufficiently formidable without our putting bad mathematics into his mouth.

### The word ānantya

**आनन्त्य - ānantya**

Endlessness, infinity, unlimitedness.

From:

**अनन्त - ananta**

Without end.

And the abstract derivative formation:

**आनन्त्य - ānantya**

The condition of being endless.

The last line of the numerical instruction identifies the procedure in relation to knowing or exhibiting this *ānantya* of the *kula-śaktis*.

Now the philosophical point is beautiful.

A determinate inventory of phonemic powers can manifest through an extraordinary diversity of arrangements.

And those arrangements symbolize the freedom of Śakti.

But the freedom is not identical with the mere arithmetic.

Mathematical permutations illustrate one aspect of differentiated possibility.

The theological claim concerns the supreme conscious power through which the whole manifestation arises.

And Abhinavagupta is about to connect these arrangements with a far more startling number.

Thirty-five million.


## 5. Thirty-five million Mantras - And they're not what you think

Now we reach the passage that will be extremely easy to misunderstand if we read it carelessly.

The scripture invokes:

**मन्त्राणां कोटयस्तिस्रः सार्धाः**

*Mantrāṇāṃ koṭayas tisraḥ sārdhāḥ.*

Three and a half crores of *Mantras*.

That's thirty-five million.

But here's the catch.

**The word Mantra here refers to a class of divine conscious beings, not simply thirty-five million different syllable strings.**

Abhinavagupta makes that distinction explicit in the commentary.

He discusses the *Mantras*, their relation to Śiva's grace, and the condition of the *Mantramaheśvaras*. ; \]

Now let's dismantle the numerical Sanskrit properly.

### Koṭi - कोटि

**कोटि - koṭi**

Among its meanings, a crore: ten million in Indian numerical usage.

Singular:

**कोटिः - koṭiḥ**

Plural:

**कोटयः - koṭayaḥ**

Now look carefully at the written change.

*koṭi* becomes *koṭay-* in the nominative plural.

So:

**कोटयः - koṭayaḥ**

Crores.

This is a feminine *-i* stem.

Now:

### Tisraḥ - तिस्रः

**तिस्रः - tisraḥ**

Three, feminine nominative plural.

Why feminine?

Because *koṭi* is feminine.

The numeral agrees with it.

Compare:

**त्रयः - trayaḥ**

Three, masculine.

**तिस्रः - tisraḥ**

Three, feminine.

**त्रीणि - trīṇi**

Three, neuter.

This is an extremely useful grammatical distinction.

Sanskrit numerals can vary according to grammatical gender.

And the form in the scripture isn't arbitrary.

### Sārdhāḥ - सार्धाः

**सार्ध - sārdha**

With a half, plus a half.

Here the feminine plural form **sārdhāḥ** modifies the three crores.

So:

**tisraḥ koṭayaḥ sārdhāḥ**

Three crores and an additional half crore.

Now:

- Three crores = thirty million.
- Half a crore = five million.
- Total = thirty-five million.

The number is a traditional cosmological count in the quoted scripture.

It is not a count of all permutations mathematically generated from the preceding phonemic instruction.

That distinction is absolutely essential.

### The full theological context

The scripture describes these *Mantras* as appointed by Śiva.

And as having extended grace to multitudes of limited beings before attaining the **Anāmaya** state.

Now we need to learn:

**अनामय - anāmaya**

Free from disease, trouble or affliction.

From:

**अन् - an-**

Negative prefix.

**आमय - āmaya**

Illness, affliction.

Thus:

*anāmaya*.

In the present cosmological context, **Anāmayapada** denotes an exalted spiritual state.

Not merely the condition of having no fever.

And:

**पद - pada**

Station, state, position, place, word or foot, depending on context.

Here:

*anāmaya + pada → anāmayapada*.

The state beyond affliction.

And now we reach the real distinction between *Mantras* and *Mantramaheśvaras*.


## 6. Mantra versus Mantramaheśvara - Sanskrit creates an ontological trap

First, the word:

**मन्त्र - mantra**

In ordinary religious usage, a sacred formula or utterance.

But in Śaiva cosmology it can also designate a **class of conscious divine agents**.

These are not interchangeable senses.

And that matters enormously here.

Then:

**मन्त्रमहेश्वर - mantramaheśvara**

A great lord of mantra.

Break it apart.

**मन्त्र - mantra**

Mantra.

**महा - mahā**

Great.

**ईश्वर - īśvara**

Lord, ruler, sovereign.

The combination:

*mahā + īśvara → maheśvara*.

Because:

**ā + ī → e**

Thus:

**मन्त्र + महेश्वर → मन्त्रमहेश्वर**

The great lord associated with the mantra class.

Now the theology.

In the scriptural hierarchy, *Mantras* and *Mantramaheśvaras* are distinguished by their level of divine realization and agency.

The commentary describes the *Mantramaheśvaras* as beings whose prior sphere of activity is complete and who reach a higher condition of repose.

These are not just ordinary practitioners who learned an especially impressive Sanskrit syllable.

They belong to a technical cosmological classification.

### Niradhikārībhūta - निरधिकारीभूत

Now here's the fascinating expression.

**निरधिकारीभूत - niradhikārībhūta**

Having become free from, or no longer possessing, a particular office or authority.

Let's break it down.

**निर् - nir-**

Without, away from, out of.

**अधिकार - adhikāra**

Authority, qualification, office, assigned function.

**अधिकारी - adhikārī**

One possessing authority or an assigned role.

**भूत - bhūta**

Become.

Thus:

*niradhikārībhūta*.

One who has become free from an assigned function or authority.

Now picture a divine being whose role has been to administer a particular field of mantraic activity.

The function is fulfilled.

The being attains a higher condition.

And the commentary says that mantras are not to be extracted from that state in the same manner.

Not because the being has become spiritually inferior.

Precisely the opposite.

The particular differentiated operative role has been surpassed.

### The cosmic retirement department

Imagine the divine bureaucracy.

A Mantra-being has spent an inconceivably long period performing an assigned function within the universe.

Guiding beings.

Operating through a particular manifestation of Śakti.

Then the function is completed.

The being attains an exalted state.

And a student arrives.

"Excuse me, sir, can you provide another operational mantra?"

"My brother, I've retired from that entire ontological department."

"But I have a ritual appointment on Thursday."

"Speak to someone still assigned to that level."

Ridiculous, yes.

But the analogy reveals the philosophical distinction.

A being may possess a more exalted spiritual condition while no longer functioning as an operative source within a particular lower-level system.

**Higher realization and continued performance of a differentiated cosmic role are not the same thing.**

And the commentary explicitly says that obtaining a mantra from the beings in question would be fruitless in the relevant ritual sense.

That's a claim within the Tantric cosmology, not a demonstrated empirical fact about actual supernatural beings.

### The subtle distinction in the Sanskrit

Abhinavagupta is not simply saying:

"There are thirty-five million different mantras, and when somebody recites all of them, they become a Mantramaheśvara."

Nor is he saying that the preceding permutation algorithm produces exactly thirty-five million outputs.

He's citing a theological account of divine agents.

The number belongs to that account.

The combinatorial procedure belongs to the arrangement of phonemic powers.

The two subjects are related by the doctrine of mantra and Śakti, but their numerical meanings must not be collapsed.

And now he returns to the actual written arrangement.


## 7. The Kali Yuga instruction - From न to फ

The next cited line begins:

**अभिन्नयोनिमध्ये तु नादिफान्तं कलौ युगे।**

*Abhinnayonimadhye tu nādiphāntaṃ kalau yuge.*

The instruction concerns an arrangement from **na** through **pha**, situated within the *abhinnayoni* context, in the Kali age.

Now this is a beautiful opportunity for Sanskrit analysis.

### Abhinnayoni

**अभिन्नयोनि - abhinnayoni**

The undivided generative matrix.

Compare:

**भिन्नयोनि - bhinnayoni**

Differentiated matrix.

Now add:

**मध्य - madhya**

Middle, centre.

So:

**अभिन्नयोनिमध्ये - abhinnayonimadhye**

Within the undivided matrix.

The ending **-e** is locative singular of the compound's final *-a* element, *madhya*.

Now here's the subtle point.

The text refers to an arrangement beginning with *na* and ending with *pha*, while invoking the undivided matrix.

We should not mechanically equate every appearance of *abhinnayoni* with "write the ordinary alphabet exactly as usual."

The preceding discussion has made the relations among Mātṛkā, Mālinī and their permutations much more sophisticated than that.

The verse is specifying a particular placement convention within a transmitted ritual framework.

### Nādiphāntam

**नादिफान्तम् - nādiphāntam**

Now look at the apparent spelling.

At first glance, you might read:

*nāda + something*

And think of:

**नाद - nāda**

Subtle sound, resonance.

But here the intended compound concerns:

**न - na**

The phoneme *na*.

**आदि - ādi**

Beginning.

**फ - pha**

The phoneme *pha*.

**अन्त - anta**

End.

So:

*na + ādi + pha + anta*

Through vowel sandhi:

**ना - nā** at the start.

And:

**फान्त - phānta** at the end.

Thus:

**नादिफान्तम् - nādiphāntam**

Beginning with *na* and ending with *pha*.

This is a perfect example of why context matters in Sanskrit.

A continuous sound sequence can resemble a different lexical expression.

But the grammar and surrounding discussion tell us how to segment it.

The reference is to the phonemic ordering.

Not necessarily a new philosophical statement about Nāda itself.

### Kalau yuge

**कलौ - kalau**

In Kali.

Locative singular of *Kali*.

**युगे - yuge**

In the age.

Locative singular of *yuga*.

Thus:

**कलौ युगे - kalau yuge**

In the Kali age.

Now notice the endings.

A masculine *-i* stem can form the locative singular in **-au**, as in *kalau*.

A neuter *-a* stem forms the locative in **-e**, as in *yuge*.

Both are locatives.

Different declensional classes.

And the verse specifies a temporal-religious context for the arrangement.

Not an independently verified historical prediction about the objective efficacy of a particular alphabet during Kali Yuga.

### Why this passage belongs here

The commentary has just explained why not every exalted mantra-being remains an operative source of mantra.

Now the scriptural instruction provides a specific arrangement relevant to a particular transmitted age and practice.

The juxtaposition is deliberate.

The divine phonemic system possesses a range of possible combinations.

But ritual prescription is not simply unlimited free improvisation.

The relevant system distinguishes divine levels, operative functions, appropriate sources and authorized arrangements.

And now, astonishingly, Abhinavagupta moves away from the numerical machinery.

He returns to **Parā**, the supreme Goddess.

Because all these arrangements have to be grounded in something.

And we're about to discover that the same Goddess can be letter, word, mantra, object of purification, and instrument of liberation.

This is where the chapter gets properly philosophical again.


## 8. The great turn - Parā becomes Mālinī in Madhyamā

Now we arrive at the central prose of p. 158.

Abhinavagupta describes the venerable supreme Goddess as manifesting through the differentiated modes of speech, and especially as **Mālinī** in the principal operation of **Madhyamā**.

The important Sanskrit expressions are:

**परा भट्टारिका - parā bhaṭṭārikā**

The supreme venerable Goddess.

**मध्यमा - madhyamā**

Intermediate speech.

**मालिनीरूपा - mālinīrūpā**

Having the form of Mālinī.

And:

**अनन्ता - anantā**

Endless, infinite.

Now that's the key.

The preceding combinatorial exercise demonstrated a vast possible diversity of phonemic configurations.

Abhinavagupta brings that diversity back to the Goddess herself.

Her manifestation as Mālinī is an expression of her own infinite power.

She hasn't become a different independent substance merely because she is represented through a differentiated ordering.

### Padabhedaśālinī

**पदभेदशालिनी - padabhedaśālinī**

Endowed with differentiations of expressions, states or words.

Break it down.

**पद - pada**

Word, expression, state, position.

**भेद - bheda**

Difference, distinction.

**शालिनी - śālinī**

Possessing, endowed with, characterized by.

So:

*padabhedaśālinī*.

The Goddess characterized by differentiated expressions.

Now the philosophical point.

Mālinī's many arrangements are not accidental variations imposed upon an otherwise inert substance.

They are manifestations of Śakti's own expressive freedom.

But this freedom isn't limited to the outward written phonemes.

The expression proceeds through the levels of speech.

And here Abhinavagupta invokes an astonishing threefold structure:

**वर्ण - varṇa**

Letter, phoneme.

**पद - pada**

Word, meaningful unit or expression.

**मन्त्र - mantra**

Mantra.

Now, the online explanatory translation sometimes rearranges these English labels into familiar linguistic categories.

But the actual Sanskrit compound presents:

**वर्ण-पद-मन्त्र**

*Varṇa-pada-mantra.*

Letter.

Word.

Mantra.

And the point is that the Goddess possesses the character of these differentiated expressive forms through the predominance of three aspects within her own nature.

### The threefold expression

**वर्ण**

varṇa

The individual phonemic power.

**पद**

pada

The meaningful articulated unit or expression.

**मन्त्र**

mantra

Sacred expressive power in its particular configuration.

Now here's the central question.

How do these forms relate to the deeper levels of consciousness?

If they're genuinely differentiated, doesn't that imply separation?

If they're not separated, why do they appear as different at all?

Abhinavagupta's answer is that their distinction belongs to manifestation while their ultimate reality remains grounded in Parā.

And now he introduces a completely new perspective.

**Purification.**

The one consciousness is being considered in three different roles:

What is purified.

The means by which purification occurs.

And the power that purifies.

And this, my brother in Bhairava, is where the entire alphabet becomes a spiritual laundry operation.


## 9. The three great words - Śodhya, Śodhana, Śodhaka

These are among the most useful Sanskrit words in this entire installment.

Learn them together.

**शोध्य**

śodhya

That which is to be purified.

**शोधन**

śodhana

Purification, or the means/process of purifying.

**शोधक**

śodhaka

The purifier, agent or power that purifies.

All three belong to the root-family:

**√शुध् - √śudh**

To become pure.

Through the causative:

**शोधयति - śodhayati**

One purifies.

Now look at the different formations.

### Śodhya

**शोध्य - śodhya**

A gerundive or adjectival formation expressing what is to be purified.

The ending **-ya** helps form the notion of something requiring or suitable for the action.

### Śodhana

**शोधन - śodhana**

Purifying, purification, or the instrument/process of purification.

The **-ana** formation expresses the action or relevant means.

### Śodhaka

**शोधक - śodhaka**

The purifier.

The **-aka** formation can express an agent or instrument.

Now here's a wonderfully simple analogy.

Imagine a dirty cloth.

The cloth is what is to be cleaned.

The washing procedure is purification.

The agent or operative means accomplishing the cleaning is the purifier.

Three different roles.

One activity.

And now Abhinavagupta applies that structure to the threefold manifestation of Śakti.

Except there's a problem.

**All three roles belong to the same universal consciousness.**

So who is cleaning whom?

Is Śiva washing himself?

Is the Goddess both the detergent and the cloth?

And if the purifier is ultimately Śiva, how can the purifier himself require purification?

Well.

Welcome to the next few pages.


## 10. Paśyantī - The bonds exist before they're fully articulated

Abhinavagupta begins with the *Paśyantī* aspect.

He explains that the bonds arising there are extremely subtle.

They have not yet appeared in the fully articulated form familiar from outward differentiated experience.

The relevant phrase is:

**पश्यन्त्यंशोल्लसन्तो हि पाशाः सूक्ष्मा एव शोध्या भवन्ति।**

*Paśyanty-aṃśollasanto hi pāśāḥ sūkṣmā eva śodhyā bhavanti.*

The bonds emerging within the Paśyantī aspect are subtle and are to be purified.

Now let's dismantle it.

### Paśyantī

**पश्यन्ती - paśyantī**

The seeing or visionary level of speech.

From √**paś**, to see.

A feminine present participial formation.

Remember the ordinary form:

**पश्यति - paśyati**

He or she sees.

And:

**पश्यन्ती - paśyantī**

She who sees.

But in the speech doctrine, the term is technical.

It describes a level of expression prior to the completely articulated word-object distinction.

### Aṃśa

**अंश - aṃśa**

Part, portion, aspect.

Thus:

**पश्यन्त्यंश - paśyantyaṃśa**

The Paśyantī aspect.

The combination:

*paśyantī + aṃśa → paśyantyaṃśa*

Uses the familiar glide:

**ī + a → ya**

And now:

### Ullasantaḥ

**उल्लसन्तः - ullasantaḥ**

Shining forth, emerging, becoming manifest.

From *ud-* and √*las*, to shine, play, move or manifest.

A masculine nominative plural participial form.

Agreeing with:

**पाशाः - pāśāḥ**

Bonds, fetters, nooses.

The same *pāśa* that gives us:

**पशु - paśu**

The bound individual in Śaiva terminology, although the two words are distinct lexical forms.

Now:

**सूक्ष्माः - sūkṣmāḥ**

Subtle.

Masculine nominative plural.

Again agreeing with *pāśāḥ*.

And:

**शोध्याः - śodhyāḥ**

To be purified.

Same agreement.

So the sentence's grammar is beautifully coordinated.

The bonds are subtle.

They emerge in Paśyantī.

They are objects of purification.

### The extraordinary implication

Bondage doesn't begin only when an explicit thought appears:

"I am a limited individual."

The differentiation underlying that identification can already be implicit at subtler levels.

Now, this isn't a claim that Paśyantī is necessarily psychologically unhealthy.

Nor that every subtle movement of consciousness is itself bondage.

The relevant bonds are those differentiated conditions that manifest limitation.

Abhinavagupta's point is that such limitation can be **latent before becoming fully articulate**.

### The thought before the thought

Imagine a moment of irritation.

Before you formulate:

"That person has insulted me, and I must spend the next six hours constructing a legal prosecution in my head,"

there may be an initial disturbance.

A movement.

A sense of threat.

An orientation of the mind.

Only afterward does the fully formed story arrive.

The analogy helps illustrate how a differentiated condition can begin subtly and later become conceptually explicit.

But Paśyantī isn't simply an ancient Sanskrit synonym for a modern preconscious neural process.

It's a metaphysical level of speech and cognition.

The point is structural:

**An implicit differentiation can be present before it becomes an explicit verbal construction.**

And therefore the purification of bondage must reach beyond the removal of merely external behaviours or spoken thoughts.

Which brings us to Madhyamā.


## 11. Madhyamā - The instrument of purification emerges

The next part of the commentary is extremely important.

Abhinavagupta distinguishes the subtler bonds of Paśyantī from the more manifest expressive power operating through **Madhyamā**.

And he says that in Madhyamā the relevant manifestation takes on the character of a **means of purification**.

This makes sense when you think about the relationship between implicit experience and conceptual articulation.

### Madhyamā

**मध्यमा - madhyamā**

The intermediate level.

From:

**मध्य - madhya**

Middle.

The form is feminine because we're referring to the Goddess's level of speech.

Now remember the hierarchy.

Parā is undivided supreme speech.

Paśyantī is the more unified emerging apprehension.

Madhyamā makes the relation between signifier and signified increasingly distinct.

Vaikharī manifests articulated outward speech.

So Madhyamā occupies a critical position.

The differentiation is not yet simply an external sound.

But it has become sufficiently articulated to participate in the process of understanding and transformation.

### The Sanskrit: Vijṛmbhā

**विजृम्भा - vijṛmbhā**

Expansion, opening, blossoming, unfolding.

Related to *vi-* and √*jṛmbh*, to open wide, expand or yawn.

Yes.

There is a verbal relation to yawning.

Which makes the image surprisingly concrete.

Something that was contracted begins to open.

And:

**उदित - udita**

Arisen, emerged.

From *ud-* and √*i*, to go or arise.

The repeated expression *uditodita* emphasizes increasingly evident manifestation.

The Śākta power expands into more explicit forms.

And these forms can operate as instruments of purification.

### Why articulation can be liberating

Imagine someone experiencing a persistent disturbance.

They can't explain it.

They only experience a vague contraction.

Then through careful examination, they begin distinguishing its components.

A sensation.

A memory.

A belief.

A particular identification.

The undifferentiated discomfort becomes more intelligible.

Now the act of articulation doesn't automatically remove the condition.

But it can make examination possible.

And that's a useful analogy for the function of Madhyamā.

The more differentiated power of speech can disclose the subtle condition that previously remained implicit.

And this is why Abhinavagupta associates it with purification.

The power of expression can become the power through which misrecognition is removed.

### The extremely important qualification

**The same power that manifests differentiation can also reveal its real nature.**

That is the Trika thesis.

Speech can bind.

Conceptual construction can reinforce the limited "I."

But speech can also instruct.

Distinctions can clarify.

Reflection can disclose their common conscious ground.

Thus the means of purification does not need to arrive from a completely separate metaphysical universe.

It can arise through the very power that manifests differentiation.

And now Abhinavagupta gives an astonishingly domestic analogy.

A dirty cloth.


## 12. The cosmic laundry - Remove the hidden dirt

Abhinavagupta compares the purification of subtle bonds with removing dirt hidden within a piece of cloth.

The relevant Sanskrit includes:

**अन्तर्लीनपटमलापसरणे**

*Antarlīnapaṭamalāpasaraṇe.*

When the dirt lying inside a cloth is removed.

Now let's dismantle that.

**अन्तर् - antar**

Within.

**लीन - līna**

Absorbed, concealed, merged, hidden.

From √*lī*, to cling, dissolve or become absorbed.

So:

**अन्तर्लीन - antarlīna**

Hidden within.

Then:

**पट - paṭa**

Cloth, fabric.

Then:

**मल - mala**

Dirt, impurity, stain.

And:

**अपसरण - apasaraṇa**

Moving away, removal, departure.

Related to *apa-* and √*sṛ*, to move.

Thus:

**antarlīna-paṭa-mala-apasaraṇa**

The removal of dirt hidden within a cloth.

A glorious Sanskrit compound describing the sort of thing that happens when you finally wash a shirt properly.

### The analogy

Suppose a cloth has been soaked in dirt.

You can wipe the visible surface.

Perhaps it looks cleaner.

But dirt remains embedded in the fibres.

You wash it more thoroughly.

The internal dirt is removed.

And the outward appearance changes as a result.

The visible dirt wasn't completely independent of the underlying condition.

Now consider the relationship between subtle and gross bondage.

The outward manifestation of a condition may depend upon something more subtle.

If the underlying condition is transformed, the outward expression can change.

The commentary uses precisely this sort of relation to explain how the purification associated with Paśyantī and Madhyamā can be connected.

### The danger of a superficial reading

Now, don't reduce this to:

"All negative thoughts are dirt, and you should scrub your mind until nothing happens."

That would be a rather crude interpretation.

The *mala* of Trika is not simply ordinary unpleasant emotion.

The central issue is the **limitation of consciousness through misrecognition**.

And the deeper purification concerns the recognition of the real nature of the experiencer.

Removing one particular thought may be useful.

But it doesn't necessarily remove the underlying assumption:

"I am merely this incomplete, separate individual."

Similarly, the cloth analogy concerns different levels of obstruction.

Not the moral condemnation of ordinary mental activity.

And Abhinavagupta is still very much interested in the metaphysical relation between the levels of speech.

Because next comes an objection.

A very sensible objection.

If Vaikharī is the external level of speech, how can it already be present within the subtler levels?

Surely the external word has to be produced first?

Well.

Abhinavagupta has an answer.

And it involves a toddler learning to talk.


## 13. Abhinavagupta becomes a developmental linguist

This is one of my favourite moments in the whole commentary.

Because after all the divine alphabet permutations and cosmological mantra-beings, he suddenly asks us to consider **children learning language**.

The issue is straightforward.

How does articulated speech develop?

And does its emergence require that no relation to the subtler level existed previously?

Abhinavagupta argues that Vaikharī cannot be wholly absent from the more subtle field of expression.

Otherwise it would be difficult to explain the gradual development of articulated language.

### The child who learns to speak

He refers to children of two or three years of age.

Their organs of articulation are already evident.

Yet their proficiency continues developing.

Month by month.

Day by day.

The child acquires more refined speech.

And Abhinavagupta asks:

If the phonetic distinctions that later become explicit in Vaikharī were not in some sense present within Madhyamā, why would language development differ between a newborn, a one-month-old child and a one-year-old child?

Now, his account is a medieval philosophical argument.

It isn't a modern empirical model of child language acquisition.

But the structure of the reasoning is interesting.

**The later articulated expression must have an intelligible relation to the capacities from which it develops.**

The fully expressed word doesn't simply erupt from an absolute metaphysical void.

### Vyutpatti - व्युत्पत्ति

**व्युत्पत्ति - vyutpatti**

Proficiency, derivation, linguistic understanding or acquisition of understanding, depending on context.

The word is connected with *vi-ud-√pad*, to arise or develop, through a nominal formation.

And its locative singular is:

**व्युत्पत्तौ - vyutpattau**

In the process of becoming proficient.

Now note the ending **-au** again.

This is a feminine *-i* stem locative.

Not a dual.

Sanskrit is relentlessly reminding us not to guess grammatical number from one ending without looking at the stem.

### Māsa-anumāsa

**मास - māsa**

Month.

**अनुमास - anumāsa**

Month by month.

And:

**दिनानुदिनम् - dinānudinam**

Day by day.

These expressions emphasize gradual development.

Now here's Abhinavagupta's observation.

The child hears words.

Sees objects.

Connects the sounds with what they designate.

And develops linguistic competence.

### Śṛṇvan - शृण्वन्

**शृण्वन् - śṛṇvan**

Hearing.

Masculine nominative singular present participle from √*śru*, to hear.

And:

**पश्यन् - paśyan**

Seeing.

From √*paś*.

Now compare:

**शृणु - śṛṇu**

Listen!

That's an imperative.

**शृण्वन् - śṛṇvan**

Hearing.

That's a participle.

Different forms.

Same root-family.

And Abhinavagupta's example concerns the child hearing external sounds and seeing their referents.

The relationship between signifier and signified is gradually established.

### The important epistemological point

The child doesn't merely receive a sequence of meaningless noises and then somehow acquire language through a process entirely disconnected from conscious apprehension.

The sounds are recognized.

The objects are apprehended.

The relationships become meaningful.

Abhinavagupta locates that meaningful integration within the conscious power associated with Madhyamā.

And this lets him argue that Vaikharī, articulated external speech, is already related to the inner expressive capacity.

Now here's the key distinction.

**Vaikharī need not already be externally audible for its power to be present in a subtler condition.**

That's very different from saying an unspoken sentence is physically travelling through the air.

The distinction is between manifest articulated speech and the inner capacity that makes its manifestation possible.

And Abhinavagupta takes the point even further.


## 14. The mute person - External speech is not the whole of language

Now he invokes a person who cannot speak.

The Sanskrit term is:

**मूक - mūka**

Mute, unable to speak.

Abhinavagupta's point is that the absence of ordinarily articulated external speech does not establish the absence of all inner verbal or conscious capacity.

This is a distinction that remains philosophically important.

A person's ability to produce audible words is not identical to their entire capacity for understanding or consciousness.

But we should not treat his discussion as a comprehensive account of every modern speech or language disorder.

He's making a narrower argument about the relationship between levels of expression.

### The actual distinction

Consider someone who understands a spoken sentence but cannot produce one aloud.

The capacity for meaningful apprehension may remain even when certain outward forms of articulation are unavailable.

And therefore the inner and outer dimensions of speech cannot simply be identified.

Now think about the four levels again.

Parā.

Paśyantī.

Madhyamā.

Vaikharī.

They're related manifestations of one conscious power.

But they are distinguishable in their degree and manner of articulation.

External speech is not the sole definition of consciousness.

Nor is internal speech the sole definition of the supreme reality.

The forms are related.

Yet the supreme ground is more comprehensive.

### The Sanskrit: Sarvātmakatva returns

**सर्वात्मकत्व - sarvātmakatva**

The condition of encompassing all.

Abhinavagupta explicitly invokes the all-containing character of the Goddess's consciousness.

The differentiated level exists within the more comprehensive reality.

The more comprehensive reality is not produced by the differentiated level.

And that's why he can maintain the presence of Vaikharī within the wider expressive power of Parā.

It's the same philosophical structure we encountered with the tattvas.

Earth implies the higher principles.

But the higher principles are not reduced to Earth.

Similarly, external speech depends upon a wider conscious capacity.

But the wider capacity is not reducible to any one spoken sentence.

**Speech is another case of differentiated manifestation depending upon a more comprehensive ground.**

And now Abhinavagupta returns to the three Goddesses.

Aparā.

Parāparā.

Parā.

And this is where the purification doctrine becomes exceptionally precise.


## 15. Aparā, Parāparā and Parā - Three expressions of the purifying power

Abhinavagupta now identifies the different levels of expression with the threefold divine power.

The relevant relationships are:

**Vaikharī** in its expanded field of differentiated words and objects is associated with **Aparā**.

The inner unfolding through **Madhyamā**, and the emergence of **Paśyantī**, is associated with **Parāparā** in the relevant interpretive relation.

And the supreme nature is **Parā**.

Now, here's the important qualification.

This isn't a simple one-to-one permanent dictionary rule in which every level of speech belongs exclusively to one named goddess under every doctrinal classification.

Abhinavagupta is discussing their relation within this particular exposition of purification.

The wider theology permits different modes of triadic manifestation.

But the present structure is clear.

### Aparā

**अपरा - aparā**

The differentiated power.

Here associated with the outwardly expanded expression of Vaikharī.

The words are manifest.

The objects are differentiated.

The signifier and signified are clearly distinguished.

The full network of tattvas is involved.

### Parāparā

**परापरा - parāparā**

The intermediate power.

Associated with the subtler manifestation through Madhyamā and Paśyantī.

Here differentiation becomes apparent but has not reached the same external articulation as Vaikharī.

### Parā

**परा - parā**

The supreme power.

The undivided conscious ground.

Not merely one further phonetic sound at the top of a list.

And now the important statement.

**The purifying power is threefold because the entire manifestation of the Goddess is threefold.**

Not because there are three independent purification machines.

But because the one conscious power can manifest through distinct modalities.

### The Sanskrit: Traidham

**त्रैधम् - traidham**

Threefold, in three ways.

Related to:

**त्रि - tri**

Three.

And the adverbial formation expressing threefold division.

Abhinavagupta uses the term to describe the establishment of the purifier through three modes.

Now remember our original Trika triad.

Nara.

Śakti.

Śiva.

The three grammatical persons.

The three powers.

And now the three roles of purification.

The system is deeply recursive.

The threefold structure keeps appearing because it expresses the relationship between differentiated subjectivity, expressive power and supreme consciousness.

But here's the interesting part.

The purifier is not merely an external agent operating upon a foreign substance.

Abhinavagupta identifies the ultimate purifying reality with the universal Self.

And its expansion is threefold.

So the purifier is the very consciousness whose freedom makes the whole system possible.

Now we're ready for the final distinction.

**Who performs purification?**

And **what is actually being purified?**

These aren't quite the same question.


## 16. The purifier is the universal Self

Abhinavagupta states that the purifier is:

**विश्वात्मा - viśvātmā**

The Self of the universe.

Let's dismantle it.

**विश्व - viśva**

All, universal, the world.

**आत्मन् - ātman**

Self.

So:

*viśva + ātman → viśvātman*.

And the masculine nominative singular form:

**विश्वात्मा - viśvātmā**

The universal Self.

Now, what does that mean within the purification argument?

The ultimate purifying power isn't independent of universal consciousness.

Its capacity to remove limitation derives from the same reality that makes manifestation possible.

But the commentary doesn't simply collapse the agent, instrument and object into one undifferentiated word.

It distinguishes their functions.

And that's important.

Because if we say:

"The purifier is Śiva, the thing being purified is Śiva, and the purification is Śiva,"

we could mistakenly conclude that the distinctions are pointless.

But Abhinavagupta wants to explain how they become manifest.

The one reality can appear in three distinct roles.

And those roles have different significance within the process.

### The operative power

The relevant expression concerns the agency of the doer who has accepted contraction through his own freedom while resting in the greatness of Śakti.

Let's learn the important components.

**कर्तृ - kartṛ**

Doer, agent.

From √**kṛ**, to do or make.

The genitive singular is:

**कर्तुः - kartuḥ**

Of the doer.

Now:

**कर्तृत्व - kartṛtva**

Agency, the condition of being a doer.

That abstract suffix **-tva** again.

Then:

**स्वातन्त्र्य - svātantrya**

Freedom.

And:

**सङ्कोच - saṃkoca**

Contraction, limitation.

So the doctrine concerns consciousness freely manifesting the appearance of limited agency.

### Why the doer can become limited

This is one of the great Trika paradoxes.

If Śiva is absolutely free, how can the finite subject experience itself as constrained?

Abhinavagupta's answer is that the limited manifestation belongs to divine freedom itself.

The supreme consciousness doesn't become limited because an independent enemy has overpowered it.

Limitation is one of the possible modes of its self-manifestation.

But the limited subject does not thereby possess the unlimited freedom of Śiva within its ordinary empirical condition.

That's the crucial distinction.

**The metaphysical ground is universal freedom; the manifested individual experiences contracted agency.**

Now the agency involved in purification is a manifestation of that same power.

It is not an independent substance capable of repairing consciousness from outside.

And that's why Śakti is so central.

Her freedom manifests both the condition of limitation and the means through which it can be recognized and transcended.

Now, before somebody announces that this makes every act of the individual automatically divine perfection, remember:

The ultimate reality of an action and its practical or ethical quality are not the same classification.

You can recognize that anger occurs within consciousness without concluding that angry behaviour is therefore wise.

Similarly, the fact that contraction is a manifestation of Śakti does not mean contraction is identical to liberation in the ordinary experiential sense.

Recognition matters.

And that is precisely what the purifying relation addresses.


## 17. The thing being purified is the bound Nara

Now Abhinavagupta identifies the object of purification more explicitly.

It is the network of bonds belonging to the limited individual, **Nara**.

Now learn:

**नर - nara**

Person, man, human being; in Trika, the limited subject in the Nara-Śakti-Śiva triad.

And:

**पाशजाल - pāśajāla**

A network of bonds.

Break it down:

**पाश - pāśa**

Bond, fetter, noose.

**जाल - jāla**

Net, mesh, web.

Together:

**पाशजाल - pāśajāla**

A network of fetters.

Now that's an evocative expression.

Not a single bond.

A network.

A structure of interrelated restrictions.

The limitation of knowledge.

The limitation of agency.

The distinction between self and other.

The appropriation of experience as belonging exclusively to a finite individual.

The whole apparatus of bondage.

### The first appearance of difference

Abhinavagupta traces this network back into the subtler manifestation associated with the Paśyantī condition.

The distinction is not originally an independent material substance.

It first appears as a differentiation within conscious manifestation.

And through the subsequent powers of cognition, it becomes more explicit.

Now the philosophical sequence is important.

The bound subject isn't simply an ordinary individual who has accidentally acquired the wrong ideas.

The entire structure of limited subjectivity has a metaphysical explanation within Trika.

The differentiation of consciousness.

The emergence of limited powers.

The restriction of universal agency.

And the appearance of a world apprehended as other.

All belong to the one manifested order.

But those limitations don't establish an independent ultimate consciousness outside Śiva.

### The Sanskrit: Saṅkocaikarūpa

**सङ्कोचैकरूप - saṅkocaikarūpa**

Having contraction as its sole or defining form.

Break it down:

**सङ्कोच - saṃkoca**

Contraction.

**एक - eka**

One, sole.

**रूप - rūpa**

Form, nature.

Now the vowel sandhi:

*saṃkoca + eka → saṃkocaika*.

Because:

**a + e → ai**

Then:

*saṃkocaika + rūpa → saṃkocaikarūpa*.

Thus the expression characterizes the limited condition through contraction.

And that is the object of purification.

Not the intrinsic luminosity of consciousness.

**The limited identification is what must be transformed.**

And now the rather astonishing conclusion follows.

If the purifier, the means of purification and the object to be purified all arise within consciousness, then the entire process can be interpreted as Śiva's own self-manifestation.

And Abhinavagupta cites Somānanda to explain it.


## 18. Somānanda's astonishing prayer - Śiva bows to himself

Now comes a verse from Śivadṛṣṭi.

The text opens:

**अस्मद्रूपसमाविष्टः स्वात्मनात्मनिवारणे।**

*Asmadrūpasamāviṣṭaḥ svātmanātmanivāraṇe.*

The verse invokes Śiva, who has entered our own form, and his supreme power in removing the obstacles that belong to his own manifestation.

The remainder of the stanza is a striking prayer that Śiva should perform an act of reverence toward himself through Parā Śakti.

Now that's extraordinary.

Śiva.

Bowing to himself.

Through Śakti.

To remove his own obstruction.

My brother.

The Absolute has become simultaneously the patient, surgeon, operating theatre and medical regulator.

But this isn't just a clever piece of theological wordplay.

It expresses the Trika account of liberation with astonishing precision.

### Asmad-rūpa

**अस्मद् - asmad**

The pronominal base associated with "I" and "we."

**रूप - rūpa**

Form, nature.

Thus:

**अस्मद्रूप - asmadrūpa**

Our form, the form of the individual experiencer.

Now:

### Samāviṣṭa

**समाविष्ट - samāviṣṭa**

Entered into, completely pervaded, absorbed.

From:

*sam-ā-√viś*.

The past participial formation describes the act of entering or pervading.

And in the prayer, Śiva is described as entering our form.

But that doesn't mean Śiva has physically moved from one location to another.

The language concerns the manifestation of universal consciousness as finite subjectivity.

### The reflexive nature of the prayer

Now, why does Śiva bow to himself?

Because there is no independent second ultimate reality to which he must submit.

The act of reverence.

The power through which it occurs.

And the reality toward which it is directed.

All belong to consciousness.

But their distinction is meaningful within the manifestation.

Now that is precisely the structure of purification.

The limited subject is the apparent object.

The divine power functions as the means.

The universal Self is the ground of the whole process.

And the goal is not to create a new Śiva.

It is to remove the misrecognition through which the individual's true nature is obscured.

### The most important philosophical distinction

Imagine someone saying:

"I need to acquire divine consciousness."

The sentence suggests that consciousness is a foreign possession.

Something not presently fundamental to the experiencer.

But the Pratyabhijñā teaching says that the reality being recognized is already the ground of experience.

The obstruction concerns recognition, not the absence of the ground itself.

And this is why Somānanda's prayer is so powerful.

Śiva appears as the individual.

The individual experiences limitation.

The liberating power manifests.

And the whole process occurs within Śiva.

**The Absolute has not become metaphysically incomplete; its free manifestation includes the experience and removal of incompleteness.**

Now, this is a hard philosophical claim.

And Abhinavagupta is not pretending it can be reduced to an ordinary psychological slogan.

The entire apparatus of Trika cosmology is designed to explain how it is possible.

But the prayer captures the essential movement.

The supreme reality recognizes itself through its own power.

And now we're going to reach something even more radical.

**The purifying means itself must ultimately be left behind.**


## 19. The purifier also needs purifying

We're now approaching Kashmir Series p. 161.

And this is a natural stopping point because Abhinavagupta explicitly pushes the threefold relation toward its own transcendence.

He says that the distinction between what is purified, the means of purification and the purifier is not the ultimate state.

The higher stages involve the dissolution of these distinctions.

Now be careful.

He's not saying the distinctions never function.

We just spent pages establishing their roles.

But their functioning doesn't make them independent ultimate realities.

Thus even the purifying means, once taken as a separately existing entity, remains within differentiation.

And ultimately, differentiation must be recognized in its own conscious ground.

### The Sanskrit: Vigalanam

**विगलन - vigalana**

Dissolution, falling away, loosening, melting.

From *vi-* and √*gal*, to drip, melt or fall away.

This is such a beautiful word for the present context.

The distinctions don't need to be violently destroyed as though they were foreign substances attacking consciousness.

Their mistaken independent status dissolves through recognition.

And:

**उत्तरोत्तरम् - uttarottaram**

Successively higher, further and further, in progressive stages.

Now notice the word-family.

*Uttara*.

Further.

Higher.

Subsequent.

The same family that gave us *anuttara*.

And here Abhinavagupta discusses the progressive dissolution of the purifying distinctions.

But that is not a claim that the ultimate reality itself must wait for the sequence to finish before becoming complete.

The progressive process belongs to the differentiated manifestation.

The supreme reality is its ground.

### A very useful analogy

Imagine a ladder.

You use it to reach a particular height.

It has genuine practical value.

Without it, you may not be able to reach the destination.

But if you arrive at the destination and insist that you must continue climbing the ladder forever, you've misunderstood its function.

The ladder was the means.

Not the ultimate object of attainment.

Similarly, contemplative and ritual means can be meaningful without becoming ultimate realities independent of consciousness.

The means can help remove misrecognition.

But the completion of recognition is not the endless preservation of the means as something separate.

Now this is where Abhinavagupta introduces the most extraordinary instruction.


## 20. The final instruction - Abandon even the thing by which you abandon

He quotes a verse that begins:

**त्यज धर्ममधर्मं च**

*Tyaja dharmam adharmaṃ ca.*

Abandon both *dharma* and *adharma*.

And the conclusion is even more startling:

**येन त्यजसि तत्त्यज।**

*Yena tyajasi tat tyaja.*

Abandon that by which you abandon.

Now this is one of the most philosophically dangerous passages to read carelessly.

Because somebody can take it and announce:

"Abhinavagupta told me to abandon morality."

No.

That would be an extraordinarily poor reading of the argument.

The passage concerns the transcendence of conceptual and instrumental distinctions at the level of ultimate realization.

It is not a general instruction to behave irresponsibly.

### Tyaja - त्यज

Abandon!

Second-person singular imperative of √**tyaj**, to abandon, relinquish or leave.

Now compare:

**त्यजति - tyajati**

He or she abandons.

**त्यजसि - tyajasi**

You abandon.

**त्यज - tyaja**

Abandon!

Different personal endings.

And remember:

**येन - yena**

By which.

Instrumental singular of the relative pronoun *yad*.

Then:

**तत् - tat**

That.

So:

**येन त्यजसि तत्त्यज**

By whatever you abandon, abandon that too.

Now the philosophical structure is spectacular.

First, you abandon the objects of bondage.

Then you abandon the conceptual distinctions through which those objects are understood.

And eventually, even the separately conceived means of abandonment must be relinquished.

Because otherwise the means itself becomes another object of attachment.

### The spiritual collector strikes again

Imagine someone practicing nonattachment.

They abandon possessions.

Fine.

Then they abandon social status.

Excellent.

Then they abandon their attachment to particular spiritual experiences.

Wonderful.

And finally they develop a magnificent attachment to being the most nonattached person in the monastery.

"Unlike the rest of you, I have completely transcended attachment."

My brother.

You've simply moved the attachment into the management office.

The teaching doesn't end with identifying increasingly subtle things to relinquish.

It points toward recognition of the conscious reality in which the distinction between possessor, possession and means of relinquishment is ultimately grounded.

That's why the final line matters.

**Even the instrument of transcendence cannot become an independent ultimate object.**

### And what does this mean for mantra?

Now return to the entire alphabetic discussion.

The phonemes are meaningful.

The arrangements matter within their traditional contexts.

The mantras possess different theological functions.

The levels of speech are distinguishable.

The purifying process can operate through those distinctions.

But the completion of recognition is not simply the accumulation of more and more elaborate ritual structures.

The sacred alphabet is a means of understanding manifestation.

It isn't a second Absolute standing outside consciousness.

And that brings us all the way back to the original question of the Parātrīśikā.

How does recognition of Anuttara grant immediate Kaulika perfection?

Through recognition of the reality underlying the entire differentiated manifestation.

Not by manufacturing a new reality from the accumulation of phonemes.

And not by preserving the means as an independent object after its purpose has been fulfilled.

This is the astonishing philosophical conclusion of the passage.


## 21. The entire Part X argument in one movement

Let's gather what Abhinavagupta has done.

First, he describes methods for generating different configurations of Mātṛkā and Mālinī.

These arrangements use the relationships between vowel and consonant powers, positional placement, numerical distinctions and *bindu*.

Then he invokes scriptural testimony concerning a vast population of divine Mantra-beings.

And distinguishes those beings from ordinary mantra formulas and from the higher condition of the Mantramaheśvaras.

Next, he returns to the metaphysics of Mālinī.

The Goddess manifests as differentiated expressive power.

Within that power, letters, words and mantras arise.

The levels of speech are distinguishable, yet remain within the same universal consciousness.

And then he applies the entire structure to purification.

Paśyantī contains subtle emerging bonds.

Madhyamā participates in their clarification and removal.

Vaikharī represents the fully articulated manifestation.

Aparā, Parāparā and Parā are interpreted through their respective roles in this threefold process.

But the purifier, means of purification and object to be purified are not ultimately independent.

Their reality depends upon Śiva.

And in the final movement, even the means of purification must be recognized as a differentiated manifestation rather than an independently ultimate reality.

The culmination is extraordinary.

**The same consciousness manifests the bondage, the means, and the liberation.**

Not because bondage and liberation are practically identical.

But because neither possesses an ultimate reality independent of consciousness.

### Why this matters for the Sanskrit

And notice how much actual grammar we've encountered along the way.

The genitive plural:

**परेषाम् - pareṣām**

Of others.

The locative plural:

**विनिविष्टेषु - viniviṣṭeṣu**

Among things placed.

The optative:

**क्षिपेत् - kṣipet**

One should place.

The instrumental:

**येन - yena**

By which.

The causative:

**शोधयति - śodhayati**

One purifies.

The gerundive:

**शोध्य - śodhya**

To be purified.

And the imperative:

**त्यज - tyaja**

Abandon!

Now that is an extraordinary grammatical progression.

We began with rules for placing phonemes.

And ended with an imperative to abandon even the means by which separation is overcome.

You couldn't design a more Abhinavaguptan curriculum if you tried.

## 23. Where we stop in the original commentary

We've followed Kashmir Series pp. 157-161, from the phonemic permutation verses through the *bindu* instructions, the numerical claim concerning Mantras, the distinction between Mantra and Mantramaheśvara, the special Kali-age arrangement, the three expressive modes of Mālinī, the relationship between subtle and gross speech, and the purification doctrine.

We've reached the passage in which Abhinavagupta invokes the instruction to abandon even the means of abandonment.

**The next movement, still on p. 161, asks whether the different levels of consciousness exist simultaneously.**

And the discussion becomes exceptionally subtle.

If Vaikharī, Madhyamā and Paśyantī are all present within Parā, are they simultaneous?

But what does "simultaneous" mean when time itself is a manifestation within consciousness?

Can several cognitions occur together?

Does that imply independent simultaneous substances?

And how do memory and recognition make the relation intelligible?

Abhinavagupta is about to take another apparently simple linguistic word-

**युगपत् - yugapat**, simultaneously-

and turn it into a comprehensive argument about the nature of time and awareness.

We'll resume at that precise point in Part XI.

### The final insight

The phonemic combinations are finite as particular arrangements.

Their possible diversity is vast.

But their ground isn't simply the total number of mathematical permutations.

It's the expressive freedom of consciousness.

The purifying means are meaningful.

But they aren't ultimate independently existing substances.

And the very subject who seeks liberation is also a manifestation of the consciousness whose recognition constitutes liberation.

That is why the ultimate teaching doesn't conclude with a new object to acquire.

It concludes with the need to relinquish even the separate identification with the means.

The Goddess isn't a distant power standing outside your experience.

Within Trika's metaphysics, her reality is the power through which experience, knowledge, limitation and recognition are all manifest.

And now the alphabet has brought us back to the Heart.

Abhinavagupta has spent several pages explaining how to place a dot.

And somehow we've ended up investigating the complete dissolution of subject-object duality.

The man is absolutely unstoppable.
