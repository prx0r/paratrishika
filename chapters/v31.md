## I. The Uninitiated Man Who Is Already Initiated

### KSTS p. 251 - Gnoli p. 273

Abhinavagupta begins with his own prose:

**अस्येदानीं त्रिकार्थस्य यदुक्तं कुलात्परतरं त्रिकम् इति सर्वोत्तरमनुत्तरत्वं तन्निरूपयति ।**

*asyedānīṃ trikārthasya yad uktaṃ kulāt parataraṃ trikam iti sarvottaram anuttaratvaṃ tan nirūpayati.*

My literal translation:

“He now explains the unsurpassable condition, supreme above everything, of this Trika teaching, about which it has been said that Trika is higher than Kula.”

That's the formal transition.

Notice that Abhinavagupta isn't simply announcing the abolition of initiation.

He is developing his earlier account of the **supremacy of Trika**.

That is the context.

He has already described the powers of the heart-mantra and the ritual conditions belonging to particular forms of practice.

Now he investigates the higher principle that makes Trika unsurpassable.

And the root text states:

### Parātriṃśikā 18 - the full authentic verse

Root tantra - Bhairava speaking to the Goddess

## अदृष्टमण्डलोऽप्येवं यः कश्चिद्वेत्ति तत्त्वतः ।

## स सिद्धिभाग्भवेन्नित्यं स योगी स च दीक्षितः ॥१८॥

*adṛṣṭamaṇḍalo 'py evaṃ yaḥ kaścid vetti tattvataḥ*

*sa siddhibhāg bhaven nityaṃ sa yogī sa ca dīkṣitaḥ.*

**My literal translation:**

“Even one who has not seen a maṇḍala-whoever truly understands in this way-shall always be a participant in accomplishment. That person is a yogin, and that person is initiated.”

There.

The fucking bomb has landed.

The guy hasn't seen the maṇḍala.

He may never have performed the elaborate ritual.

He might be entirely unknown to the official custodians of the religious tradition.

But if he **knows in the relevant ultimate sense**, the text calls him a yogin and an initiate.

Not potentially initiated.

Not eligible to join the waiting list.

**Initiated.**

Now let's be precise.

The operative phrase is not merely *adṛṣṭamaṇḍala*, “one who has not seen the maṇḍala.”

It is:

**वेत्ति तत्त्वतः - vetti tattvataḥ.**

“He knows according to reality.”

The entire assertion depends upon that qualification.

Without it, we'd simply have:

“Anybody who misses the ritual is enlightened.”

And Abhinavagupta, being an exceptionally formidable philosopher, has no intention of making that ridiculous assertion.

### The grammar of the explosion

**अदृष्ट - adṛṣṭa**

The negative prefix *a-* plus *dṛṣṭa*, the past passive participle of √*dṛś*, “to see.”

Thus *adṛṣṭa* means unseen, not seen.

**मण्डल - maṇḍala**

Circle, diagram, assemblage, ritual arrangement, divine configuration.

In the compound **adṛṣṭamaṇḍalaḥ**, the adjectival construction describes someone who has not seen a maṇḍala.

The masculine nominative singular ending *-aḥ* becomes *-o* before the following *api*:

*adṛṣṭamaṇḍalaḥ + api → adṛṣṭamaṇḍalo 'pi.*

The avagraha **ऽ** marks the elision of the initial *a* of *api*.

The IAST apostrophe represents that elision.

**अपि - api**

Even, also, although.

In this context it is concessive.

*Even though* he has not seen the maṇḍala.

**एवम् - evam**

Thus, in this way.

This points back to the preceding teaching.

You cannot detach it from the thirty instalments of metaphysics we've just completed.

The “thus” refers to knowledge of the supreme heart, not any vague spiritual conviction.

**यः कश्चित् - yaḥ kaścit**

Whoever, any person whatsoever.

This is where the verse becomes socially radical.

The indefinite expression deliberately refrains from specifying a prequalified class of persons.

**वेत्ति - vetti**

Present third-person singular of √*vid*, “to know.”

A direct verb of knowing.

Not *śṛṇoti*, “he hears.”

Not *paṭhati*, “he recites.”

Not *vadati*, “he says.”

**He knows.**

**तत्त्वतः - tattvataḥ**

According to reality, truly, in accordance with the essential nature.

From *tattva*, truth or that-ness, with the adverbial suffix *-tas*.

This is the indispensable word.

The verse is about real understanding.

Not the possession of a verbal formula.

Not the performance of an external credential.

Not the experience of a particularly impressive meditative hallucination.

**सिद्धिभाक् - siddhibhāk**

One who possesses or participates in accomplishment.

The final member is related to √*bhaj*, “to share, participate in, partake of.”

The compound is masculine nominative singular.

**भवेत् - bhavet**

Third-person singular optative of √*bhū*, “to be, become.”

“He would be,” “he shall be,” “he may become,” depending on context.

**नित्यम् - nityam**

Always, perpetually.

This intensifies the statement: the status described is not just a temporary ritual achievement.

**योगी - yogī**

A yogin, masculine nominative singular of *yogin*.

**दीक्षितः - dīkṣitaḥ**

Initiated, consecrated.

A past passive participle derived from the verbal family of *dīkṣ*, to consecrate or initiate.

It is not enough to declare that its historical etymology is simply *dā* \+ *kṣi*, “give knowledge and destroy impurity.” That is a traditional doctrinal interpretation of what initiation accomplishes, not a demonstrated historical derivation.

And Abhinavagupta will provide exactly such a doctrinal explanation shortly.

### The metre

The verse is anuṣṭubh, with four eight-syllable pādas.

| Pāda | Spoken syllables | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *adṛṣṭamaṇḍalo 'py evaṃ* | a-dṛṣ-ṭa-maṇ-ḍa-lo-pye-vaṃ | 8 |
| *yaḥ kaścid vetti tattvataḥ* | yaḥ-kaś-cid-vet-ti-tat-tva-taḥ | 8 |
| *sa siddhibhāg bhaven nityaṃ* | sa-sid-dhi-bhāg-bha-ven-ni-tyaṃ | 8 |
| *sa yogī sa ca dīkṣitaḥ* | sa-yo-gī-sa-ca-dīk-ṣi-taḥ | 8 |

The crucial conjuncts are:

**दृष्ट - dṛṣṭa:** द् + ऋ vocalism, followed by ष् + ट.

**ण्ड - ṇḍa:** ण् + ड, a retroflex nasal-stop sequence.

**त्त्व - ttva:** त् + त् + व, in *tattvataḥ*.

**द्ध - ddha:** द् + ध, in *siddhi*.

**क्ष - kṣa:** क् + ष, in *dīkṣitaḥ*.

And the nominative compounds undergo normal external sandhi.

No special mystical exception is being granted to the grammar.

The absolute can apparently transcend all ontological limitation, but the verse still has to scan.


## II. What Exactly Is the Maṇḍala That He Hasn't Seen?

### KSTS pp. 251-252 - Abhinavagupta's prose

The commentator begins:

**मण्डलं देवताचक्रम्**

*maṇḍalaṃ devatācakram.*

“The maṇḍala is a circle or assembly of deities.”

Right. A perfectly conventional tantric answer.

A maṇḍala can be a prescribed ritual arrangement, often involving particular deities, spatial divisions, mantras, and modes of worship.

And in an initiatory setting, the disciple may be introduced into this divine configuration.

But Abhinavagupta immediately complicates the definition.

**अपश्यन्नप्यप्राप्तमेलकोऽपि**

*apaśyann apy aprāptamelako 'pi.*

“Even though he has not seen it, even though he has not attained the meeting.”

The *melaka* is a meeting or conjunction, here connected with the divine configuration and its initiatory significance.

A person might not have undergone the relevant ritual encounter.

Yet the verse's proposition remains.

Now here is where the 1918 editor inserts a square-bracketed explanation.

### The bracketed editorial note

The KSTS note begins:

**$$अत्रायमभिप्रायः - एकैका वाहदेवी ...$$**

*$$atrāyam abhiprāyaḥ - ekaikā vāhadevī ...$$*

“Here the intended meaning is this: each of the carrying goddesses…”

The editor explains that the twelve sensory divinities can bring about a meeting within their respective domains through the power of the supreme goddess.

This is interesting, especially given our preceding discussion of the senses and the goddesses.

But it is a **later editorial explanation**, not part of Abhinavagupta's running prose. The bracketed material appears in the KSTS-derived electronic text and is absent from the corresponding GRETIL passage. ; \]

Do not attribute this specific explanation of the twelve deities to Abhinavagupta at this location.

The underlying philosophical comparison is reasonable, but textual ownership matters.

One cannot write a critical edition by taking every helpful footnote and declaring that Abhinavagupta personally said it.

Otherwise, in a thousand years, somebody will publish our audiobook and announce that the great Kashmiri philosopher spent his afternoons swearing about the administrative structure of the universe.

He did not.

That is entirely our contribution to Sanskrit intellectual history.

### The second maṇḍala: inside the body

Abhinavagupta continues by naming:

**शरीरनाडीचक्रानुचक्ररूपाणि**

*śarīranāḍīcakrānucakrarūpāṇi.*

“Having the form of the body, subtle channels, cakras, and subsidiary circles.”

The structure is complicated but intelligible.

**śarīra** - body.

**nāḍī** - channel, particularly a subtle channel in a yogic context.

**cakra** - wheel, circle, centre, or structured configuration.

**anucakra** - a subsidiary or associated cakra.

**rūpa** - form or configuration.

The final **-āṇi** is neuter nominative or accusative plural, agreeing with *maṇḍalāni*, “maṇḍalas.”

Thus he is not speaking only about the ritual diagram spread out before a practitioner.

There are also internalised maṇḍalas of the yogic body.

The practitioner can contemplate the divine configuration through the body and the network of channels and centres.

And yet the root verse says that even one who has not directly realised these configurations may possess the relevant knowledge.

That is more radical than merely waiving the requirement for an external ceremony.

The text is also refusing to make a particular internal yogic experience the universal prerequisite for recognition.

Suppose someone has never successfully visualised a cakra.

Has never experienced a subtle channel.

Has never perceived a particular energetic configuration.

The question is whether they nevertheless know the supreme heart *tattvataḥ*.

If they do, the verse's statement still applies.

Why?

Because the ultimate nature of consciousness cannot depend on the appearance of a particular subtle bodily object.

A cakra may be meaningful within the practice.

But it is still something manifested in awareness.

And the reality of awareness cannot be reduced to that one manifestation.

### The third maṇḍala: trident and lotus

The next expression is:

**त्रिशूलाब्जादिमण्डलम्**

*triśūlābjādimaṇḍalam.*

“A maṇḍala such as the trident, lotus, and so forth.”

**triśūla** - trident, from *tri*, three, and *śūla*, spike, spear, or pointed weapon.

**abja** - lotus; literally “water-born,” from *ap* \(water, in its compositional form) and *ja*, born, connected with √*jan*.

**ādi** - and so forth.

These are recognisable forms within tantric religious symbolism.

The trident is especially significant in Trika, given the three Śaktis and the triadic structure underlying so much of the system.

The lotus has its own rich associations with the unfolding of divine manifestation.

But note the specific point.

Abhinavagupta says that someone can fail to see these ritual or yogic configurations and still know the ultimate reality.

That does not render the symbols meaningless.

It means their significance is not ultimately confined to their visible shapes.

The symbol points toward a metaphysical reality.

The symbol is not the entirety of that reality.

### The devastating conclusion

Abhinavagupta now says:

**नात्र मण्डलादिदीक्षोपयोगः**

*nātra maṇḍalādidīkṣopayogaḥ.*

“Here, initiation involving a maṇḍala and such things is not required.”

Notice **atra**.

*Here.*

In this particular case.

He is speaking about the person described by the root verse, the one who truly knows the supreme heart.

He is not issuing a universal proclamation that every tantric rite, every teacher, and every form of initiation is useless.

That would contradict his careful distinction in the previous instalment between supreme recognition and the requirements of particular yogic accomplishments.

Rather, the supreme case is distinguished from the restricted cases.

**The ceremony can be a means, but it does not constitute the ultimate reality that it is intended to disclose.**

And this is where the explosive term arrives.

**Śaktipāta.**


## III. Śaktipāta: When Śiva Stops Sending You Hints and Directly Reveals Himself

Abhinavagupta writes:

**एवमेव कश्चित् परशक्तिपातानुगृहीतो वेत्ति**

*evam eva kaścit paraśaktipātānugṛhīto vetti.*

“Someone, having been graced by the descent of the supreme Śakti, knows in precisely this way.”

This is the explanatory condition attached to the extraordinary verse.

The person has not simply decided to skip initiation because they cannot be bothered travelling to the monastery.

They have been touched by the supreme power of grace.

Their knowledge arises through **paraśaktipāta**.

### The morphology

**para** - supreme, highest, transcendent.

**śakti** - power, capacity, divine energy.

**pāta** - falling, descent, from √*pat*, to fall.

**anugṛhīta** - favoured, blessed, assisted, graced; a past passive participial form connected with *anu-* \+ √*grah*, to grasp, accept, or favour.

The compound **paraśaktipātānugṛhītaḥ** describes someone who has received divine grace through the descent of supreme Śakti.

Its masculine nominative singular ending agrees with *kaścit*, “someone.”

And **vetti**, again, means “knows.”

Not merely “believes.”

Not “has attended a workshop.”

Not “has had a pleasant meditation.”

The person knows the ultimate principle.

### But if Śiva is everywhere, how can grace descend?

Excellent question.

This is where the spatial metaphor can deceive us.

If Śiva is universal consciousness, then Śiva cannot literally be somewhere above the universe, periodically sending energy packages down into individual human skulls.

That would create an absurd spatial dualism.

There would be:

Śiva up there.

The student down here.

Grace travelling between them like a metaphysical parcel.

But Abhinavagupta's ultimate account cannot be reduced to that.

**Śaktipāta is the manifestation of Śiva's liberating power within the contracted subject.**

The word “descent” expresses the occurrence of grace from the standpoint of the finite practitioner.

It does not require the supreme consciousness to move through physical space.

The same divine power that permits contraction also permits the overcoming of contraction.

And because consciousness is ultimately free, the intensity and mode of its liberating manifestation are not completely reducible to a fixed sequence of human actions.

This is what allows Abhinavagupta to describe radically immediate recognition.

### Does this contradict the necessity of a guru?

Only if we confuse two different senses of guru.

There is the **human guru**, a teacher functioning within a transmitted religious tradition.

And there is the **supreme guru**, Bhairava himself, the liberating reality of consciousness.

In the ordinary initiatory situation, the human teacher may function as the means through which grace becomes manifest.

But the highest case need not depend upon a separate human teacher.

The supreme guru is not absent when no human teacher appears.

We should not collapse these levels.

Abhinavagupta is not saying that every person who claims a private revelation should automatically be recognised as an accomplished master.

The condition is actual knowledge of the supreme reality, not the confidence with which someone asserts that they have it.

That difference is enormous.

Someone might proclaim:

“I don't need a guru. Śiva initiated me personally.”

Very well.

But has the relevant recognition occurred?

Has the supposed limited subject actually understood its nature as universal consciousness?

Or has the autobiographical ego merely awarded itself a new cosmic title?

The two possibilities are not the same.

And a subjective claim of divine initiation is not an independent verification of spiritual attainment.

### The wider Tantrāloka confirms the principle

In *Tantrāloka* 4.41-43, Abhinavagupta distinguishes knowledge arising through a teacher, scripture, and spontaneously from oneself.

The three sources are expressed as:

**गुरुतः शास्त्रतः स्वतः**

*gurutaḥ śāstrataḥ svataḥ.*

“From a teacher, from scripture, from oneself.”

He explicitly ranks the succeeding modes higher, culminating in spontaneously arising true discernment.

He then describes the person whose own goddesses of consciousness have initiated them:

**अभिषिक्तः स्वसंवित्तिदेवीभिर्दीक्षितश्च सः**

*abhiṣiktaḥ svasaṃvittidevībhir dīkṣitaś ca saḥ.*

“That person is consecrated and initiated by the goddesses of their own consciousness.”

This is not a modern reconstruction of what Abhinavagupta would have liked to say.

It is his own attested teaching elsewhere.

Now the philosophical system becomes coherent.

A human guru can teach.

A scripture can disclose.

But recognition may also manifest spontaneously through the supreme Śakti.

The source of authority is not ultimately the external form of the teaching.

It is the truth revealed through it.

### The gradations of grace

Abhinavagupta develops this extensively in *Tantrāloka* chapter 13, his discussion of divine grace and its different intensities.

The tradition distinguishes strong, intermediate, and weaker manifestations of *śaktipāta*, further subdivided in its systematic treatment.

These gradations are connected with different relationships between recognition, instruction, scripture, practice, and initiation.

The highest forms of grace can make external means unnecessary.

Other degrees may require intellectual clarification, instruction, contemplative practice, or ritual support.

This is the wider framework in which our present passage makes sense. ; \]

The crucial point is not that practice is always necessary.

Nor that it is always unnecessary.

It is that **the need for particular means depends upon the condition of the finite subject, while the supreme reality recognised is not produced by those means.**

This is a remarkably elegant solution.

And it is also extremely inconvenient for anyone trying to sell a guaranteed, universal, twelve-stage package of enlightenment.

Apparently the supreme consciousness has not agreed to standardise its disclosure according to your payment plan.

### Why the very strongest grace doesn't make everybody a master

Here we need to distinguish possibility from evidence.

Within the metaphysics, spontaneous recognition is possible.

But merely asserting the possibility doesn't establish that a particular claimant has achieved it.

Nor is spectacular behaviour necessarily proof.

A person may experience overwhelming bliss.

Trembling.

A sense of vastness.

A cessation of ordinary thought.

A powerful visionary state.

Those experiences may be meaningful, but their occurrence does not independently establish the comprehensive realisation described by Abhinavagupta.

The passage makes the criterion **tattvataḥ vetti**.

Knows according to reality.

In other words, the question is not simply how impressive the experience was.

It is what the subject actually recognises.

And this leads to one of the most outrageous sentences in the whole commentary.


## IV. “This Knowledge Itself Is Initiation. What Other Initiation Is There?”

### KSTS p. 252 - Abhinavagupta's own prose

**एतज्ज्ञानमेव हि दीक्षा कान्यात्र दीक्षा**

*etajjñānam eva hi dīkṣā kānyātra dīkṣā.*

“This knowledge itself is initiation. What other initiation is there here?”

Read that again.

Not:

“This knowledge is helpful preparation for initiation.”

Not:

“This knowledge complements proper initiation.”

Not:

“This knowledge will improve your chances of being accepted into an advanced initiatory programme.”

**This knowledge itself is initiation.**

The distinction is not merely rhetorical.

It redefines the highest meaning of *dīkṣā*.

### What is dīkṣā?

Ordinarily, **दीक्षा - dīkṣā** denotes religious consecration or initiation.

In tantric Śaiva traditions, initiation can include formal rites performed by a qualified guru, the transmission of mantras, purification, and transformation of the disciple's ritual and spiritual status.

But its highest purpose is liberation from bondage.

If the purpose is achieved through direct recognition, what additional metaphysical achievement could the external rite produce?

That is Abhinavagupta's question.

The rite may remain meaningful.

It may be prescribed within a particular practice.

It may prepare the practitioner.

It may convey instruction.

But it cannot add a second ultimate consciousness beyond the one already recognised.

### The strange logic of attaining what is already yours

Imagine spending ten years searching for your spectacles.

You consult experts.

You construct diagrams.

You learn the metaphysics of spectacle-location.

You perform elaborate ritual gestures involving the left eyebrow.

Finally someone says:

“They're on your head.”

Now what happened?

You acquired a piece of knowledge.

But you did not create the spectacles.

The recognition altered your understanding of the situation.

It did not manufacture the object recognised.

The analogy is imperfect because supreme consciousness is not a pair of physical spectacles.

But the logical structure is useful.

Recognition can be transformative without producing its ultimate referent.

That is the heart of Pratyabhijñā.

The ultimate subject was not absent before recognition.

What was absent was the appropriate understanding of its nature.

### Why the guru still matters

Abhinavagupta continues:

**एवं जानन् विभुना भैरवभट्टारकेण दीक्षितः**

*evaṃ jānan vibhunā bhairavabhaṭṭārakeṇa dīkṣitaḥ.*

“One who knows thus has been initiated by the omnipresent Lord Bhairava.”

**vibhu** - all-pervasive, mighty, omnipresent.

**Bhairavabhaṭṭāraka** - the venerable Lord Bhairava.

**dīkṣitaḥ** - initiated.

The instrumental singular **vibhunā** marks the agency or means of initiation.

The ultimate initiator is the all-pervasive Lord.

And that is the solution to the apparent contradiction.

The individual may not have met a human teacher.

But they are not without the supreme guru.

The universal consciousness itself is the ground of the awakening.

A qualified human guru can function as its manifest instrument.

But the ultimate efficacy belongs to Bhairava.

This avoids two opposite errors.

The first is **ritual absolutism**: the idea that no person can possess ultimate recognition unless a particular external sequence has been performed.

The second is **egoic self-certification**: the idea that any unverified claim of self-realisation must be accepted as equivalent to genuine knowledge.

Abhinavagupta accepts neither.

His criterion is the actual recognition of the supreme heart, arising through grace.

### The syntax of *kānyātra*

This small phrase is worth examining.

**का अन्या अत्र दीक्षा**

*kā anyā atra dīkṣā.*

“What other initiation is there here?”

Through vowel sandhi, this becomes:

**कान्यात्र दीक्षा - kānyātra dīkṣā.**

The question is not requesting a list of alternative ritual systems.

It is rhetorical.

If knowledge of the supreme reality is itself the liberating initiation, no second initiation can surpass it in ultimate purpose.

And the force of **eva** in *etajjñānam eva* is restrictive.

This knowledge **alone**, in the relevant ultimate sense.

Not knowledge plus an independently existing second metaphysical substance.

Not knowledge plus an external certificate that creates the freedom.

The philosophical argument is now razor-sharp.

But then we arrive at an apparent contradiction with what Abhinavagupta said in the previous instalment.

What about self-received mantras?

What about books?

And what about that spectacular prohibition against reading a mantra off a page and declaring oneself a master?


## V. The Self-Received Mantra: A Textual Trap That Absolutely Must Not Be Missed

### KSTS p. 252

Abhinavagupta now brings up the idea of a **svayaṃgṛhītamantra**.

**स्वयंगृहीतमन्त्र - svayaṃgṛhītamantra**

A mantra received or taken by oneself.

In many tantric initiatory contexts, simply taking a mantra from a written source without the appropriate instruction is not considered equivalent to proper transmission.

But here we have a person initiated directly by Bhairava.

So what happens to the restriction?

Abhinavagupta's answer is highly specific.

**इत्येतदेतद्धृदयातिरिक्तमन्त्रविषयम्**

*ity etad etaddhṛdayātiriktamantraviṣayam.*

“This concerns mantras other than this heart.”

Here is the crucial distinction.

The rule about self-acquired mantras is being distinguished from the supreme heart itself.

The heart is not merely another ordinary mantra in a long list of subordinate ritual formulae.

It is the very reality from which those formulae derive their significance.

Abhinavagupta explains the exception by saying that the heart transcends the states of mantras and their lords.

**मन्त्रमहेशतन्महेशरूपोत्तीर्णत्वात्**

*mantramaheśatanmaheśarūpottīrṇatvāt.*

“Because it transcends the forms of the Mantramaheśas and those above them.”

This refers to levels in the Śaiva hierarchy of conscious subjects, not to a contemporary academic degree or clerical office.

The distinction is metaphysical.

The heart is the ultimate ground of the differentiated mantric system.

The restrictions governing particular initiated mantras cannot simply be extended to negate the possibility of direct recognition of that very ground.

### The 1918 editor inserts a warning

At this exact point, the KSTS edition places a bracketed passage beginning:

**$$यदुक्तं स्वयङ्गृहीतमन्त्राश्च ...$$**

*$$yad uktaṃ svayaṃgṛhītamantrāś ca ...$$*

It includes the statement:

**लिपिस्थितस्तु यो मन्त्रो निर्वीर्यः**

*lipisthitas tu yo mantro nirvīryaḥ.*

“A mantra merely situated in written characters is without potency.”

The note also warns against relying on book-learning without the required initiatory understanding.

But the **entire explanatory insertion is editorial**, including the quoted material appearing inside the brackets. It must not be read as though Abhinavagupta inserted those exact quotations into his continuous prose at this point.

This is one of the most important source-critical details in our entire reading.

Why?

Because otherwise you could falsely construct an argument in which Abhinavagupta first condemns all self-received mantras, then immediately contradicts himself by endorsing one.

The actual structure is subtler.

The editor supplies conventional warnings concerning mantra reception.

Abhinavagupta distinguishes the supreme heart from other mantras.

The higher principle is the recognition of the heart through supreme grace.

### Why book-learning isn't automatically initiation

Suppose you read the mantra.

You memorise it.

You can pronounce it perfectly.

You can explain its letter structure.

You can identify its place within the tantric taxonomy.

You can even give a devastatingly entertaining lecture about it.

None of that, by itself, proves that you have attained the recognition Abhinavagupta describes.

You may possess correct information about the doctrine without possessing its realisation.

This is a familiar distinction in philosophy.

Knowing the proposition “all phenomena are manifestations of consciousness” is not necessarily the same as having transformed one's mode of self-recognition accordingly.

A person can intellectually understand a theory and still habitually experience themselves through incompatible assumptions.

In Trika, the problem concerns contracted subjectivity, not merely deficient information.

And the phrase *tattvataḥ vetti* demands more than verbal familiarity.

But equally, the text does not make the mere possession of an external credential the final criterion.

The issue is genuine understanding.

### The heart is not a ritual object

Abhinavagupta again recalls that the heart should not simply be written in books:

**पुस्तकेष्वलेख्यमेवेदं हृदयम्**

*pustakeṣv alekhyam evedaṃ hṛdayam.*

“This heart is indeed not to be written in books.”

And then his conclusion:

**परशक्तिपातानुग्रहादेव एतल्लाभस्तत्त्वतः**

*paraśaktipātānugrahād eva etallābhas tattvataḥ.*

“Genuine attainment of this comes precisely through the grace of the descent of supreme Śakti.”

So we have an extraordinary three-way distinction.

A written character can preserve a phonetic sequence.

A traditional initiation can transmit that sequence within the appropriate ritual and pedagogical framework.

But real attainment of the supreme heart is grounded in divine grace and recognition.

These are related, but not identical.

The text does not teach that simply obtaining a sequence from a book is equivalent to spiritual attainment.

Nor does it teach that the supreme heart is absent unless an institutional authority has approved access.

And that is a philosophically powerful position.

Because it makes the ultimate source of spiritual authority neither ink nor social status.

It makes it the actual disclosure of the supreme reality.

Which brings us to the most socially explosive two words in the passage.

**Yaḥ kaścit.**

Whoever.


## VI. “Whoever.” Not “Whoever Has the Correct Religious Paperwork.”

### KSTS p. 253 - the conclusion of the commentary on verse 18

Abhinavagupta now returns to the expression:

**यः कश्चित् - yaḥ kaścit.**

Whoever.

And explains its force:

**जातिव्रतचर्यादिनैरपेक्ष्यम्**

*jātivratacaryādinairapekṣyam.*

“Independence from considerations such as birth, religious observance, prescribed conduct, and so forth.”

Then:

**अत्र वेदनमेव हि प्रधानम्**

*atra vedanam eva hi pradhānam.*

“Here, knowing alone is paramount.”

That's the sentence.

The entire elaborate ritual tradition has been placed under a higher criterion: genuine knowledge.

And the ordinary classifications used to distinguish people do not govern the supreme recognition described here.

### Jāti - जाति

Birth, kind, class, lineage, or caste, depending on context.

The word is connected with √*jan*, “to be born.”

Here Abhinavagupta explicitly denies that *jāti* is a necessary qualification for the supreme understanding under discussion.

That is a significant theological statement in a society where birth-related classifications could determine access to particular forms of religious activity.

We should neither minimise it nor inflate it into the claim that Abhinavagupta was a modern egalitarian political theorist.

The passage establishes a specific point about spiritual eligibility at the highest level.

It does not, by itself, demonstrate that he rejected every social distinction in every institutional context.

But the point he makes is clear.

**Birth does not determine whether the supreme heart can be recognised.**

### Vrata - व्रत

A vow, observance, religious discipline, or prescribed commitment.

A *vrata* can regulate conduct, bodily practices, food, recitation, ritual activity, or other matters, depending on the tradition.

Such observances can be important within specific paths.

But here Abhinavagupta says that the recognition described by the verse does not depend upon them as universal prerequisites.

The reason is philosophical.

A vow is an action undertaken within differentiated experience.

Recognition concerns the nature of the consciousness in which the action occurs.

The vow may prepare the subject.

It cannot manufacture universal consciousness.

### Caryā - चर्या

Conduct, observance, practice, religious way of life.

Related to √*car*, “to move, proceed, act, conduct oneself.”

A person may follow an elaborate daily discipline.

Another may not.

Within a particular religious system, that difference may have significance.

But the supreme reality is not itself produced by the distinction.

The question in this verse is whether recognition has occurred.

And now the commentator has put the matter so emphatically that the supposed religious résumé has begun to look suspiciously irrelevant to the final criterion.

Imagine approaching the supreme heart with the following application:

“Dear Bhairava,

“I have completed seventeen preliminary courses, received six certificates of attendance, purchased the advanced mantra package, and memorised the correct hand gestures.

“Please find attached my spiritual qualifications and a reference from my teacher.”

Bhairava replies:

“Do you recognise your nature?”

“Well, no, but the certificates are embossed.”

“Fantastic. You've mastered the stationery.”

### Vedana - वेदन

Knowing, experiencing, apprehension.

Here it is the relevant understanding of the supreme principle.

Connected with √*vid*, to know.

**pradhāna** means principal, primary, most important.

Thus:

**vedanam eva pradhānam** -

Knowing itself is paramount.

This does not eliminate the importance of determining what counts as genuine knowledge.

Indeed, the verse's *tattvataḥ* makes that question unavoidable.

But it prevents any external qualification from automatically taking the place of recognition.

### The definition of yoga

Abhinavagupta quotes the short phrase:

**योगमेकत्वमिच्छन्ति**

*yogam ekatvam icchanti.*

“They understand yoga as unity.”

GRETIL identifies the quotation with the *Mālinīvijayottaratantra*, although its precise numbering is not securely supplied there, so I won't manufacture a verse number.

Now this is a decisive interpretive move.

*Yoga* is not here being defined primarily through physical posture, breath regulation, or concentration.

It is understood as **ekatva**, oneness.

The relation between the limited subject and Bhairava is recognised as fundamentally nondual.

The practitioner is a *yogī* because that unity has been realised.

And the practitioner is *dīkṣita*, initiated, because the liberating knowledge has become manifest.

### What does initiation actually accomplish?

Abhinavagupta says:

**ज्ञानदानमायाक्षपणलक्षणा च तस्यैव दीक्षा**

*jñānadānamāyākṣapaṇalakṣaṇā ca tasyaiva dīkṣā.*

“His very initiation is characterised by the giving of knowledge and the elimination of Māyā.”

There is an important nuance.

The word **māyā** here belongs to a metaphysical framework concerning restriction and differentiation.

The expression does not mean that all perceptual appearances physically disappear.

It concerns the removal of the limiting condition through which the subject fails to recognise its supreme nature.

**jñāna** - knowledge.

**dāna** - giving, bestowal.

**māyā** - the principle of limiting differentiation.

**kṣapaṇa** - destruction, removal, exhaustion, connected with √*kṣi*, to diminish or destroy.

**lakṣaṇa** - characteristic, defining mark.

The compound tells us what initiation does in its liberating function.

It confers knowledge.

It removes bondage.

And that is why the highest initiation can be identified with recognition.

### A second bracketed editorial quotation

The 1918 editor adds a bracketed couplet:

**ददाति ज्ञानसद्भावं क्षिपयत्यखिलं मलम् ।**

*dadāti jñānasadbhāvaṃ kṣipayaty akhilaṃ malam.*

**दानक्षपणधर्मत्वाद्दीक्षेति हि प्रकीर्तिता ॥**

*dānakṣapaṇadharmatvād dīkṣeti hi prakīrtitā.*

The verse explains *dīkṣā* through giving knowledge and destroying impurity.

Its appearance here is **inside the editor's brackets**, not inside Abhinavagupta's continuous exposition. Its independently established original source is not specified at this point.

Abhinavagupta's own preceding prose already expresses the doctrine, so we can understand why the editor supplied the comparison. But the distinction must remain visible.

### The final particle: ca

And then, because Abhinavagupta cannot resist extracting philosophical significance from every last syllable, he turns to:

**च - ca.**

“And.”

The root verse says:

*sa yogī sa ca dīkṣitaḥ.*

“He is a yogin, and he is initiated.”

Abhinavagupta says:

**चकारोऽवधारणे**

*cakāro 'vadhāraṇe.*

“The particle *ca* has a restrictive or emphatic force.”

He interprets the sequence as emphasising the identity of the person possessing all three designations.

The person who truly knows is a possessor of accomplishment.

That same person is a yogin.

That same person is initiated.

These are not three unrelated achievements accidentally collected in one person.

They follow from the supreme recognition.

The Sanskrit's repeated **sa**-“he,” “that person”-reinforces the identification.

And **nityam** makes it enduring.

This is the conclusion of Abhinavagupta's explanation of verse 18.

But the tantra is not finished describing what becomes possible when the supreme heart is known.

In fact, it now introduces a company of goddesses.

And apparently they all know who you are.


## VII. Verse 19: Every Divine Power Knows You, and Grammar Is About to Become Metaphysics Again

### KSTS pp. 253-254 - Gnoli pp. 273-274

The next verse is introduced in two halves, with commentary between them.

This division is important.

Abhinavagupta does not present the complete verse and then one undivided explanation. He comments on the first half, introduces the second with **tathā**, and then explains the Śākinī doctrine.

The complete verse is:

**अनेन ज्ञातमात्रेण ज्ञायते सर्वशक्तिभिः ।**

*anena jñātamātreṇa jñāyate sarvaśaktibhiḥ.*

**शाकिनीकुलसामान्यो भवेद्योगं विनापि हि ॥१९॥**

*śākinīkulasāmānyo bhaved yogaṃ vināpi hi.*

My translation:

“By merely knowing this, one becomes known to all the Śaktis; indeed, even without yoga, one comes to share the common nature of the Śākinī family.”

That is the first, straightforward construal.

But Abhinavagupta goes considerably further.

### First half: anena jñātamātreṇa

**अनेन - anena**

Instrumental singular of the demonstrative *idam*, “this.”

“By this.”

In context, by this supreme heart or its knowledge.

**ज्ञातमात्रेण - jñātamātreṇa**

A compound involving:

*jñāta* - known, understood, past passive participle of √*jñā*.

*mātra* - mere, only, just that amount.

The final instrumental singular ending is **-eṇa**.

Thus:

“By the mere fact of this having been known.”

The construction stresses immediacy.

There is no additional condition inserted between genuine knowledge and the stated consequence.

But again, *mere knowledge* does not mean superficial acquaintance.

It refers to the *tattvataḥ vetti* of the previous verse.

The underlying condition has already been specified.

### Jñāyate - ज्ञायते

A passive present form, third-person singular, from √*jñā*, to know.

“He is known,” or “it is known,” depending on what subject is supplied by the contextual construction.

Now comes the grammatical difficulty.

**सर्वशक्तिभिः - sarvaśaktibhiḥ**

All the powers.

The ending **-bhiḥ** is instrumental plural feminine.

Normally, in a passive construction, the instrumental can identify the agent:

“He is known by all the Śaktis.”

And Abhinavagupta does indeed explain the passage this way.

The deities, the omniscient powers, recognise the person who possesses this knowledge.

But then he offers another interpretation.

### The instrumental of means

He says:

**सर्वाभिः शक्तिभिरिति करणे तृतीया**

*sarvābhiḥ śaktibhir iti karaṇe tṛtīyā.*

“In the expression ‘by all the Śaktis,’ the third case is instrumental in the sense of an instrument.”

This is a specifically grammatical observation.

**tṛtīyā** means the third case, the instrumental, in the traditional Sanskrit case enumeration.

**karaṇa** means instrument or means.

Thus *sarvaśaktibhiḥ* can signify not merely the beings who know the practitioner, but the powers **through which knowledge occurs**.

Abhinavagupta says that whatever is known by those omniscient powers is also known through this knowledge of the heart.

This is a much more expansive interpretation.

It shifts the emphasis from recognition by a group of goddesses to participation in the knowledge of the divine powers.

### What is the actual philosophical claim?

At one level, the verse describes extraordinary recognition by divine beings.

At another, Abhinavagupta interprets it through the relation between the finite subject and the universal power of knowing.

Ordinarily, the individual experiences knowledge through restricted cognitive faculties.

“I see this.”

“I remember that.”

“I can understand one thing but not another.”

The field of possible knowledge appears limited.

But Trika understands the faculties as manifestations of a universal conscious power.

If a practitioner recognises their identity with that power, their relation to knowledge itself is radically reinterpreted.

The powers of knowledge are no longer taken as fundamentally alien.

They belong to the one consciousness.

The scripture frames that recognition through the language of the Śaktis.

Yet the assertion of omniscient knowledge remains a traditional religious claim, not an experimentally demonstrated human capacity.

Recognising the metaphysical unity of consciousness does not establish that someone can correctly predict tomorrow's weather or know the contents of another person's private thoughts.

The philosophical argument and the asserted siddhi need to be distinguished.

And Abhinavagupta is careful enough about grammar to show us how the verse can sustain the two interpretations.

### The metre

The four pādas scan as eight syllables each:

| Pāda | Division | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *anena jñātamātreṇa* | a-ne-na-jñā-ta-mā-tre-ṇa | 8 |
| *jñāyate sarvaśaktibhiḥ* | jñā-ya-te-sar-va-śak-ti-bhiḥ | 8 |
| *śākinīkulasāmānyo* | śā-ki-nī-ku-la-sā-mā-nyo | 8 |
| *bhaved yogaṃ vināpi hi* | bha-ved-yo-gaṃ-vi-nā-pi-hi | 8 |

Now notice **ज्ञ**.

The conjunction of ज् + ञ represents a conventional conjunct, romanised *jñ*.

In different modern Sanskrit pronunciation traditions it may be realised differently, but the IAST representation keeps the underlying consonant sequence visible.

The word **ज्ञात - jñāta** contains a long ā.

**ज्ञायते - jñāyate** also begins with long ā.

In the metrical division, *jñā* counts as one long syllable, not two syllables simply because the conjunct is graphically elaborate.

And in **शाकिनी**, the vowels are:

śā - long ā.

ki - short i.

nī - long ī.

Do not reduce the phonological structure to “sha-ki-ni.”

The long vowels matter.

Now Abhinavagupta turns to the second half of the verse.

And the Śākinīs become metaphysically extraordinary.


## VIII. Śākinīkulasāmānya: The Common Vibration Beneath the Entire Family of Goddesses

### KSTS p. 254 - Gnoli p. 274

The commentator explains:

**शाकिनीकुलस्य विशेषस्पन्दात्मनः सामान्यस्पन्दरूपोऽकुलरूपः शक्तिचक्रेश्वरो भवेत्**

*śākinīkulasya viśeṣaspandātmanaḥ sāmānyaspandarūpo 'kularūpaḥ śakticakreśvaro bhavet.*

“He becomes the Lord of the circle of powers, having the nature of Akula, the general vibration underlying the Śākinī family, whose character is differentiated vibration.”

This is a philosophically rich sentence.

And it needs to be read very carefully.

### Viśeṣaspanda - विशेषस्पन्द

**viśeṣa** - distinction, particularity, specificity.

**spanda** - vibration, pulsation, dynamic manifestation.

We have discussed *Spanda* at length in the Śiva Sūtras and the *Spandakārikā*.

The word should not be reduced to mechanical vibration in the modern physical sense.

Abhinavagupta is not suggesting that the goddesses are simply oscillations with measurable frequencies.

*Spanda* concerns the dynamic character of conscious self-manifestation.

Its power to reveal.

Its freedom to differentiate.

Its intrinsic activity without dependence upon an external mover.

**viśeṣaspanda** therefore concerns differentiated manifestations of that power.

The various Śākinīs are connected with the distinct operations of divine Śakti.

But their distinctness does not make them metaphysically independent substances.

### Sāmānyaspanda - सामान्यस्पन्द

**sāmānya** - common, general, universal, shared.

**spanda** - pulsation or dynamic manifestation.

The common vibration is the undivided ground of the differentiated powers.

This is not the assertion that all individual activities are literally identical.

Rather, their manifestation belongs to one underlying conscious freedom.

Imagine an orchestra.

A violin sounds.

A flute sounds.

A drum sounds.

Each has a distinct timbre, register, and function.

But the music is not merely a random heap of unrelated noises.

The ensemble can possess a coherent unity expressed through differentiated parts.

The analogy is limited.

A musical ensemble depends upon external coordination.

Supreme consciousness, in Trika, is understood as intrinsically self-unified.

Nevertheless the distinction between differentiated activity and encompassing unity is illuminating.

The Śākinī powers are distinguished.

The underlying conscious dynamism is one.

### Akula - अकुल

Now comes one of the most important words in the entire Kaula-Trika system.

**Kula** can mean family, group, aggregate, clan, or a structured totality.

Within the tantric philosophical vocabulary it can signify the manifested grouping of powers.

**Akula** is not merely a negative assertion that no grouping exists.

It designates the supreme reality transcending restriction to a differentiated aggregate.

The relation between Kula and Akula is central to the nondual Kaula interpretation of Śiva and Śakti.

Akula is not somewhere outside the manifested powers.

It is their ultimate undivided ground.

And the practitioner who recognises the supreme heart is said to become **akularūpa**, having the nature of Akula.

### Śakticakreśvara - शक्तिचक्रेश्वर

Here we have:

**śakti** - power.

**cakra** - circle, wheel, assemblage.

**īśvara** - lord, master, sovereign.

The compound refers to the lord of the circle of powers.

This is an extraordinary status.

The practitioner is not simply recognised by the goddesses.

The practitioner comes to possess the nature of the common principle underlying their distinct powers.

In the text's metaphysics, that principle is the supreme consciousness.

### But does the ego become ruler of the goddesses?

No.

Absolutely not.

This is exactly where careless readings go catastrophically wrong.

The empirical ego might say:

“Excellent. I have learned that I am the Lord of the Śākinīs. Please inform the universe that all departments now report directly to me.”

That is not recognition.

That is an exceptionally elaborate personality disorder wearing a Sanskrit costume.

The distinction concerns the finite subject's identification.

Ordinarily, the person identifies as a restricted knower.

Through recognition, they discover the universal consciousness that grounds both their own experience and the differentiated divine powers.

The resulting lordship belongs to that universal consciousness.

It is not an unlimited new personal privilege acquired by the finite ego.

### What does *yogaṃ vināpi* mean?

The verse explicitly says:

**योगं विनापि हि - yogaṃ vināpi hi.**

“Even without yoga.”

But which yoga?

Abhinavagupta explains that the person need not rely on **ābhyāsika yoga**, yoga constituted through repeated practice, or on the condition of identity that is attained only after the fall of the Māyic body.

This is a striking contrast between a gradual practice-dependent model and immediate recognition.

The higher realisation does not require the body to die.

Nor does it necessarily require prolonged preparatory yogic procedures.

But do not misread this as a universal instruction that no one should practise.

The sentence concerns a person who has already attained the relevant knowledge through supreme grace.

For that person, the ultimate identity is not something awaiting production by further practice.

The difference is between practice as a possible means of recognition and practice as an alleged creator of the supreme Self.

The latter is impossible in this metaphysics.

The supreme Self is not a product.

### The great philosophical paradox

If the supreme consciousness is already present, why do people not ordinarily recognise it?

Because its freedom includes the power of contraction.

The limitation is not ultimately an external force capable of overpowering Śiva.

It is a mode of manifestation.

The finite subject experiences itself through that limitation.

When recognition arises, the underlying identity is disclosed.

But the identity was not absent beforehand.

That is why the highest *śaktipāta* can produce recognition without a gradual construction of the Self.

And that is why *yogaṃ vināpi* has such philosophical force.

The person is not liberated because they have successfully completed the longest practice.

They are liberated through recognition of the nature that makes practice, experience, and knowledge possible in the first place.

Now Abhinavagupta says:

**किं च - kiṃ ca.**

“And furthermore.”

Because apparently becoming the lord of the Śākinī circle is not enough.

We are now going to discover that someone who doesn't know the rules of worship can become a knower of worship.

And the explanation involves a particularly merciless joke about ignorance.


## IX. Verse 20: The Person Who Doesn't Know the Rules Somehow Becomes the Knower of Worship

### KSTS pp. 254-255 - Gnoli p. 274

Abhinavagupta introduces the next statement:

**अविधिज्ञो विधानज्ञो जायते यजनं प्रति ॥२०॥**

*avidhijño vidhānajño jāyate yajanaṃ prati.*

My translation:

“One who does not know the prescribed procedure becomes knowledgeable concerning the performance of worship.”

An extraordinarily compact claim.

And notice: this is **a two-pāda unit**, not a complete four-pāda śloka. We must retain the form in which Abhinavagupta's edition presents it rather than inventing another half-verse to make it look tidy. The independent root edition joins these words to the beginning of what the commentary calls verse 21, producing a different numbering and segmentation. ; \]

### The grammar

**अविधिज्ञः - avidhijñaḥ**

The prefix *a-* negates.

*Vidhi* means prescription, procedure, rule, or prescribed action.

*Jña* means knowing, from √*jñā*.

Thus:

“Not knowing the prescribed procedure.”

**विधानज्ञः - vidhānajñaḥ**

*Vidhāna* - prescription, arrangement, performance, ordering, institution.

*Jña* - knowing.

“One who knows the prescribed arrangement or procedure.”

**जायते - jāyate**

Third-person singular present middle of √*jan*, to be born, arise, become.

**यजनम् - yajanam**

Worship, sacrifice, ritual performance, from √*yaj*, to worship, sacrifice, or make an offering.

**प्रति - prati**

Toward, with respect to, concerning.

So:

*yajanaṃ prati* -

“With respect to worship.”

The phrase is grammatically significant because it restricts the claim to the context of religious worship or ritual performance.

It is not simply an assertion that someone suddenly becomes omniscient about every subject.

### The metre

The two pādas have eight syllables apiece:

*avidhijño vidhānajño*

a-vi-dhi-jño-vi-dhā-na-jño - **8**.

*jāyate yajanaṃ prati*

jā-ya-te-ya-ja-naṃ-pra-ti - **8**.

The first pāda contains two instances of the conjunct **ज्ञ - jña**.

And notice that *vidhānajña* contains **ध**, the voiced aspirated dental stop *dh*, distinct from **द**, unaspirated *d*.

Sanskrit phonology is not optional simply because we are discussing supreme consciousness.

### Abhinavagupta's explanation is more subtle than the surface reading

He explains:

**विधिः क्रिया ज्ञा ज्ञानम्**

*vidhiḥ kriyā jñā jñānam.*

“*Vidhi* is action; *jñā* is knowledge.”

This is an interpretive decomposition of the root verse's compounds.

The point is not merely the difference between knowing a ritual rulebook and failing to know it.

Abhinavagupta is identifying two capacities:

**kriyā** - action, doing, performance.

**jñāna** - knowing, understanding.

The finite subject may be deficient in both.

They may lack correct knowledge.

They may lack proper agency.

And when those limitations govern the subject, the tradition calls that subject a **paśu**.

Right.

A paśu.

Literally a bound creature or animal in ordinary Sanskrit, but in tantric Śaiva technical vocabulary, a condition of bound individuality.

To clarify this, Abhinavagupta cites another scripture.

The *Kiraṇatantra*.

And the verse is one of those passages that forces you to completely rethink what “bondage” means.


## X. The Bound Soul That Is Eternal, Bodiless, and All-Pervading. Excuse Me?

### Authentic quotation: Kiraṇatantra 1.15

**पशुर्नित्यो ह्यमूर्तोऽज्ञो निष्क्रियो निर्गुणोऽप्रभुः ।**

*paśur nityo hy amūrto 'jño niṣkriyo nirguṇo 'prabhuḥ.*

**व्यापी मायोदरान्तःस्थो भोगोपायविचिन्तकः ॥**

*vyāpī māyodarāntaḥstho bhogopāyavicintakaḥ.*

My literal translation:

“The bound soul is eternal, incorporeal, ignorant, inactive, without qualities, and not sovereign; all-pervading, situated within the belly of Māyā, and preoccupied with the means of enjoyment.”

This is supported by the independently edited *Kiraṇatantra* and the accompanying commentary of Rāmakaṇṭha.

Now, look at that.

The *paśu* is described as:

Eternal.

Bodiless.

All-pervading.

And yet bound.

How the fuck?

We ordinarily imagine bondage as being physically trapped in something.

A prisoner in a cell.

A person bound by ropes.

A creature locked inside a cage.

But here the soul is described as all-pervading and nevertheless not sovereign.

That is an extraordinary philosophical claim.

### Nitya - नित्य

Eternal, enduring, permanent.

The Kiraṇatantra is not presenting the bound subject as something momentarily created from non-conscious matter.

It attributes enduring existence to the soul.

### Amūrta - अमूर्त

The negative prefix *a-* plus *mūrta*, embodied, formed, physically configured.

Thus “without material form,” “incorporeal.”

This does not mean the bound soul never has any relation to a body.

It means the soul is not identical with a gross material body.

### Ajña - अज्ञ

Unknowing.

From *a-* \+ *jña*, knowing.

Now this is particularly interesting.

The soul is not necessarily absolutely devoid of every possible cognitive event.

Rāmakaṇṭha's commentary explains the relevant restriction through the limited or deficient condition of knowledge and activity.

The technical claim concerns the soul's bound condition, not a simple identification with a physical rock.

### Niṣkriya - निष्क्रिय

Without activity.

From *nis-/niṣ-* \+ *kriyā*.

In the restricted technical context, this concerns the absence or limitation of sovereign agency.

Again, not necessarily the complete inability of an embodied person to move a hand or speak.

The limited person obviously acts.

But their activity is conditioned.

They do not possess the supreme freedom of Śiva.

### Nirguṇa - निर्गुण

Without qualities.

A very dangerous expression if misunderstood.

In various Indian philosophical traditions, *guṇa* can have different technical meanings.

Here the Kiraṇatantra's accompanying commentary clarifies the intended denial in relation to philosophical doctrines of the soul's qualities.

It does not simply imply that a bound person lacks every observable characteristic.

### Aprabhu - अप्रभु

Not sovereign.

Not lord.

Not master.

And here is an important textual point.

The independent *Kiraṇatantra* reads **'prabhuḥ**, representing **aprabhuḥ** after sandhi.

The 1918 KSTS electronic transcription at this place presents **prabhuḥ** without the privative *a*.

Those are philosophically very different expressions.

But GRETIL's quotation and the independent Kiraṇatantra both support **aprabhuḥ**. Rāmakaṇṭha's explanation also specifically treats *aprabhu* as restricted lordship. The KSTS reading should therefore be treated as a suspect textual or transcriptional divergence, not silently made authoritative. ; ; \]

And the loss of one little negative prefix changes the entire sentence.

**Prabhu:** sovereign.

**Aprabhu:** not sovereign.

One letter.

One fucking letter.

The soul goes from being a ruler to being bound.

This is why we keep checking the Sanskrit.

### Māyodarāntaḥstha

Now the magnificent compound:

**मायोदरान्तःस्थ - māyodarāntaḥstha**

*maya*? No.

**māyā** - Māyā, the power associated with limitation and differentiation.

**udara** - belly, interior, abdomen.

**antaḥ** - within.

**stha** - standing, situated, from √*sthā*.

Thus:

“Situated within the belly of Māyā.”

This is not an anatomical claim.

It is a metaphysical description of the soul's restricted condition.

And here is the striking paradox.

A soul can be regarded as all-pervading in one ontological sense while nevertheless subject to limitation in its modes of knowledge and action.

The limitation need not be explained by spatial confinement.

It concerns what the subject can know and do.

### Bhogopāyavicintaka

**bhoga** - enjoyment, experience, consumption, fruition.

**upāya** - means, method, instrument.

**vicintaka** - contemplating, considering, preoccupied with.

Thus:

“Concerned with the means of enjoyment.”

The bound subject is oriented toward obtaining particular results through particular means.

And this connects beautifully with the preceding discussion.

The finite subject seeks objects.

Experiences desire.

Acts through limited faculties.

Pursues fulfilment.

But does not recognise its supreme nature.

It is not merely trapped in a physical location.

It is trapped in a mode of identification.

### A doctrinal qualification

The *Kiraṇatantra* has its own Śaiva Siddhānta philosophical background.

Abhinavagupta cites it within a nondual Trika argument.

We must not assume that the metaphysical systems are identical merely because he appropriates a verse.

The Kiraṇatantra's account of the enduring bound soul and its relation to the Lord cannot simply be equated with the Pratyabhijñā doctrine of the supreme Self.

Abhinavagupta is using the verse to illuminate the condition of the unknowing, restricted subject.

His own higher interpretation identifies that subject's ultimate ground with universal consciousness.

The distinction matters.

Otherwise we turn every Śaiva scripture into one undifferentiated system and erase the very philosophical disagreements that made the intellectual tradition so interesting.

### Return to verse 20: the transformation

Abhinavagupta says that even the *paśu*, through knowing this supreme heart, becomes a knower and agent.

**कर्ता ज्ञाता च - kartā jñātā ca.**

“An agent and a knower.”

Now the relationship between *jñāna* and *kriyā* becomes central.

The bound subject possesses them in restricted form.

The awakened subject recognises their grounding in supreme freedom.

And even worship that was incomplete can be regarded as complete because the heart encompasses everything.

This is his actual philosophical claim:

**यजनं चास्यापूर्णमपि पूर्णं भवति**

*yajanaṃ cāsyāpūrṇam api pūrṇaṃ bhavati.*

“And even his incomplete worship becomes complete.”

Why?

**सर्वमयत्वाद्धृदयस्य**

*sarvamayatvād dhṛdayasya.*

“Because the heart consists of everything.”

This is a stunning sentence.

But be careful.

Abhinavagupta is not giving everyone permission to pretend they have performed rituals they haven't performed.

He is not saying that any arbitrary negligence produces the same restricted ritual result as a properly completed rite.

The point is about **the supreme purpose of worship**.

If all the differentiated powers are already encompassed within the supreme heart, and that heart is genuinely recognised, then the fundamental theological objective is not lacking.

The fullness of recognition is not dependent upon having completed every external differentiation of the rite.

The supreme heart is not merely one component of worship.

It is the ground of the worshipper, the act of worship, the deity worshipped, and the entire field of ritual manifestation.

This is why incomplete worship can be called complete at the higher level.

And now Abhinavagupta has to explain why the heart can make that claim.

So naturally he returns to the alphabet.

Of course he fucking does.


## XI. Verse 21: The Entire Cosmos Is Packed Inside Three Phonemic Powers

### KSTS p. 255 - Gnoli p. 274

Abhinavagupta introduces the next verse with:

**तथाहि - tathā hi.**

“For indeed…”

He is now explaining why knowledge of the heart can supply the fullness that ordinary differentiated worship lacks.

The root text reads:

**कालाग्निमादितः कृत्वा मायान्तं ब्रह्मदेहगम् ।**

*kālāgnim āditaḥ kṛtvā māyāntaṃ brahmadehagam.*

**शिवो विश्वाद्यनन्तान्तः परं शक्तित्रयं मतम् ॥२१॥**

*śivo viśvādyanantāntaḥ paraṃ śaktitrayaṃ matam.*

A cautious, interpretive translation:

“Taking Kālāgni as the beginning and Māyā as the limit, this is contained within the body of Brahman; from Viśva onward, extending to Ananta and Śiva, the supreme triad of powers is taught.”

The syntax is highly compressed. The commentary is indispensable for identifying how the cosmic levels are distributed among the mantra's constituents; the English should not disguise that compression. ; \]

And now the cosmic alphabet returns.

# सौः

sauḥ - the supreme heart-mantra

# स

**sa**

The lower cosmic spheres, through Māyā

# औ

**au**

The Śākta sphere and higher manifestation

# ः

**visarga**

Supreme emission and the power beyond the spheres

A conceptual presentation of the commentary's mantra-cosmology, not an ordinary phonetic definition of the sounds.

### Kālāgni - कालाग्नि

Literally:

**kāla** - time, death, or the determining temporal principle, depending on context.

**agni** - fire.

Here the reference concerns the cosmic hierarchy associated with Kālāgni, the lower end of the relevant series of worlds or levels.

This is not simply a description of an ordinary fire.

It belongs to tantric cosmological classification.

The commentary explicitly associates the range from Kālāgni through the world of the earth-element and up to Māyā with the body of the first mantra constituent.

### Māyānta - मायान्त

**māyā** - the limiting power or principle of differentiation.

**anta** - end, boundary, limit.

Thus *māyānta* means “extending as far as Māyā.”

The verse indicates a range.

Its purpose is to map a portion of the cosmic hierarchy onto the relevant phonemic power.

### Brahmadehaga - ब्रह्मदेहग

This is particularly interesting.

**brahma** - here interpreted within the mantra's technical terminology.

**deha** - body.

**ga** - going, situated, present within, related to √*gam*, to go.

Thus:

“Situated within the body of Brahman.”

But Abhinavagupta immediately makes the intended phonemic identification explicit.

**ब्रह्मणः सकारस्य देहे**

*brahmaṇaḥ sakārasya dehe.*

“In the body of Brahman, that is, of the phoneme *sa*.”

There.

The *sa* sound again.

The same little sibilant that Part XXIX associated with supreme bliss is now being used to encompass an enormous range of cosmic manifestation.

The theological explanation is not that the sound S physically contains planets and galaxies inside an acoustic waveform.

Rather, the phoneme is treated as a representation of divine expressive power.

The cosmic hierarchy is interpreted through the mantra because both are understood as differentiations of consciousness.

### The second domain: au

Abhinavagupta continues with the range beginning at **Viśva** and extending through the higher Śākta manifestation.

The GRETIL text explicitly mentions:

**अनन्तस्यौकारस्यान्तः**

*anantasyaukārasyāntaḥ.*

“Within the *aukāra* of Ananta.”

Here *aukāra* designates the vowel **au**.

There is a noteworthy textual problem.

The Muktabodha-derived KSTS transcription gives **akāra** at this point, while GRETIL gives **aukāra**. These are not interchangeable: *a* and *au* are distinct Sanskrit vowels. In light of the immediate threefold mantra analysis and the earlier quotation concerning the trident's pervasion, *aukāra* fits the doctrinal sequence better. But I have not established that this difference originates in the original manuscript witnesses rather than electronic transmission. It must be flagged as a textual discrepancy, not confidently announced as a newly discovered manuscript variant. ; \]

This is exactly why accuracy in vowel representation matters.

**अ - a**

And:

**औ - au**

Do not signify the same phoneme.

Even within nondual metaphysics, distinctions still matter.

Otherwise one could replace every vowel with a convenient approximation and declare the entire grammatical system liberated.

Pāṇini would rise from the dead purely to express disapproval.

### The third power: visarga

Abhinavagupta concludes the mapping:

**परं विसर्गात्मकं शक्तित्रयम्**

*paraṃ visargātmakaṃ śaktitrayam.*

“The supreme triad of powers, whose nature is visarga.”

Here:

**para** - supreme.

**visarga** - emission, sending forth, release.

**ātmaka** - having the nature of.

**śakti-traya** - triad of powers.

The three powers should be understood through the broader Trika account of divine manifestation.

The primary conceptual triad is often expressed as:

**icchā** - will.

**jñāna** - knowledge.

**kriyā** - action.

But we should not assume that every occurrence of *śaktitraya* is simply a mechanical repetition of those three words with no difference in context.

Here its significance is tied to the complete heart-mantra and its pervasion of the cosmic levels.

### Why this matters for initiation

The argument now becomes wonderfully simple.

The supreme heart is not one restricted object within the cosmos.

Its phonemic structure is understood as encompassing the cosmic hierarchy.

Everything is within the scope of its manifestation.

Therefore a practitioner who truly recognises the heart does not lack the underlying reality of the individual powers invoked in ritual.

And this is why the incomplete act of worship can possess completeness at the ultimate level.

The individual ritual might contain a particular deity.

A particular maṇḍala.

A particular mantra.

A particular sequence of offerings.

But the supreme heart is the ground of all these manifestations.

Knowledge of that ground is not simply one accomplishment competing with the others.

It is the basis through which their ultimate significance becomes intelligible.

### The metrical investigation

The verse is anuṣṭubh.

| Pāda | Division | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *kālāgnim āditaḥ kṛtvā* | kā-lāg-nim-ā-di-taḥ-kṛt-vā | 8 |
| *māyāntaṃ brahmadehagam* | mā-yān-taṃ-brah-ma-de-ha-gam | 8 |
| *śivo viśvādyanantāntaḥ* | śi-vo-viś-vā-dya-nan-tān-taḥ | 8 |
| *paraṃ śaktitrayaṃ matam* | pa-raṃ-śak-ti-tra-yaṃ-ma-tam | 8 |

Look at the difference between **ब्रह्म - brahma** and **ब्राह्म - brāhma**.

The verse has the former in the compound.

The conjunct **ह्म** represents ह् + म, and the preceding ब्र represents ब् + र.

In **शक्तित्रय**, the cluster क्त is क् + त, and त्र is त् + र.

And **अनन्तान्तः - anantāntaḥ** contains the long vowel ā arising through compound formation.

This is not decorative transliteration.

We are preserving the phonological structure on which the argument is explicitly built.

### The deeper philosophical move

Notice how the argument is becoming increasingly recursive.

The supreme consciousness manifests the phonemic powers.

The phonemic powers are used to articulate the cosmic structure.

The cosmic structure includes the limited subject.

The limited subject recognises the phonemic heart.

And through that recognition, the subject recognises the consciousness that was never confined to the phonemic structure.

It is an extraordinary reversal.

At first, the individual seems to be a small observer confronting a vast universe.

Now the universe is understood as manifested within consciousness.

The mantra maps the manifestation.

The recognition discloses its ground.

And the supposed little individual discovers that the reality of the entire process was never separate from the supreme Self.

The Sanskrit alphabet has now become a cosmic mirror.

And the person looking into the mirror has discovered that the mirror, the reflection, and the act of seeing are not ultimately independent realities.

Which is beautiful.

And also a slightly alarming amount of responsibility to place on the letter S.


## XII. Verse 22: The Finite Creature Receives Sovereign Knowledge Without Having to Become an Infinite Object

### KSTS pp. 255-256 - Gnoli pp. 274-275

Now the root text continues.

**तदन्तर्वर्ति यत्किञ्चिच्छुद्धमार्गे व्यवस्थितम् ।**

*tadantar varti yat kiṃcic chuddhamārge vyavasthitam.*

**अणुर्विशुद्धमचिरादैश्वरं ज्ञानमश्नुते ॥२२॥**

*aṇur viśuddham acirād aiśvaraṃ jñānam aśnute.*

My translation:

“Whatever is contained within that and established in the pure path, the finite subject soon attains pure, sovereign knowledge of it.”

The syntax is compressed, but Abhinavagupta explains its essential point.

Whatever differentiated thing exists within the relevant manifestation belongs to the heart-seed.

And the finite subject, through the power of the Lord, attains the knowledge associated with that divine reality.

### Tadantarvarti

**tad** - that.

**antar** - within.

**vartin** - existing, situated, occurring, from √*vṛt*.

The compound signifies something existing within that previously described reality.

The referent is the cosmic structure encompassed by the mantra's powers.

It is not simply a physical interior.

The phrase concerns metaphysical inclusion within the supreme heart.

### Yat kiṃcit

Whatever.

Anything whatsoever.

The indefinite expression is expansive.

The entire range of differentiated manifestation is being considered.

The emphasis is not on one special ritual object.

It is on the scope of the conscious reality.

### Śuddhamārga - शुद्धमार्ग

**śuddha** - pure, purified.

**mārga** - path, way, course, domain.

Here the term belongs to the technical Śaiva account of the pure levels of manifestation.

It must not be flattened into a vague moral compliment such as “the nice spiritual path.”

The *śuddhādhvan*, or pure path, has a technical role in Śaiva cosmology.

The relevant discussion concerns the levels of manifestation encompassed by the supreme heart.

### The little subject: aṇu

**अणुः - aṇuḥ**

Small, minute, atomic, or the finite individual subject in the technical context.

In Trika, the *aṇu* is the contracted subject.

The individual who experiences limited knowledge and action.

The being who takes its restricted mode of manifestation as its ultimate nature.

And now the verse says that this *aṇu* attains **aiśvaraṃ jñānam**.

Sovereign knowledge.

Knowledge belonging to the Lord.

This is the metaphysical drama.

The finite subject does not need to grow physically until it fills the universe.

It is not supposed to acquire a new gigantic cosmic body.

The recognition concerns the ultimate identity of the subject with consciousness itself.

The contraction is overcome at the level of self-understanding.

### Abhinavagupta's strange explanation of aṇu

He writes:

**अण्यते प्राणिति अणति नदति**

*aṇyate prāṇiti aṇati nadati.*

A compressed cluster of verbal explanations involving breathing, sounding, and movement.

This is a traditional doctrinal *nirvacana* of *aṇu*.

It connects the finite subject with the restricted expression of vitality and sound.

The word **prāṇiti** is related to √*an*, to breathe, with the prefix *pra-*.

**nadati** means “sounds” or “resounds,” from √*nad*.

Abhinavagupta is investigating the finite subject through the movement of vital and expressive power.

But these explanations should not all be treated as secure historical derivations of the noun *aṇu*.

He is using linguistic analysis as a theological method.

### The retroflex nasal

He also invokes **mūrdhanya**, the retroflex or cerebral articulation.

This concerns the sound **ण - ṇa**.

The consonant is not identical with dental **न - na**.

The distinction matters in the very word:

**अणु - aṇu**

The nasal is retroflex ṇ.

Not dental n.

Do not write *anu* when the intended word is *aṇu*.

Because *anu* is a different Sanskrit element, frequently used as a prefix or preposition.

Again, one little dot in IAST can preserve a meaningful linguistic distinction.

The absolute may be undivided, but your transliteration still needs to know which nasal it is using.

### Acirāt - अचिरात्

Soon, without delay, before long.

From *a-* \+ *cira*, long-lasting or prolonged, with an adverbial ablative formation.

The text stresses the immediacy or rapidity of attainment.

This connects with the repeated theme of *sadyaḥ*, immediate recognition.

But the grammar does not prove that a person's psychological habits are instantly transformed in every possible respect.

The claim concerns the attainment described by the text.

Its doctrinal setting remains important.

### Aiśvaraṃ jñānam

**aiśvara** - belonging to Īśvara, lordly, sovereign, divine.

**jñāna** - knowledge.

Both appear in the accusative singular neuter.

They are governed by the verb:

**अश्नुते - aśnute**

“He reaches,” “attains,” “obtains.”

A middle-voice present form of √*aś*, to reach or attain.

Do not automatically translate it as “eats” because another Sanskrit verbal family has associations with eating.

The context is the attainment of knowledge.

Thus:

*aṇur ... aiśvaraṃ jñānam aśnute.*

“The finite subject attains sovereign knowledge.”

The subject is the *aṇu*.

The object of attainment is *jñāna*.

And the character of the knowledge is *aiśvara*.

The grammar is precise.

### The metre

| Pāda | Division | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *tadantarvarti yat kiñcit* | ta-dan-tar-var-ti-yat-kiñ-cit | 8 |
| *śuddhamārge vyavasthitam* | śud-dha-mār-ge-vya-vas-thi-tam | 8 |
| *aṇur viśuddham acirāt* | a-ṇur-vi-śud-dham-a-ci-rāt | 8 |
| *aiśvaraṃ jñānam aśnute* | ai-śva-raṃ-jñā-nam-aś-nu-te | 8 |

Here **ai** is one diphthongal vowel, counted as one long syllable.

In **ऐश्वरम् - aiśvaram**, the independent vowel ऐ is not a sequence of two separately pronounced syllables.

And **अश्नुते - aśnute** contains the conjunct श्न, composed of श् + न.

The metre remains regular.

### The especially important editorial bracket

The 1918 KSTS edition now supplies an extended square-bracketed interpretation of the differences between pure cognition and supreme agency.

The editor discusses how a condition may possess pure knowledge without the complete sovereignty associated with Śiva.

He cites other philosophical material and draws a distinction between forms of knowledge described as *brahmajñāna* and *śivajñāna*.

This material is intellectually relevant, especially for the distinction between the limited subject, the *vijñānākala* condition, and the supreme Śiva state.

But it is an **editorial insertion**, not Abhinavagupta's uninterrupted prose. The continuous GRETIL text moves from the brief explanation of the *aṇu* and the attainment of divine knowledge directly to the next transition, **katham**. ; \]

This is a critical distinction.

Otherwise we would be inserting a substantial additional doctrinal discussion into Abhinavagupta's argument and pretending that he wrote it at this point.

The editor's interpretation may be informative.

But the attribution would be false.

### What is Abhinavagupta's actual argument?

It is compact.

The heart-seed encompasses the differentiated realities.

The finite subject is a restricted manifestation of consciousness.

Through the supreme power, that subject can attain the sovereign knowledge of its ground.

The central insight is not that the finite ego becomes a new independent omniscient entity.

It is that the limitation of subjectivity is not ultimate.

The ground of awareness is the same supreme consciousness through which differentiated reality is manifested.

And the recognition of that ground transforms the subject's status.

Now the text asks:

**कथम् - katham?**

“How?”

And then immediately returns us to the guru.

Just when we thought we'd successfully escaped the entire initiation system, the commentator brings the teacher back into the room.

But this time the teacher is not simply another person.

The teacher is Śiva.

And the Sanskrit is about to expose a beautiful difference between something that can be known as an object and the reality of the knower.


## XIII. Verse 23: The Guru Is Śiva, but Śiva Is Not an Object You Can Put Under a Microscope

### KSTS p. 257 - Gnoli p. 275

Abhinavagupta introduces the next root verse with a single question:

**कथम् - katham?**

“How?”

And the tantra answers:

**तच्चोदकः शिवो ज्ञेयः सर्वज्ञः परमेश्वरः ।**

*taccodakaḥ śivo jñeyaḥ sarvajñaḥ parameśvaraḥ.*

**सर्वगो निर्मलः स्वच्छस्तृप्तः स्वायतनः शुचिः ॥२३॥**

*sarvago nirmalaḥ svacchas tṛptaḥ svāyatanaḥ śuciḥ.*

My translation, following the KSTS and GRETIL root reading:

“The one who impels or teaches this is to be understood as Śiva: the all-knowing Supreme Lord, all-pervading, stainless, transparent, fulfilled, established in his own domain, and pure.”

And now the guru problem resolves itself.

Not by getting rid of the guru.

But by asking what the guru ultimately is.

### Taccodakaḥ - तच्चोदकः

This is the decisive term.

**tat** - that, referring to the knowledge or supreme principle under discussion.

**codaka** - one who impels, prompts, directs, or instructs, connected with √*cud*, in its causative family, to urge or impel.

Thus:

“The one who impels toward that.”

The teacher who brings about the disclosure.

And the text identifies this figure with Śiva.

Abhinavagupta says:

**यस्तच्चोदको गुरुः स शिव एव ज्ञेयः**

*yas taccodako guruḥ sa śiva eva jñeyaḥ.*

“The guru who impels toward that is indeed to be understood as Śiva.”

This is not simply a statement that every person claiming to be a guru is automatically omniscient.

The identification concerns the *taccodaka*-the one who genuinely directs the subject toward supreme knowledge.

Its theological significance depends on that function.

A person who deceives or exploits students is not vindicated by merely adopting the title “guru.”

Abhinavagupta's philosophical claim concerns the divine reality operating through genuine instruction.

### The text's astonishing alternative reading

Here we encounter one of the most important variants in this instalment.

The main KSTS and GRETIL reading has:

**शिवो ज्ञेयः - śivo jñeyaḥ**

“Śiva is to be known.”

But an independently transmitted root reading has:

**शिवोऽज्ञेयः - śivo 'jñeyaḥ**

“Śiva is not knowable.”

The SanskritDocuments collation explicitly reports this alternative, while the KSTS and GRETIL commentary tradition follows the *jñeyaḥ* reading in the root verse. ; ; \]

Now this is an extraordinary case.

Because the difference turns on the privative prefix *a-*.

**jñeya** - knowable, something to be known.

**ajñeya** - unknowable, not knowable.

And here's the really interesting part.

Under ordinary Sanskrit sandhi:

*śivaḥ + jñeyaḥ → śivo jñeyaḥ.*

But:

*śivaḥ + ajñeyaḥ → śivo 'jñeyaḥ.*

The avagraha distinguishes the second interpretation in writing.

The pronunciation after sandhi is effectively identical.

And the metre cannot tell us which reading is earlier.

The written representation carries a semantic distinction that the realised phonetic sequence does not fully disambiguate.

We have just spent two instalments discussing whether phonemes carry meaning intrinsically.

And the manuscript tradition has now supplied a beautiful real-life problem involving semantic ambiguity through sandhi.

Abhinavagupta would probably have enjoyed that.

### Does the commentary settle it?

Not entirely.

Because immediately after identifying the teacher with Śiva, Abhinavagupta writes:

**स चाज्ञेयो ज्ञातैव**

*sa cājñeyo jñātaiva.*

“And he is not knowable $$as an object$$; he is the knower alone.”

This is the key philosophical distinction.

The supreme consciousness cannot be treated as just another object of knowledge.

Why?

Because every object is manifest through awareness.

If awareness itself had to be known as an object by another awareness, one could ask what makes that second awareness manifest.

And so on.

An infinite regress threatens.

Pratyabhijñā understands supreme consciousness as intrinsically self-manifesting.

It does not require a second consciousness to illuminate it.

This is connected with the distinction between **prakāśa** and **vimarśa**.

*Prakāśa* - luminosity, manifestation, the fact of conscious appearing.

*Vimarśa* - reflexive apprehension, consciousness's self-awareness.

The supreme subject is not an inert object illuminated from outside.

Its manifestness includes self-awareness.

Thus Śiva can be **known through recognition** without becoming an ordinary **object** standing opposite an independent knower.

And that is why both readings are philosophically intelligible.

“Śiva is to be known” can mean that the guru's ultimate nature must be recognised as Śiva.

“Śiva is unknowable” can mean that the supreme subject is not an object of ordinary cognition.

The commentary articulates both dimensions.

But philosophical compatibility does not establish that both words were present in an original manuscript.

We must preserve the textual difference.

### The danger of confusing the subject with an object

Suppose someone asks:

“Where is consciousness?”

You could point to a brain.

You could study the neural correlates of conscious experience.

You could investigate the relationship between physiological processes and subjective reports.

Those are legitimate empirical enquiries.

But Abhinavagupta is investigating a different philosophical question.

What is the status of the awareness through which anything is experienced at all?

The subjective character of manifestation cannot be described simply by placing another observed object inside the field of awareness.

An object can appear.

Its features can be distinguished.

Its changes can be measured.

But the experiencing of that object raises a question about the nature of awareness itself.

Abhinavagupta's answer is supreme self-luminous consciousness.

That is his metaphysical position; it is not an experimentally demonstrated conclusion about the relationship between consciousness and neural activity.

Yet the philosophical problem is genuine.

A theory that describes only objects must still account for the conscious appearing of those objects.

And the Pratyabhijñā tradition makes that problem central.

### Sarvajñaḥ - सर्वज्ञः

All-knowing.

**sarva** - all.

**jña** - knowing.

The word qualifies Parameśvara.

The text attributes universal knowledge to the supreme Lord.

Again, this is not a claim that an ordinary empirical ego can access every fact on demand.

The supreme subject is being understood as the ground of all manifestation.

The relationship between that metaphysical omniscience and specific siddhis is not trivial.

Abhinavagupta has already distinguished the ultimate nature of consciousness from the conditions governing particular accomplishments.

### Sarvagaḥ - सर्वगः

All-pervading.

From *sarva*, all, and *ga*, going or present, connected with √*gam*.

The supreme consciousness is not located in a particular region of the universe.

It is the ground of the universe's appearing.

And thus the teacher identified with Śiva is not simply a physically localised individual considered in isolation.

The teacher's liberating function is understood as a manifestation of the universal divine power.

### Nirmalaḥ - निर्मलः

Without stain.

From *nir-* \+ *mala*, impurity, stain, obscuration.

In the tantric philosophical context, *mala* is a technical term for limiting impurity.

The supreme Lord is not subject to the contraction that makes the individual misrecognise their nature.

### Svacchaḥ - स्वच्छः

Clear, transparent, pure.

The word evokes the absence of obstruction or opacity.

This has an obvious resonance with the metaphor of consciousness as luminosity.

But do not simply interpret it as a claim that consciousness has a literal optical transparency.

It is a philosophical and theological description.

### Tṛptaḥ - तृप्तः

Satisfied, fulfilled.

A past passive participial form related to √*tṛp*, to be satisfied.

We have returned to the theme of fullness.

At the beginning of Part XXX, the guru was described as **hṛṣṭacetas**, delighted in mind.

Now the supreme Lord is **tṛpta**, fulfilled.

The distinction matters.

An ordinary person may be satisfied because a desire has been fulfilled.

The supreme consciousness, in Trika, is understood as intrinsically full.

Its nature is not dependent upon acquiring an external object.

That is the deeper sense in which the supreme subject possesses *pūrṇatā*, fullness.

### Svāyatanaḥ - स्वायतनः

Having one's own abode, ground, domain, or support.

The term is particularly interesting because Abhinavagupta supplies a doctrinal interpretation.

He connects the word with the manifestation of forms of awareness.

The key wording is:

**स्वान् अयान् विज्ञानरूपान् भावांस्तनोति**

*svān ayān vijñānarūpān bhāvāṃs tanoti.*

“He extends or manifests his own movements or courses, the states whose nature is cognition.”

This is another example of tantric *nirvacana*.

The compound is interpreted through the divine subject's capacity to manifest its own cognitive forms.

But the ordinary lexical sense of *āyatana* need not be erased.

The theological interpretation is an additional layer.

### Śuciḥ - शुचिः

Pure, bright, radiant.

The final adjective reiterates the purity of the divine subject.

The sequence of epithets is significant.

All-knowing.

All-pervading.

Unstained.

Clear.

Fulfilled.

Grounded in itself.

Pure.

These are not seven unrelated possessions of a separate object.

They articulate the status of the supreme consciousness that serves as the ground of the guru's true function.

And the entire passage now returns to the beginning of our question.

What if a person has never seen the maṇḍala?

They may nevertheless know the supreme heart.

What if they have never received the formal rite?

The supreme Lord may itself be the source of their awakening.

What if they have never acquired the outward qualifications of a particular tradition?

The real question is whether *tattvataḥ vetti* has occurred.

And what is the source of the genuine knowledge?

The self-revealing consciousness of Bhairava.

The guru is not eliminated.

The guru is understood at the ultimate level.

### The final metrical examination

The two halves scan as follows:

| Pāda | Syllables | Count |
|--------|--------|--------|
| *taccodakaḥ śivo jñeyaḥ* | tac-co-da-kaḥ-śi-vo-jñe-yaḥ | 8 |
| *sarvajñaḥ parameśvaraḥ* | sar-va-jñaḥ-pa-ra-me-śva-raḥ | 8 |
| *sarvago nirmalaḥ svacchaḥ* | sar-va-go-nir-ma-laḥ-svac-chaḥ | 8 |
| *tṛptaḥ svāyatanaḥ śuciḥ* | tṛp-taḥ-svā-ya-ta-naḥ-śu-ciḥ | 8 |

For the final pāda, notice **तृप्तः - tṛptaḥ**.

The consonant cluster प्त is प् + त.

And the syllabic ṛ in *tṛp* counts as the vowel nucleus.

In **स्वायतनः**, the sequence स्व is स् + व, with the long ā following.

In **स्वच्छः**, the word begins with the cluster स् + व, and contains the geminated affricate sequence represented by च्छ.

This is a useful case where reading every graphic component as a separate syllable would destroy the metre.

The root text is metrically regular.

And, importantly, **both *jñeyaḥ* and *ajñeyaḥ* fit the same metre after sandhi**.

The scansion does not resolve the manuscript problem.

That must be decided through textual evidence and interpretation, not through wishful metrical reconstruction.


## XIV. The Great Return: Why the Teacher, the Teaching, and the Student Are One Without Becoming Meaningless

We have reached the end of the commentary on verse 23.

But before moving to the next authentic transition, consider how the entire argument has unfolded.

At first, initiation seemed to require an external teacher and a prescribed ritual.

Then the root text declared that one who truly knows the supreme heart is already initiated, even without seeing the maṇḍala.

Abhinavagupta explained that the ultimate condition is the descent of divine grace.

Then he identified real knowledge with initiation.

Then he distinguished the supreme heart from ordinary self-acquired mantras.

Then he dismissed birth, vows, and prescribed conduct as universal prerequisites for this highest recognition.

Then he identified genuine yoga with unity.

Then he described initiation as the bestowal of knowledge and the removal of limitation.

Then he explained how the practitioner becomes known by the Śaktis and can be identified with the common principle underlying the differentiated powers.

Then he argued that the supreme heart makes even incomplete worship complete in its ultimate significance.

And finally he returned to the guru.

But not as an independent external authority standing between a powerless individual and a remote God.

The teacher's true function is a manifestation of Śiva.

The ultimate power of initiation is the liberating disclosure of consciousness.

The entire process belongs to the supreme Self.

### The highest meaning of initiation

Consider the ordinary understanding of an initiation.

Before the ceremony, a person lacks a particular religious status.

After the ceremony, that status has been conferred.

This is perfectly intelligible within a ritual institution.

A community can establish conditions of membership.

A teacher can transmit practices.

A ceremony can mark a real change in social and religious relationships.

But Abhinavagupta distinguishes this from the ultimate sense of initiation.

If liberation is recognition of universal consciousness, then no external rite can manufacture that consciousness.

Nor can the rite make it more universal.

Nor can the guru transfer it as if it were an object.

The highest initiation must therefore be understood through **the removal of misrecognition**.

And that does not necessarily require every conventional external condition.

### Why this isn't spiritual laziness

It would be extremely easy to misread the passage.

“Wonderful. No guru required. No mantra required. No meditation required. No ritual required. I am already Śiva. Time to open a spiritual teaching business.”

But that confuses a metaphysical proposition with realised understanding.

The root verse says *vetti tattvataḥ*.

The person knows according to reality.

Abhinavagupta explicitly connects that knowledge with *paraśaktipāta*.

He is not describing a person who has merely adopted a religious opinion.

A belief can be asserted.

A proposition can be repeated.

A philosophical system can be memorised.

But the deeper transformation concerns the way the subject actually apprehends itself and its relation to manifestation.

It is possible to know the sentences of Pratyabhijñā while continuing to identify entirely with the limited empirical ego.

The distinction between conceptual familiarity and transformative recognition is not erased.

And so the passage does not render serious practice or learning irrelevant.

It gives them their proper place.

They can help disclose a reality that is not themselves a product of their activity.

### Why this isn't ritual absolutism either

Now take the opposite view.

“Unless you have undergone precisely my recognised ceremony, you cannot possess ultimate knowledge.”

The verse directly challenges that assertion.

The person who has not seen the maṇḍala may nevertheless be a genuine initiate.

The limiting social and ritual classifications do not govern the highest recognition.

The supreme consciousness is not the possession of an institution.

It is not a substance distributed through a restricted chain of human ownership.

Its freedom includes the possibility of spontaneous disclosure.

And that is why the highest teaching cannot be reduced to an external rite.

### The significance for Pratyabhijñā

The word **pratyabhijñā** means recognition.

In ordinary experience, recognition concerns identifying something already encountered.

You see someone.

At first you fail to recognise them.

Then suddenly you realise who they are.

The person did not become that person at the moment of recognition.

The recognition reveals an identity that was already the case.

In the supreme case, the analogy reaches its limit.

The recognised reality is not a separate object encountered by a second subject.

It is the nature of the subject itself.

The limited subject recognises its identity with universal consciousness.

The knowing is therefore reflexive.

The supreme subject is not objectified as something standing elsewhere.

It is the ground of all knowing.

And the recognition is the disclosure of that ground.

That is why the teacher can be Śiva.

That is why the knowledge can be initiation.

That is why the supreme mantra can be the heart.

And that is why someone who has never seen the ritual maṇḍala can nevertheless possess the highest meaning of initiation.

**The ceremony may introduce the person to the truth. But the truth is not produced by the ceremony.**

And now Abhinavagupta has reached a natural conclusion.

He has explained the powers of the supreme heart.

He has explained the conditions of recognition.

He has explained how differentiated religious means relate to ultimate knowledge.

He has identified the teacher with Śiva.

And now he announces that he is going to summarise the entire argument.

Not through another extraordinary list of cosmic spheres.

Not through another grammatical investigation of a single consonant.

But through a seed.

A tiny seed.

Containing an enormous tree.

And through that image, the entire manifest universe.


## XV. The Next Authentic Transition: The Banyan Seed and the Initiation That Needs No Fire Offering

### KSTS p. 258 - Gnoli p. 275

The next sentence is unmistakable:

**एवं विस्तरशोऽभिधाय तात्पर्येण निगमयति ।**

*evaṃ vistaraśo 'bhidhāya tātparyeṇa nigamayati.*

“Having thus explained at length, he now brings the matter to its conclusion according to its essential purport.”

This is **the next substantial authenticated transition**, and the natural stopping point for Part XXXI.

Notice **nigamayati**.

It means to conclude, summarise, establish by way of conclusion.

In this case, the preceding elaborate argument is about to be compressed into a single image.

Abhinavagupta introduces the next verses:

**यथा न्यग्रोधबीजस्थः शक्तिरूपो महाद्रुमः ।**

*yathā nyagrodhabījasthaḥ śaktirūpo mahādrumaḥ.*

“Just as a great tree exists in the banyan seed in the form of power…”

Then:

**तथा हृदयबीजस्थं जगदेतच्चराचरम् ॥**

*tathā hṛdayabījasthaṃ jagad etac carācaram.*

“So this entire moving and unmoving world exists within the seed of the heart.”

And the following verse announces that genuine knowledge of this relation constitutes an initiation leading to liberation, without the conventional offerings of sesame and clarified butter.

This is the extraordinary conclusion we've been approaching across the entire work. ; \]

But the seed analogy deserves its own careful reading.

Because it raises one of the deepest problems in the entire metaphysics.

How can the entire universe be present within something that is not spatially large enough to contain it?

What does it mean to say that the banyan tree is already in the seed?

And what does that analogy establish about the relationship between consciousness and manifestation?

If the universe is contained within the heart, is it contained potentially?

Actually?

As differentiated powers?

Or in a condition where the distinction between potential and actual has not yet emerged?

The Sanskrit will matter enormously.

And so will the difference between a biological seed and the metaphysical *hṛdayabīja*.

Because Abhinavagupta is not claiming that there is a physically tiny universe folded up inside your chest.

He is making a much more sophisticated claim about how differentiation arises from an undivided ground.

And that is exactly where Part XXXII must begin.


## Closing: The Initiation That Never Had to Happen

Return to the original question.

What if you've never seen the maṇḍala?

What if you haven't received the secret instruction?

What if you don't possess the required ritual qualifications?

What if you have no impressive lineage certificates?

What if your Sanskrit is imperfect?

What if you've never experienced a spectacular vision?

What if the elaborate machinery of initiation has simply never been made available to you?

Abhinavagupta's answer is not that those differences never matter.

They may matter within particular religious practices.

They may matter for the acquisition of particular skills.

They may matter for the transmission of specialised mantras.

But **they cannot be absolute obstacles to recognition of the supreme reality**.

Because that reality is not manufactured by any of them.

The ultimate subject was never absent.

The conscious light by which the ceremony would be perceived was already present before the ceremony.

The awareness through which the mantra would be heard was already present before the mantra.

The power through which the teacher would be recognised was already present before meeting the teacher.

The capacity for understanding was not created from nothing by the words of instruction.

And the supreme consciousness, in Abhinavagupta's system, is not the property of any restricted human institution.

The teacher may reveal it.

The scripture may explain it.

The mantra may articulate it.

The ritual may symbolise it.

The discipline may prepare the subject for recognition.

But the reality recognised is not an object acquired from elsewhere.

It is the ground of the entire process.

This is why *śaktipāta* is so important.

Grace is the disclosure of the supreme nature through consciousness's own freedom.

And this is why the most radical form of initiation can occur without the external ritual.

Not because the individual ego has decided to promote itself to divinity.

But because the universal consciousness has become manifest to itself through the contracted subject.

And now the supreme joke reveals itself.

You were searching for consciousness.

You used consciousness to conduct the search.

You studied Sanskrit.

You used consciousness to understand the Sanskrit.

You contemplated Śiva.

You used consciousness to contemplate Śiva.

You examined your thoughts, sensations, doubts, memories, desires, and acts of recognition.

Every single one appeared within the field of awareness.

And after the entire investigation, the question is whether the aware reality in which the investigation occurred was ever fundamentally separate from what you were looking for.

According to Abhinavagupta, it wasn't.

You could spend an entire lifetime attempting to reach the supreme subject as though it were a distant object.

But the very act of attempting already occurs within consciousness.

The seeker appears within it.

The search appears within it.

The imagined distance appears within it.

And the recognition that the distance was not ultimate also appears within it.

Which is why the highest initiation is not simply an event that turns a nondivine substance into a divine one.

It is the disclosure of the nature that was never absent.

The ritual might help.

The teacher might help.

The scripture might help.

But the reality to be recognised is the condition of their helping at all.

And then comes the final absurdity.

You could read a thousand pages explaining how consciousness is the ground of everything.

You could memorise every Sanskrit quotation.

You could master the distinction between *prakāśa* and *vimarśa*, the structure of the thirty-six tattvas, and the exact phonemic arrangement of the Mātṛkā.

And you could still fail to recognise the subject through which the entire performance was intelligible.

A magnificent philosophical achievement.

The universe has spent several billion years developing the conditions under which a human being can argue about consciousness.

And the human being may still conclude:

“Very interesting. But where exactly is this awareness thing?”

Abhinavagupta's answer is the most devastatingly simple one imaginable.

**Who is asking?**
