# Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa - Part XXXIII

Abhinavagupta's Absolutely Unhinged Guide to the Universe

## “The Supreme Lord Has Five Heads, Twenty-Seven Mantras, Ten Directions, and Absolutely No Intention of Letting You Escape the Alphabet.”

*The great ritual reversal: how worship becomes the recognition of consciousness, how the entire universe becomes an offering, and why the guy who told you that liberation requires no fire ceremony is now explaining a fire ceremony.*

### Previously, on *Abhinavagupta Has Apparently Declared War on His Own Ritual Manual*

We have reached the final great movement of the *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa*.

For thirty-two instalments, this magnificent Kashmiri philosophical menace has been dismantling the ordinary assumption that consciousness is a little thing inside your head, peering out at a universe of independently existing objects.

He has explained that the world is the manifestation of Śiva's freedom.

That the Sanskrit phonemes can be understood as differentiated powers of consciousness.

That **सौः - sauḥ**, the supreme Heart-mantra, expresses the relation between undivided reality and cosmic manifestation.

That initiation, *dīkṣā*, is in its highest meaning the recognition of what you already are.

And, most recently, that the whole moving and unmoving universe exists within the seed of the supreme Heart, just as an enormous banyan tree is present in its seed in the form of generative power.

Then came the glorious conclusion:

**Whoever knows this truly possesses an initiation leading to liberation, without offerings of sesame and clarified butter.**

No external sacrificial fire required for the highest recognition.

Excellent.

We have transcended ritual.

We have transcended the necessity of external religious qualifications.

We have discovered the supreme Self.

We are free.

And then Abhinavagupta turns the page.

“Right. Now put something on your head, face, chest, and secret parts. Bind the topknot. Do twenty-seven repetitions. Seal the ten directions. Clap three times. Sprinkle the flowers. Arrange the altar. Worship the Goddess. Offer everything into the fire.”

**EXCUSE ME?**

Sir, you've just told us that consciousness is absolutely free.

Why does the omnipresent Lord apparently require a twenty-seven-point checklist?

And how can worship possibly work in a philosophy that denies any ultimate separation between worshipper and worshipped?

That is what makes this passage extraordinary.

Abhinavagupta does not simply abandon the ritual instructions of the root tantra. Nor does he retreat from his nonduality.

He interprets the ritual itself as a manifestation of the Heart.

**The ritual is no longer merely something a limited person performs to reach Śiva. It becomes a way of recognising how Śiva's own powers constitute the person, the action, the offering, and the deity.**

That is the argument we're about to follow.

## I. The textual terrain: seven verses that aren't numbered the same everywhere

We begin at **KSTS XVIII, printed p. 261**, with the continuous root passage running through p. 262. Abhinavagupta's prose explanation then extends through approximately p. 268. GRETIL uses the pagination of Gnoli's 1985 edition, beginning around p. 276 for this ritual section.

One immediate philological complication: the independent *Parātriṃśikā* transcription encoded by Marino Faliero arranges the verse boundaries differently from the 1918 commentary edition. The root passage is largely the same, but the KSTS verse 26 begins with *mūrdhni*, whereas the independent witness places that half-verse at the end of its verse 26 and begins its next verse with *nyāsaṃ kṛtvā*. The numbering remains displaced through the ritual block.

There are also substantive differences, not merely shifted numbers. ; ; \]

| Passage | KSTS commentary reading | Independent root reading |
|--------|--------|--------|
| Direction-binding | *ekaikaṃ tu … yojayet* | *ekaikena … kārayet* |
| Ritual location | *liṅge vā sthaṇḍile 'tha vā* | *liṅgaṃ vā sthaṇḍilaṃ ca vā* |
| Goddess's adornment | *sarvābharaṇabhūṣitām* | *sarvāvayavaśobhitām* |
| Goddess addressed or described | *devīṃ maheśānīm* | *devīṃ mahābhāgā* |
| Flowers | *sugandhipuṣpais tu* | *sugandhipuṣpaiś ca* |
| Self-offering | KSTS electronic *ātmanāṃ* | independent *svātmanaṃ* |

The final line is particularly troublesome: the digitised KSTS form *ātmanāṃ* does not give the expected accusative object of “offer.” A Sanskrit form resembling *ātmānaṃ* is grammatically expected, while the independent root tradition transmits its own variant. We'll keep the uncertainty explicit rather than pretending the witnesses agree.

For the narrative, the **1918 commentary's verse sequence governs the order**. The independent text provides checks and variants, not a licence to reconstruct a different composition.


## II. Verse 26: Put the Universe on Your Head. Then Put It on Your Face. Then Realise There Was Never a Second Universe.

### KSTS p. 261 - root tantra

**मूर्ध्नि वक्त्रे च हृदये गुह्ये मूर्तौ तथैव च ।**

*mūrdhni vaktre ca hṛdaye guhye mūrtau tathaiva ca.*

**न्यासं कृत्वा शिखां बद्ध्वा सप्तविंशतिमन्त्रिताम् ॥२६॥**

*nyāsaṃ kṛtvā śikhāṃ baddhvā saptaviṃśatimantritām.*

My literal translation:

“Having performed the placement on the head, face, heart, secret region, and bodily form likewise, and having bound the topknot empowered by twenty-seven recitations…”

The sentence continues through the next ritual instructions.

And that is important.

We are reading an extended series of absolutives and prescriptions, not seven unrelated aphorisms.

The root tantra is describing a procedure.

The ritualist is instructed to place mantras on different parts of the body, bind the *śikhā*, and proceed through a series of protective and worshipful actions.

The external meaning is perfectly intelligible within a tantric ritual setting.

But Abhinavagupta now takes each location and asks:

**What does this body part represent when understood through the supreme consciousness?**

Because the body is not being treated as an inert object entirely separate from Śiva.

It is a differentiated manifestation of the powers of the Lord.

And the process of placing mantras upon it is interpreted as an act of recognition.

### First, the grammar

**मूर्ध्नि - mūrdhni**

Locative singular of *mūrdhan*, head or crown.

Not *mūrdhe*.

Not *mūrdhane*.

The actual form is **mūrdhni**, with a consonantal cluster reflecting the inflection of the *-an* stem.

In Devanāgarī, **र्ध्न** contains the sequence र् + ध् + न. The repha represents the preceding *r*, and the remaining conjunct holds *dh* and *n* together without intermediate inherent vowels.

**वक्त्रे - vaktre**

Locative singular of *vaktra*, face or mouth.

The word is related to the verbal family of √*vac*, to speak, through a formation involving an instrumental or agentive suffix.

The consonant cluster **क्त्र** represents क् + त् + र.

The root text is placing a mantra at the site of speech.

That will matter when Abhinavagupta relates the body to the powers of expressive consciousness.

**हृदये - hṛdaye**

Locative singular of *hṛdaya*, heart.

The vowel ऋ appears in its dependent form beneath ह.

It is vocalic *ṛ*, not an ordinary consonant *r* followed by *i*.

And in this commentary, *hṛdaya* has already acquired a technical theological significance far exceeding the physical organ.

**गुह्ये - guhye**

Locative singular of *guhya*, hidden, secret, private.

In this bodily sequence, it designates the secret or genital region.

The word is related to √*guh*, to conceal.

We should not euphemise the anatomical reference away, but neither should we reduce its theological interpretation to sexuality alone.

The commentary will associate the bodily locations with powers of manifestation.

**मूर्तौ - mūrtau**

Locative singular of the feminine *mūrti*, form, embodiment, manifestation.

This is a **-i** stem with locative **-au**.

The alternation is worth learning. Compare *śakti → śaktau* in the locative singular.

The text therefore moves from particular bodily locations to embodied form.

**न्यासम् - nyāsam**

Accusative singular of *nyāsa*, placing, depositing, assignment, installation.

From *ni-* \+ √*as*, to throw or place, in the relevant derivational sense.

In tantric ritual, *nyāsa* refers to the placement or installation of mantric or divine powers upon the body or another locus.

**कृत्वा - kṛtvā**

The absolutive of √*kṛ*, to do.

“Having performed.”

**बद्ध्वा - baddhvā**

Absolutive of √*bandh*, to bind.

“Having bound.”

Both are subordinate actions preceding the later finite prescription.

**शिखाम् - śikhām**

Accusative singular feminine of *śikhā*.

A crest, tuft, topknot, or peak.

In the ritual setting, the *śikhā* can be an element of the practitioner’s bodily and mantric configuration.

**सप्तविंशतिमन्त्रिताम् - saptaviṃśatimantritām**

A feminine accusative singular compound qualifying *śikhām*.

**sapta** - seven.

**viṃśati** - twenty.

**saptaviṃśati** - twenty-seven.

**mantrita** - empowered or consecrated through mantra, from the verbal denominative family associated with *mantra*.

Thus the *śikhā* is characterised as having undergone twenty-seven mantric repetitions or consecrations.

Why twenty-seven?

Abhinavagupta's explanation is astonishing.

But first he introduces the **five Brahmas**.


## III. The Five Brahmas: Why One Śiva Apparently Needs Five Departments

### KSTS pp. 262-263 - Abhinavagupta's commentary

The commentary begins by distinguishing the external bodily places from their ultimate significance.

The head, face, heart, secret region, and embodied form possess appropriate external ritual meanings.

But in reality, he says, they are manifestations of the fivefold divine structure, encompassing the levels from space down to earth and the corresponding powers of consciousness.

He then supplies five short mantra designations:

## १

**ईशानमूर्ध्ने**

*īśānamūrdhne*

“To Īśāna, associated with the head.”

## २

**तत्पुरुषवक्त्राय**

*tatpuruṣavaktrāya*

“To Tatpuruṣa, associated with the face.”

## ३

**अघोरहृदयाय**

*aghorahṛdayāya*

“To Aghora, associated with the heart.”

## ४

**वामदेवगुह्याय**

*vāmadevaguhyāya*

“To Vāmadeva, associated with the secret region.”

## ५

**सद्योजातमूर्तये**

*sadyojātamūrtaye*

“To Sadyojāta, associated with embodied form.”

These are the actual abbreviated designations in the commentary-not complete independently reconstructed five-faced Śiva mantras with syllables supplied from some other ritual manual. ; ; \]

Now consider what Abhinavagupta is doing.

A normal ritualist has five locations on the body.

A normal exegete might say: place the five mantras at the corresponding locations, recite the required formulae, and continue.

Abhinavagupta says: the five locations themselves express the cosmic fivefoldness of consciousness.

The **external body** is a ritual configuration.

But the **ultimate body** is the manifestation of the supreme Śakti.

The mantras are therefore not simply labels attached to independent pieces of flesh.

They express the identity of bodily manifestation with divine power.

### Īśāna

Connected with lordship and ruling, from the verbal family of √*īś*, to rule or possess power.

In broader Śaiva theology, Īśāna is associated with the supreme or upward-facing aspect.

Here the immediate placement is the head.

**mūrdhne** has a dative singular ending, reflecting the noun *mūrdhan*.

It means “to the head” or “for the head” within the mantric designation.

The compound is not merely a nominative label.

It has the inflectional form of a ritual address.

### Tatpuruṣa

Literally “that person,” with *tat* and *puruṣa*.

The term has a long history in Śaiva theology and in the five-Brahma system.

Here it belongs to the face.

The form **vaktrāya** is dative singular of *vaktra*.

The face is the site of expression, especially speech.

Given the entire commentary's theory of phonemic manifestation, that association is philosophically suggestive, although Abhinavagupta does not present a separate lengthy derivation of every name at this point.

### Aghora

**a-ghora** - not terrible, not fearsome.

But this name must be understood within its theological context.

The absence of terror is not simply a personality description.

In Śaiva theology, the terrible and non-terrible aspects belong to the divine power's relation to manifestation, dissolution, and grace.

Here Aghora is associated with the heart.

**hṛdayāya** is dative singular.

The phonological sequence *hṛ* contains vocalic ṛ.

### Vāmadeva

The word *vāma* can mean left, beautiful, agreeable, or otherwise favourable depending on context.

**deva** means god or luminous divine being.

The compound denotes a recognised aspect of Śiva.

The short designation here associates it with the secret region.

This is not a justification for assigning arbitrary additional sexual symbolism absent from the passage.

The location belongs to the fivefold bodily nyāsa, which the commentary integrates into the cosmic system.

### Sadyojāta

**sadyaḥ** - immediately.

**jāta** - born, manifested, from √*jan*.

The compound means “immediately born” or “immediately arisen.”

The short mantra associates it with **mūrti**, embodied form.

The dative singular **mūrtaye** is from the feminine *-i* stem.

And the theological significance is clear: manifested form is not separable from the power of consciousness.

### The deeper fivefoldness

Abhinavagupta relates the five bodily locations to the fivefold powers of the divine.

His prose includes the progression of consciousness through such expressions as:

**cit - consciousness.**

**unmeṣa - opening or emergence.**

**icchā - will.**

**jñāna - knowledge.**

**kriyā - action.**

These terms cannot simply be assumed to form an identical ordered series in every Śaiva context, but they belong to the explanatory structure of this passage.

The broader point is that the fivefold body is not merely a collection of anatomical pieces.

It is interpreted as an articulation of divine powers.

The head, face, heart, secret region, and embodiment are differentiations.

Their ultimate ground is not differentiated into five independent realities.

**Nyāsa becomes the recognition of divine powers in the body rather than the transportation of divinity into a previously godless object.**

That is a substantial philosophical transformation.

And we haven't even reached the number twenty-seven.


## IV. Twenty-Seven: Abhinavagupta Has Somehow Turned Mantra Repetition into Divine Arithmetic

### KSTS p. 263 - Gnoli p. 277

Now the explanation becomes deliciously mathematical.

Abhinavagupta begins with the fivefold structure.

He says that the beginning and conclusion express an undivided form, while the intervening differentiation presents each of the five as itself fivefold.

This gives a structure of two encompassing aspects and twenty-five differentiated aspects.

But then he presents a second, explicitly triadic explanation of **twenty-seven**.

This is the one we must understand carefully.

His prose says:

**तिस्रश्च देव्यः प्रत्येकमिच्छादित्रययोगान्नवात्मतां प्राप्ताः**

*tisraś ca devyaḥ pratyekam icchāditrayayogān navātmatāṃ prāptāḥ.*

“And the three goddesses, each through conjunction with the triad beginning with Will, attain ninefoldness.”

Then:

**पुनरपि सृष्टिस्थितिसंहृतिवशात्त्रैधमापन्ना**

*punar api sṛṣṭisthitisaṃhṛtivaśāt traidham āpannāḥ.*

“And again, through creation, maintenance, and withdrawal, they acquire threefold differentiation.”

Now observe the mathematical structure.

The twenty-sevenfold Śakti structure

# 3

Goddesses

Parā, Parāparā, Aparā

# 3

Śaktis

Will, knowledge, action

# 3

Cosmic functions

Creation, maintenance, withdrawal

# 3 × 3 × 3 = 27

Twenty-seven differentiated aspects of the divine power

This is directly grounded in the commentary's successive expressions *navātmatāṃ prāptāḥ* and *traidham āpannāḥ*.

Now the philosophical implications.

The three goddesses are not simply three female characters standing beside one another.

They represent differentiated modes of the supreme Śakti.

Parā, Parāparā, and Aparā express the graded manifestation of consciousness, from the supreme through intermediate unity-in-difference to more differentiated experience.

Each can be considered through the three powers of will, knowledge, and action.

And these can further be considered through creation, maintenance, and withdrawal.

Thus the same divine power is manifested in twenty-seven differentiated modes.

But the multiplication does not produce twenty-seven independent ultimate substances.

It expresses the permutations of a single conscious reality.

### Why the arithmetic matters

Imagine the following.

You perceive an object.

There is the will or orientation toward perceiving.

There is the cognition of what appears.

There is the action or manifestation associated with that process.

The experience arises.

It persists for some period.

It disappears or is withdrawn.

These descriptions are not all separate entities.

They are distinguishable aspects of a dynamic event.

Abhinavagupta uses the triplication of powers and functions to express the richness of conscious manifestation.

The twenty-sevenfoldness is a structured differentiation of unity.

Not the destruction of unity.

This is why he can interpret twenty-seven mantra repetitions as corresponding to a cosmic structure rather than merely to a numerical requirement.

The number becomes meaningful within the theology.

### Why twenty-seven recitations do not manufacture twenty-seven goddesses

This distinction is crucial.

The ritual requires repetition.

The commentary supplies an ontological interpretation of the number.

But the argument is not that every correctly pronounced mantra produces a new goddess in the universe.

The divine powers are already the differentiated manifestations of consciousness.

The recitation represents or engages that structure within the ritual.

Its deepest meaning lies in recognition.

And this is where the *śikhā* becomes extraordinary.

### Binding the topknot

Abhinavagupta describes the *śikhā* as associated with the freedom through which manifestation is projected down to earth and with the highest centres of cognitive, vital, and bodily expression.

He mentions the power of supreme consciousness, the intellect, touch, life-breath, and the **brahmarandhra**.

The latter is the “opening of Brahman,” traditionally associated with the crown region in yogic subtle-body discourse.

The important phrase is:

**बन्धनं सर्वाविभागसारं तादात्म्यम्**

*bandhanaṃ sarvāvibhāgasāraṃ tādātmyam.*

“Binding is identity whose essence is the nondivision of everything.”

Fuck.

We have gone from tying a tuft of hair to the nondivision of all reality.

And the logic is not arbitrary.

Ordinarily, binding means securing one thing to another.

Two distinct items.

A knot connecting them.

But Abhinavagupta interprets the highest *bandhana* as **tādātmya**, identity.

Not a rope joining two independent substances.

The recognition that the manifest powers are not ultimately separate.

So the ritualist ties the *śikhā*.

The philosopher recognises the entire structure of manifestation as belonging to the supreme consciousness.

The external action becomes an expression of the higher meaning.

### The Sanskrit of identity

**tad-ātman** - having that as one's self or nature.

**tādātmya** - identity, having the same essential nature.

The form involves the abstract condition of being of one self or nature.

It does not mean mere similarity.

Two things might look alike without being identical.

The theological claim here concerns non-separation at the fundamental level.

Likewise:

**avibhāga** - nondivision.

From privative *a-* \+ *vibhāga*, division or differentiation.

**sāra** - essence.

Thus:

*sarvāvibhāgasāra* -

Having the nondivision of everything as its essence.

The *śikhā* is tied.

But the higher meaning is the recognition that there was never an ultimately separate collection of powers requiring an external rope.

The symbolism is magnificent.

And Abhinavagupta isn't finished.

The same principle applies not merely to the topknot, but to the five locations of the body.

Each location can be considered as containing the others, because the whole differentiated system is a manifestation of the same conscious reality.

This is an application of **sarvasarvātmakatva**, the principle that each manifestation participates in the universal nature.

Not because the physical face is numerically identical with the physical foot.

But because their ultimate manifestness is grounded in one consciousness.

The ritual locations remain different.

Their ground is one.

And now we're ready for verse 27.


## V. Verse 27: Seal Ten Directions and Clap Three Times to Inform the Universe That It Is Not Actually Divided

### KSTS p. 261 - root tantra

**एकैकं तु दिशां बन्धं दशानामपि योजयेत् ।**

*ekaikaṃ tu diśāṃ bandhaṃ daśānām api yojayet.*

**तालत्रयं पुरा दत्त्वा सशब्दं विघ्नशान्तये ॥२७॥**

*tālatrayaṃ purā dattvā saśabdaṃ vighnaśāntaye.*

My translation:

“One should apply the binding individually to the ten directions, first making three audible claps for the pacification of obstacles.”

The independent root witness gives *ekaikena* and *kārayet* in the first half, which changes the instrumental and verbal construction but preserves the broad ritual sense. The KSTS form is given above. ; \]

Now consider the scene.

The ritualist has installed the mantras.

Bound the topknot.

Repeated the relevant formula.

And now must protect or bind the directions.

Ten of them.

The universe requires perimeter security.

Apparently enlightenment has acquired a spatial access-control system.

### Why ten directions?

The ordinary eight horizontal directions are supplemented by up and down.

The result is the conventional tenfold spatial orientation.

But Abhinavagupta refuses to treat directions as absolutely independent containers.

He says:

**दिश्यमाना घटाद्या एव दिशः**

*diśyamānā ghaṭādyā eva diśaḥ.*

“The directions are simply things being indicated, such as pots and the like.”

This is a striking epistemological observation.

A direction is not necessarily an independently existing object in addition to the relations through which things are located.

A pot is over there.

A tree is behind you.

The sky is above.

The ground is below.

Spatial orientation depends on a standpoint and relational determination.

Abhinavagupta says the ten directions arise **svāpekṣayā**, relative to oneself.

That is not a declaration that physical space has no empirical structure.

It is an analysis of the way directionality is constituted relative to the subject's orientation.

### The grammar

**दिशाम् - diśām**

Genitive plural of *diś*, direction.

Notice the declension of the feminine consonant stem.

Not *diśānām*.

The word is simply *diśām*.

**दशानाम् - daśānām**

Genitive plural of *daśan* or the numeral construction referring to ten.

**बन्धम् - bandham**

Binding, fastening, securing, from √*bandh*.

**योजयेत् - yojayet**

Third-person singular optative causative of √*yuj*, to join, yoke, connect.

“One should apply or connect.”

The optative has prescriptive force here.

This is an instruction within a ritual text.

### The metaphysics of sealing space

The ritualist marks or binds the directions.

But Abhinavagupta says that the highest binding has the nature of:

**आत्मसाक्षात्कार - ātmasākṣātkāra**

Direct realisation of the Self.

The direction is not protected because someone has built a magical wall around an independently existing slice of space.

The higher interpretation concerns recognising the entire directional field as non-separate from consciousness.

The ten directions represent the spatially differentiated world.

Their binding expresses the recognition of their unity.

The apparent plurality is not denied.

The claim of ultimate separation is denied.

And that is precisely the same movement we saw with the five bodily locations.

### The three claps

**तालत्रयम् - tālatrayam**

A group of three claps or percussive strikes.

*tāla* can mean a clap, beat, or rhythmic measure, among other senses.

*traya* means a triad or set of three.

**पुरा - purā**

Before, previously, first.

**दत्त्वा - dattvā**

Having given or performed, the absolutive of √*dā*, to give.

**सशब्दम् - saśabdam**

With sound, audibly.

From *sa-* plus *śabda*, sound.

**विघ्नशान्तये - vighnaśāntaye**

Dative singular, “for the pacification of obstacles.”

**vighna** - obstacle, impediment, disruption.

**śānti** - quieting, pacification, peace.

The dative marks the purpose.

Now Abhinavagupta does something brilliant.

He doesn't merely say that the claps frighten off supernatural troublemakers.

He interprets the obstacles themselves.

They are the waves of difference and contraction through which the undivided nature of the Self becomes obscured.

His description includes:

**भेदकल्लोल - bhedakallola**

Waves of differentiation.

**saṃkoca** - contraction, restriction.

And the peace of the obstacles is:

**अभेदभैरवार्णवतादात्म्यम्**

*abhedabhairavārṇavatādātmyam.*

“Identity with the ocean of undivided Bhairava.”

That's the actual metaphysical meaning he draws from the ritual.

The claps are audible.

The sound is real as an event.

But the higher *śānti* is not simply the absence of noise.

It is the recognition that the divisions through which the universe appears do not destroy its supreme unity.

### Why sound matters again

Abhinavagupta briefly returns to the levels of speech.

He associates the meaningful sound here with **madhyamā**, the internal form of speech, while *vaikharī*, externally articulated speech, is a further differentiation.

That is important because the root verse explicitly says *saśabdam*, with sound.

The physical clap is an audible event.

But the commentator interprets the significance through the deeper expressive power of consciousness.

Sound is not exhausted by the vibration of air.

In his framework, speech is one articulation of divine self-awareness.

The outward sound can therefore function as a ritual expression of a deeper recognition.

Not because every clap has intrinsic supernatural efficacy.

But because, within the ritual system, it can represent and participate in the conscious power being contemplated.

And now Abhinavagupta calls one of his intellectual ancestors as a witness.

Somānanda.

The founder of the philosophical Pratyabhijñā line from which Utpaladeva and Abhinavagupta inherit so much.

And the quoted verse is the magnificent opening of the *Śivadṛṣṭi*.


## VI. Somānanda: Śiva Bows to Himself Through His Own Power. The Universe Has Become an Incredibly Complicated Mirror.

### Authentic citation - Śivadṛṣṭi 1.1

First, a genuine textual problem.

The KSTS and several reproductions of its *Parātriṃśikāvivaraṇa* passage read the second pāda of the second half with **parayā**:

**शिवः करोतु परया नमः शक्त्या ततात्मने ।**

*śivaḥ karotu parayā namaḥ śaktyā tatātmane.*

However, independently transmitted editions of Somānanda's *Śivadṛṣṭi* read:

**शिवः करोतु निजया नमः शक्त्या ततात्मने ।**

*śivaḥ karotu nijayā namaḥ śaktyā tatātmane.*

This is supported by both the Mahānaya text and the independently digitised Śaiva tradition with Utpaladeva's commentary. Therefore the difference is real at the level of the consulted textual witnesses; it should not be erased. ; ; \]

The independent *Śivadṛṣṭi* verse, in full, reads:

**अस्मद्रूपसमाविष्टः स्वात्मनात्मनिवारणे ।**

*asmadrūpasamāviṣṭaḥ svātmanātmanivāraṇe.*

**शिवः करोतु निजया नमः शक्त्या ततात्मने ॥१॥**

*śivaḥ karotu nijayā namaḥ śaktyā tatātmane.*

A cautious translation:

“May Śiva, entered into our very form, make obeisance through his own Śakti to his expanded Self, in the self-concealment of himself.”

The precise segmentation and philosophical explanation of *svātmanātmanivāraṇe* require attention to Somānanda's commentators, but the principal nondual movement is clear.

Śiva has entered the form of the individual subject.

And the act of homage is itself performed through Śiva's power.

This is not simply a finite human being bowing to an independently existing god located somewhere else.

It is Śiva's own power operating through a differentiated subject.

### Asmadrūpasamāviṣṭaḥ

**asmad** - the first-person pronoun base, “we” or “I” in the appropriate construction.

**rūpa** - form or nature.

**samāviṣṭa** - fully entered, pervaded, possessed, from *sam-* \+ *ā-* \+ √*viś*, to enter.

Thus:

“Entered into our form.”

The striking point is that the divine subject can manifest as the finite subject.

The ordinary person experiences themselves as “I.”

But Somānanda's nondual interpretation sees the ultimate consciousness as the ground of that I-experience.

The finite ego is not the supreme subject considered in its full unrestricted nature.

But its conscious reality is not independent of Śiva.

### Svātmanā

By his own Self.

Instrumental singular of *svātman*.

The instrumental is crucial.

The act is performed **through the Self**.

The agency does not originate in an absolutely independent second substance outside consciousness.

### Namah karotu

**namaḥ** - homage, salutation, bowing.

**karotu** - may he make, third-person singular imperative of √*kṛ*, to do.

The grammar expresses a benedictive or prayerful wish.

May Śiva make obeisance.

To whom?

To himself in the relevant expanded or supreme sense.

And through what?

Through his own Śakti.

The entire act of worship becomes self-referential.

### The difference between nija and para

**nija** means one's own, innate, personal, intrinsic.

**parā** means supreme, highest.

Thus the two readings emphasise subtly different aspects.

*nijayā śaktyā* - through his own Śakti.

*parayā śaktyā* - through the supreme Śakti.

Both are intelligible within Somānanda's nondual metaphysics.

But the independently transmitted *Śivadṛṣṭi* supports *nijayā*, and we should preserve that fact.

The philosophical closeness of the two readings is not permission to silently combine them.

### Why the quotation belongs here

The root ritual prescribes three claps to quiet obstacles.

Abhinavagupta interprets their deeper significance as the pacification of divisive misrecognition.

Somānanda's verse describes Śiva as the reality of the apparently individual subject and of the act directed toward the supreme Self.

Thus the ultimate obstacle is not simply something external that must be frightened away.

It is the misrecognition of differentiated experience as ultimately separate from consciousness.

And the highest overcoming of that obstacle is not the production of a second reality.

It is recognition.

The claps express the settling of differentiation into the undivided Heart.

Somānanda provides the underlying philosophical logic.

The worshipper is Śiva in contracted manifestation.

The worship is Śiva's power.

The reality worshipped is Śiva.

And the recognition removes the apparent absolute distance between them.

**Śiva bows to Śiva, through Śakti, while pretending for a moment to be somebody else.**

The cosmic theatre is becoming increasingly elaborate.

And we haven't even sprinkled the water.


## VII. Verse 28: Water, Flowers, the Liṅga, and the Revelation That the Real Altar Is Not Made of Stone

### KSTS pp. 261-262 - root tantra

**शिखासंख्याभिजप्तेन तोयेनाभ्युक्षयेत्ततः ।**

*śikhāsaṃkhyābhijaptena toyenābhyukṣayet tataḥ.*

**पुष्पादिकं क्रमात्सर्वं लिङ्गे वा स्थण्डिलेऽथ वा ॥२८॥**

*puṣpādikaṃ kramāt sarvaṃ liṅge vā sthaṇḍile 'tha vā.*

My literal translation:

“Then one should sprinkle all the flowers and other materials, in sequence, with water over which the same number of repetitions as for the topknot has been performed, whether at the liṅga or at the prepared ritual ground.”

The locatives *liṅge* and *sthaṇḍile* in the commentary reading are significant; the independent root witness instead has accusative forms, producing a different syntactic construction. The broad procedure remains recognisable, but the case morphology differs. ; \]

Now we have ordinary ritual materials.

Water.

Flowers.

An altar or liṅga.

A repeated mantra.

The instructions are not mysterious.

Yet Abhinavagupta again refuses to stop at the external form.

### Abhijapta - अभिजप्त

Empowered, consecrated, or ritually recited over.

From *abhi-* \+ √*jap*, to recite quietly or repeat a mantra.

**abhijaptena** is instrumental singular.

It qualifies **toyena**, water.

The water is the means by which sprinkling is performed.

### Toya - तोय

Water.

**toyena** - instrumental singular.

In the ritual, the water is placed in or drawn from an offering vessel, typically the *arghapātra* in the relevant liturgical context.

Abhinavagupta explicitly associates this with the offering-vessel procedure.

But then he says that water is understood through the Heart in its fluid form.

This is the shift.

The ritual substance is not simply discarded.

Its fluidity becomes an occasion for recognising the unrestricted nature of conscious manifestation.

### The metaphysics of fluidity

Water flows.

It takes the shape of containers.

It changes position.

It does not retain one rigid geometric configuration like a stone.

Abhinavagupta uses this fluidity as a meaningful analogy for the Heart's freedom from contraction.

But we must not pretend he is making a modern physical claim that consciousness is literally a liquid.

The analogy concerns the contrast between rigidity and unrestricted manifestation.

A contracted subject identifies with one limited form.

The supreme consciousness is not exhausted by that form.

Water's fluidity becomes an expressive symbol of this freedom.

### Puṣpa - पुष्प

Flower.

The term is the ordinary noun for a blossom.

**puṣpādikam** means flowers and the other associated ritual materials.

**ādi** indicates continuation of a class beginning with the stated item.

**kramāt** means in sequence, by order, through a prescribed progression.

And the commentary gives the flower's philosophical function.

It is associated with unrestricted expansion and the giving up of contraction.

A flower opens.

A bud unfolds.

The form becomes manifest.

The symbolism is readily intelligible, but its precise tantric interpretation belongs to the commentary rather than to the biological structure of flowers.

### Liṅga - लिङ्ग

Sign, mark, characteristic, indication, and in religious contexts a sacred emblem of Śiva.

Abhinavagupta cites a fragment:

**यत्र लीनं चराचरम्**

*yatra līnaṃ carācaram.*

“Where the moving and unmoving world is absorbed.”

This fragment is independently verified as part of **Mālinīvijayottaratantra 18.3**.

The full verse in the independent tantra reads:

**यजेदाध्यात्मिकं लिङ्गं यत्र लीनं चराचरम् ।**

*yajed ādhyātmikaṃ liṅgaṃ yatra līnaṃ carācaram.*

**बहिर्लिङ्गस्य लिङ्गत्वमनेनाधिष्ठितं यतः ॥**

*bahirliṅgasya liṅgatvam anenādhiṣṭhitaṃ yataḥ.*

My translation:

“One should worship the inner or spiritual liṅga in which the moving and unmoving world is dissolved, because the status of the external liṅga as a liṅga is grounded in this.”

This wording is attested independently, including in the Mālinīvijayottara transcription and in scholarly discussion of the tantra's internalisation of liṅga worship. ; ; \]

Now that is a spectacularly relevant source.

Because it establishes a hierarchy between the external emblem and the inner reality.

The external liṅga can function as a sign.

But its sacred significance depends on the deeper principle it expresses.

The internal or spiritual liṅga is not merely another external statue located somewhere under the ribs.

It belongs to a contemplative account of the divine reality.

### Līna - लीन

Absorbed, dissolved, merged.

Past passive participle associated with √*lī*, to dissolve or become absorbed.

The verse says that the whole moving and unmoving world is absorbed within the spiritual liṅga.

This is precisely the same underlying structure as the banyan-seed analogy.

The differentiated world is not absolutely independent.

Its manifestation and reabsorption belong to a deeper ground.

And that ground is the reality of consciousness.

### A historical qualification

The *Mālinīvijayottaratantra* does not automatically carry every later metaphysical interpretation that Abhinavagupta develops.

Scholarly work on the tantra has noted that its insistence on internalising worship need not, by itself, amount to a fully developed doctrine of ontological nonduality.

Abhinavagupta reads the passage through his own systematic Trika metaphysics.

That is a historically significant interpretive act.

We should not simply collapse the source tantra and its later philosophical commentator into one undifferentiated authorial voice.

The source teaches the supremacy of internal worship in this context.

Abhinavagupta interprets that teaching through the supreme self-manifesting consciousness of Bhairava.

Both deserve to be understood on their own terms.

### Sthaṇḍila - स्थण्डिल

A prepared ritual ground or earthen altar.

The locative singular is **sthaṇḍile**.

Notice the retroflex conjunct **ण्ड - ṇḍ**.

The nasal and stop are both retroflex.

The form is not merely a generic modern platform.

It belongs to ritual practice, where the site is prepared for offerings or worship.

And Abhinavagupta treats the question of the worship location through the nature of the supreme Heart.

If the divine reality pervades everything, why should its ultimate presence be confined to a particular sacred object?

It need not be.

But a particular object or site can still be used as a ritual locus.

The locus participates in the manifestation of consciousness.

It does not contain the supreme consciousness as though Śiva were a little object trapped inside a stone.

That's the philosophical reversal.

**The external emblem becomes meaningful because it can express the inner divine reality. The inner divine reality does not become real merely because the emblem has been consecrated.**

And now we reach the fourteenth vowel.

The letter **औ - au**.

The same vowel we encountered in **सौः**.

Apparently it has returned to explain the arrangement of the Goddess's seat.

Naturally.


## VIII. Verse 29: Fourteen Repetitions, One Flower, and the Vowel That Holds a Trident

### KSTS p. 262 - root tantra

**चतुर्दशाभिजप्तेन पुष्पेणासनकल्पना ।**

*caturdaśābhijaptena puṣpeṇāsanakalpanā.*

**तत्र सृष्टिं यजेद्वीरः पुनरेवासनं ततः ॥२९॥**

*tatra sṛṣṭiṃ yajed vīraḥ punar evāsanaṃ tataḥ.*

My literal translation:

“The arrangement of the seat is made with a flower empowered by fourteen repetitions. There the heroic practitioner should worship creation, and thereafter the seat once again.”

Notice the number.

Fourteen.

Not twenty-seven.

The text has changed the count.

And Abhinavagupta explains why.

**चतुर्दशेन औकारेण**

*caturdaśena aukāreṇa.*

“By the fourteenth, the phoneme *au*.”

Then:

**तस्यैव त्रिशूलरूपत्वात्**

*tasyaiva triśūlarūpatvāt.*

“Because that very phoneme has the form of the trident.”

There.

The vowel **औ** is back.

It is the same vowel that forms the centre of the supreme Heart-mantra **सौः**.

And its fourteenfold significance is not an arbitrary numerical superstition.

Within the relevant traditional ordering of Sanskrit vowels, *au* occupies the fourteenth position. ; \]

### Count the vowels

The classical sequence used for this reckoning is:

| Position | Devanāgarī | IAST |
|--------|--------|--------|
| 1 | अ | a |
| 2 | आ | ā |
| 3 | इ | i |
| 4 | ई | ī |
| 5 | उ | u |
| 6 | ऊ | ū |
| 7 | ऋ | ṛ |
| 8 | ॠ | ṝ |
| 9 | ऌ | ḷ |
| 10 | ॡ | ḹ |
| 11 | ए | e |
| 12 | ऐ | ai |
| 13 | ओ | o |
| 14 | औ | au |

The final two positions of the sixteenfold traditional Mātṛkā vowel series are **अं - aṃ** and **अः - aḥ**.

This is a particular tantric phonemic enumeration; one should not assume that all historical descriptions of Sanskrit vowels use precisely the same counting conventions.

But it gives the correct fourteenfold position of *au* in the present explanation.

Now remember the structure of **सौः**.

*Sa* pervades the lower triad of cosmic spheres.

*Au* is linked with the higher Śākta sphere and its trident-like powers.

*Visarga* concerns supreme emission beyond the differentiated spheres.

Thus the flower consecrated through fourteen repetitions is connected with the central vowel of the mantra.

The ritual number encodes the phonemic structure.

And the phonemic structure encodes a cosmological interpretation.

Abhinavagupta has managed to make a flower offering depend on a theory of Sanskrit vowels.

An exceptional achievement in administrative complexity.

### What does *aukāra* mean?

The phoneme *au*, named through the formation **aukāra**.

The suffix-like **-kāra** in such names designates the relevant sound-unit.

Compare:

*akāra* - the phoneme a.

*sakāra* - the phoneme sa or consonantal s in the relevant naming convention.

*aukāra* - the vowel au.

And importantly, **औ** is one Sanskrit vowel.

It is not simply the separate utterance *a-u* in ordinary pronunciation, even though its phonological and grammatical interpretation includes relationships with the simpler vowels.

Its written dependent sign is **ौ**.

Thus:

स + ौ = सौ.

Adding visarga gives:

सौः.

### The trident

**त्रिशूल - triśūla**

*tri* - three.

*śūla* - spike, spear, trident.

The theological trident expresses threefold divine power.

But Abhinavagupta's use is not merely visual.

He has repeatedly connected the *aukāra* with the higher Śākta power.

The trident is a manifestation of triadic differentiation within one principle.

One shaft.

Three points.

A useful image for the relation between unity and differentiated powers.

But it is not proof that the physical shape of the Devanāgarī letter औ itself is a trident.

That would be confusing a historically developed graphic form with a metaphysical phonemic structure.

The symbol is doctrinal.

The written glyph is a representation.

They can be associated in meditation, but they should not be treated as historically or ontologically identical.

### Āsana - आसन

Now the seat.

**āsana** means seat, sitting-place, posture, or foundation.

Connected with √*ās*, to sit.

The root verse says **āsanakalpanā**.

The arranging, imagining, or constructing of the seat.

**kalpanā** from √*kḷp*, to arrange, prepare, or make suitable.

The relevant ritualist constructs the goddess's seat.

But Abhinavagupta asks what constitutes the ultimate seat.

### The subject and support of sitting

His explanation invokes the relation between:

**adhikaraṇa** - locus, substratum, support.

**kartṛ** - agent, doer.

**āsana** - seat.

The ordinary distinction is straightforward.

The practitioner sits.

The seat supports the practitioner.

The practitioner performs the ritual.

But in the supreme nondual interpretation, the locus and agent are manifestations of the same conscious reality.

The seat is not an ultimate substance separate from the one who sits.

The agent is not independent of the field in which action occurs.

Both are expressed through Śiva's freedom.

And this is why the ritual location becomes another occasion for recognising **svātantrya**, the freedom of consciousness.

The ultimate support of all manifested objects is not a physical chair.

It is the conscious ground in which their appearance is possible.

### Sṛṣṭi - सृष्टि

Now comes the next keyword.

**सृष्टि - sṛṣṭi**

Creation, emission, manifestation.

From √*sṛj*, to emit, release, or send forth.

The root tantra says:

**तत्र सृष्टिं यजेत्**

*tatra sṛṣṭiṃ yajet.*

“There one should worship creation.”

The verb **yajet** is an optative of √*yaj*, to worship or sacrifice.

Abhinavagupta interprets *sṛṣṭi* as the entire phonemic unfolding, from **a** through **kṣa**.

**आदिक्षान्त - ādikṣānta**

From the beginning with *a* to the end with *kṣa*.

Here the alphabet again represents the totality of differentiated manifestation.

The ritualist worships *sṛṣṭi*.

The commentator interprets that as worship of manifestation within the supreme Heart.

And the root verse then returns to the seat.

Why?

Because support and supported, agent and action, are not ultimately independent.

The same conscious reality manifests all of them.

The verse's repetitions are therefore not merely redundant.

They give Abhinavagupta a way to articulate the relation between manifestation and its ground.

And now the entire creation is about to be enclosed in the Heart.


## IX. Verse 30: Put the Universe Between Two Mantras and Somehow Recover the Nondual Absolute

### KSTS p. 262 - root tantra

**सृष्टिं तु सम्पुटीकृत्य पश्चाद्यजनमारभेत् ।**

*sṛṣṭiṃ tu saṃpuṭīkṛtya paścād yajanam ārabhet.*

**सर्वतत्त्वसुसम्पूर्णां सर्वाभरणभूषिताम् ॥३०॥**

*sarvatattvasusampūrṇāṃ sarvābharaṇabhūṣitām.*

My literal translation:

“Having enclosed creation, one should afterwards begin worship of her who is completely filled with all the tattvas and adorned with every ornament.”

The final feminine accusatives anticipate **devīm**, the Goddess, in the following verse.

That syntax matters.

The verse is not describing a free-floating abstract universe without a referent.

It is preparing the description of the Goddess who is to be worshipped.

### Saṃpuṭīkaraṇa - सम्पुटीकरण

This is the key technical term.

**sam-** - together, fully.

**puṭa** - fold, container, covering, enclosure.

**-karaṇa** - making, doing, bringing into a condition.

**saṃpuṭīkaraṇa** means enclosing or encapsulating.

In a mantric context, a sound or sequence may be enclosed by another sound or by repetitions of the same seed.

The commentary explains that creation, from a to kṣa, is enclosed by the Heart-seed.

But here the philosophical significance becomes much more profound.

### The alphabet enclosed within the Heart

We previously established that the Sanskrit phonemes can represent the differentiated powers of manifestation.

The entire phonemic series from a to kṣa is associated with the emergence of the cosmos.

Now the Heart-mantra encloses that series.

The result expresses:

The entire differentiated universe is present within supreme consciousness.

The process begins from the Heart.

The powers unfold.

They return to the Heart.

And the Heart has never ceased to be their ground.

This is the relationship between:

**unmeṣa** - opening, manifestation.

**nimeṣa** - closing, withdrawal.

The manifestation does not require a second independent substance outside consciousness.

Nor does withdrawal imply that consciousness itself is destroyed.

The differentiated forms appear and are reabsorbed.

Their ultimate ground remains.

### The clash of the two powers

Abhinavagupta gives a striking explanation:

**सृष्टेश्च संपूटीकरणमुभयसंघट्टक्षोभानन्दरूपम्**

*sṛṣṭeś ca saṃpuṭīkaraṇam ubhayasaṃghaṭṭakṣobhānandarūpam.*

“And the enclosure of creation has the nature of the bliss arising through the encounter and stirring of the two.”

This is one of the extraordinary Kaula expressions in the passage.

**ubhaya** - both, the two.

**saṃghaṭṭa** - meeting, contact, collision, conjunction.

**kṣobha** - stirring, agitation, disturbance, excitation.

**ānanda** - bliss.

**rūpa** - form or nature.

The image belongs to the dynamic union of Śiva and Śakti and the corresponding process of manifestation and reabsorption.

The two are not ultimate independent substances.

Their relation expresses the self-differentiating freedom of the one consciousness.

And the bliss associated with their *saṃghaṭṭa* is not merely an ordinary pleasant sensation.

It concerns the self-relish of conscious manifestation.

This is the same philosophical territory we encountered in the earlier discussion of *visarga*, bliss, and spontaneous bodily expressions.

The Kaula vocabulary is deliberately dynamic.

It does not depict the absolute as a passive blank.

It expresses the absolute as intrinsically self-aware and capable of manifestation.

### Is the union sexual?

The terms *saṃghaṭṭa*, *kṣobha*, and *ānanda* can carry erotic resonances within Kaula religious discourse.

We should not erase that context.

But neither should we conclude that this exact sentence is a detailed instruction for sexual ritual.

Its immediate argument concerns the enclosing of *sṛṣṭi* within the Heart-seed.

Abhinavagupta interprets the process through the dynamic encounter of powers.

The literal ritual procedure and its metaphysical explanation are distinguishable.

This is one of the central requirements of a rigorous reading.

### Sarvatattvasusampūrṇām

Now the next compound.

**सर्वतत्त्वसुसम्पूर्णाम्**

*sarva-tattva-su-sampūrṇām.*

**sarva** - all.

**tattva** - principle, fundamental category, reality.

**su-** - well, thoroughly, completely.

**sampūrṇa** - fully complete, filled, perfected.

The final **-ām** is feminine accusative singular, agreeing with *devīm* in verse 31.

The Goddess is thoroughly complete with all the tattvas.

That is the theological claim.

She is not a being who possesses a few powers within one limited part of the universe.

She is the supreme Śakti through which all the categories of manifestation are expressed.

### Sarvābharaṇabhūṣitām

This is the KSTS commentary reading.

**सर्वाभरणभूषिताम्**

*sarvābharaṇabhūṣitām.*

**ābharaṇa** - ornament, decoration, adornment.

**bhūṣita** - adorned, decorated, past passive participle of √*bhūṣ*, to adorn.

Thus:

“Adorned with all ornaments.”

But the independent root text has:

**सर्वावयवशोभिताम्**

*sarvāvayavaśobhitām.*

“Resplendent with every constituent or limb.”

Both scan as anuṣṭubh and both are conceptually appropriate, but they do not mean the same thing.

The KSTS version emphasises ornaments.

The independent reading emphasises constituent parts.

And Abhinavagupta's following prose concentrates especially on the differentiated constituents of the Goddess as the forms of all manifest things.

This makes the *avayava* reading philosophically interesting, though it does not by itself establish priority over the transmitted KSTS reading. ; \]

### What is an avayava?

**अवयव - avayava**

Part, member, constituent, limb.

In Indian philosophical discourse it can refer to the components of a composite whole or the members of an argument.

Here the theological interpretation concerns the universe as the differentiated manifestation of the Goddess.

Abhinavagupta speaks about the forms of subjects ranging from ordinary objects and animals through human beings and higher divine or mantric levels.

The apparently separate components become the limbs of one all-encompassing conscious reality.

But we must be precise.

This is not simply the claim that the universe is a physically gigantic woman whose limbs are made of planets and living organisms.

It is a metaphysical interpretation of the Goddess as the totality of manifesting Śakti.

### The difference between a whole and an aggregate

An ordinary aggregate is assembled from parts.

You can build a wall out of bricks.

Before the bricks are assembled, the wall does not exist in its completed arrangement.

But Abhinavagupta's supreme consciousness is not an aggregate built out of previously independent components.

The differentiated forms are manifestations within the one conscious reality.

The whole does not depend on their external assembly to become conscious.

This is crucial.

Otherwise we would be constructing consciousness by adding together unconscious objects.

That is not the Pratyabhijñā claim.

The supreme subject is fundamental.

Its manifested powers are differentiated.

Their unity does not result from externally joining things that were originally independent.

The unity belongs to the ground itself.

And this is why the Goddess can be complete with all tattvas without being merely a sum of separate parts.

The ornamented body is the image.

The undivided conscious power is the metaphysical reality.

Now we reach the direct worship of the Goddess.


## X. Verse 31: Worship the Goddess Twenty-Seven Times. Except the Goddess Is Already Every Power by Which You Could Worship Her.

### KSTS p. 262 - root tantra

**यजेद्देवीं महेशानीं सप्तविंशतिमन्त्रिताम् ।**

*yajed devīṃ maheśānīṃ saptaviṃśatimantritām.*

**ततः सुगन्धिपुष्पैस्तु यथाशक्त्या समर्चयेत् ॥३१॥**

*tataḥ sugandhipuṣpais tu yathāśaktyā samarcayet.*

My translation:

“One should worship the Goddess, the great sovereign Lady, empowered through twenty-seven recitations; then one should honour her with fragrant flowers according to one's capacity.”

Again, this is the KSTS reading. The independent root text has *mahābhāgā* in place of *maheśānīm*, and *ca* in place of *tu* after *sugandhipuṣpaiḥ*. The *mahābhāgā* reading is not an ordinary accusative feminine singular agreeing with *devīm*; it creates a syntactic difficulty that needs independent textual evaluation rather than automatic normalisation. ; \]

### Maheśānī - महेशानी

The great sovereign Lady.

Related to *Maheśa*, the great Lord.

The feminine **Maheśānī** is used for the Goddess.

The accusative singular is **maheśānīm**, with a long ī and final nasalised consonant representation in the written form.

This agrees with **devīm**, also accusative singular.

The syntax is straightforward.

The Goddess is the object of the prescribed worship.

### The twenty-seven repetitions return

**saptaviṃśatimantritām**

The same feminine accusative singular formation as in verse 26.

Once again, twenty-seven.

But this is not a new independent cosmic calculation.

We have already seen Abhinavagupta's explanation:

Three goddesses.

Three powers for each.

Three cosmic functions.

The result is twenty-seven.

The repetitions express the differentiated structure of the divine powers.

And that structure ultimately belongs to one Śakti.

This is why repetition becomes more than counting.

Within the theological framework, the number is a way of contemplating the differentiated manifestations of the Goddess without losing the unity of their ground.

### Sugandhipuṣpaiḥ

**सुगन्धिपुष्पैः - sugandhipuṣpaiḥ**

Fragrant flowers.

**su-** - good, pleasant, favourable.

**gandha** - smell, fragrance.

**sugandhin** - fragrant.

**puṣpa** - flower.

The compound appears in instrumental plural.

The flowers are the means or offering-material.

**yathāśaktyā** - according to one's capacity.

This phrase is worth noticing.

The ritual does not specify an unlimited quantity of expensive offerings.

It permits worship according to capacity.

Historically, the expression can concern available resources or ability.

Its metaphysical coincidence with the technical term *śakti* should not obscure its ordinary grammatical sense here.

### Samarcayet

**समर्चयेत् - samarcayet**

One should honour or worship thoroughly.

From *sam-* \+ the causative or denominative verbal formation associated with √*arc*, to worship, honour, or praise.

The optative **-yet** has prescriptive force.

Now the fascinating difference between external and internal worship emerges.

The root tantra describes offering fragrant flowers.

Abhinavagupta has already interpreted the flower through the opening or unrestricted manifestation of consciousness.

Thus the offering can be understood at two levels.

The external worshipper presents a material flower.

The practitioner established in recognition apprehends the act of offering as a manifestation of divine Śakti.

These are not mutually exclusive.

The physical offering can occur.

But its ultimate reality is understood differently.

### The Goddess is not merely an externally existing recipient

Suppose the worshipper imagines:

“I am here.”

“The Goddess is over there.”

“I must give her something.”

“She might accept it and give me a result.”

This is a coherent structure of ordinary devotional practice.

But Abhinavagupta's highest interpretation challenges the ultimate independence of those distinctions.

The worshipper's awareness is a manifestation of Śiva.

The intention to worship belongs to Śakti.

The offering is manifested within consciousness.

The Goddess represents the supreme power of that same consciousness.

Thus the act is fundamentally a movement within one reality.

The dualistic form is not necessarily abolished as a ritual appearance.

But its ultimate meaning is reinterpreted.

And this prepares the final verse.

Because now the practitioner is instructed to offer not merely flowers.

Not water.

Not incense.

Not the contents of a sacrificial bowl.

**Themselves.**

And Abhinavagupta is about to ask the question that makes the entire ritual system suddenly very uncomfortable.

If everything is Śiva, **what else is there to offer?**


## XI. Verse 32: Offer Yourself. There Is Literally Nothing Else Left.

### KSTS p. 262 - final verse of the ritual block

The KSTS digital text reads:

**पूजयेत्परया भक्त्या आत्मनां च निवेदयेत् ।**

*pūjayet parayā bhaktyā ātmanāṃ ca nivedayet.*

**एवं यजनमाख्यातमग्निकार्येऽप्ययं विधिः ॥३२॥**

*evaṃ yajanam ākhyātam agnikārye 'py ayaṃ vidhiḥ.*

The first line contains a textual problem. **आत्मनां - ātmanāṃ** is not the expected accusative singular “oneself.” The parallel transcription has *ātmanam*, and Abhinavagupta's own explanatory prose clearly says **आत्मानं निवेदयेत् - ātmānaṃ nivedayet**, “one should offer oneself.” The independent root text gives *svātmanaṃ*, another form requiring morphological care. The intended sense is strongly supported; the exact original wording of the root pāda is less secure. ; ; \]

With that qualification, my translation is:

“One should worship with supreme devotion and offer oneself. Thus the method of worship has been explained; the same procedure applies to the fire rite.”

Now this is the climax.

The practitioner has installed the mantras.

Bound the topknot.

Sealed the directions.

Clapped.

Sprinkled the water.

Prepared the flowers.

Established the seat.

Worshipped creation.

Enclosed creation in the Heart.

Invoked the Goddess.

And offered fragrant flowers.

Very good.

Now:

**Offer yourself.**

The whole ritual has been steadily moving toward this point.

And Abhinavagupta's commentary asks the question that changes everything:

**What else could you possibly offer to the supreme consciousness?**

### The morphology of self-offering

**पूजयेत् - pūjayet**

One should worship.

An optative, third-person singular, from the verbal formation associated with *pūj*, to honour or worship.

The ending indicates a prescribed or contemplated action.

**परया - parayā**

Instrumental singular feminine of *parā*, supreme.

**भक्त्या - bhaktyā**

Instrumental singular feminine of *bhakti*, devotion.

Related to √*bhaj*, to share, participate, honour, or devote oneself.

The two instrumentals combine to express worship through supreme devotion.

But Abhinavagupta sees an additional possibility.

**Parā** is not merely an adjective.

It is also the supreme Goddess.

The same word can therefore carry a technical theological meaning within the Trika system.

Now the commentary becomes considerably more interesting.

### Worship through the Goddess herself

Abhinavagupta says:

**परयैव हृदयरूपया पूजयेत्**

*parayaiva hṛdayarūpayā pūjayet.*

“One should worship by Parā herself, whose nature is the Heart.”

The grammatical movement is important.

The root verse says *parayā bhaktyā*, through supreme devotion.

Abhinavagupta interprets *parayā* as pointing to the supreme Goddess who is the Heart.

This is a theological interpretation made possible by the word's grammatical form and the context.

The instrumental singular feminine can refer to the Goddess as the means of worship.

Thus the act is not performed by an independent finite agent using a power external to Śiva.

It takes place through Parā Śakti.

And this creates the central paradox:

If the Goddess is the power through which worship occurs, then the worshipper is already acting through the deity.

The offering is not travelling from an independently existing subject to an independently existing supreme object.

The entire movement belongs to consciousness.

### But what is bhakti?

Abhinavagupta does not dismiss devotion as an inferior religious emotion.

He explains it through:

**तादात्म्यानुप्रवेशप्रह्वतात्मना**

*tādātmyānupraveśaprahvatātmanā.*

A condition of being humbled or bowed through entry into identity.

Disassemble it.

**tādātmya** - identity of nature.

**anupraveśa** - entering into, penetration.

**prahva** - bent forward, bowed, inclined, humble.

**ātman** - self or one's own condition.

The expression describes devotion whose inner significance is the entry into identity with the supreme reality.

That's extremely different from an ordinary transaction.

The finite ego doesn't simply approach a powerful divine individual and offer something in exchange for a favour.

The highest devotion involves the recognition that the very subject who worships is not ultimately separate from the divine consciousness.

And yet there is reverence.

There is humility.

There is the movement of worship.

**Nonduality does not require the disappearance of devotion. It transforms the understanding of devotion.**

The worshipper bows.

But the bowing itself is Śakti.

The deity receives the offering.

But the deity's reality is the same consciousness that makes the act possible.

Now we reach one of the most striking statements in the passage.


## XII. The God You Worship Is Created by Consciousness - and That Doesn't Make the God Unreal

### Gnoli p. 278 - Abhinavagupta's prose

Abhinavagupta writes:

**पूज्यो हि स्वयं सृज्यते**

*pūjyo hi svayaṃ sṛjyate.*

“For the object of worship is itself brought forth.”

Now imagine the average religious traditionalist hearing this.

“The deity is created?”

“What the fuck are you saying?”

“Are you claiming the Goddess is imaginary?”

No.

That would miss the entire metaphysical framework.

Abhinavagupta is investigating the way consciousness manifests the distinction between worshipper and worshipped.

The form of the deity is manifested within consciousness through its own power.

But the reality of the manifesting consciousness is not thereby reduced to a fiction.

He immediately insists that the process belongs to **svatantra-cinmayatā**, the condition of being freely conscious, rather than the inertness of an independently separate object.

### The distinction between imagination and manifestation

Suppose you imagine a deity.

The mental image is a genuine occurrence of experience.

But it does not follow that the particular object imagined exists independently exactly as represented.

That would be an unjustified leap.

Abhinavagupta's claim is more fundamental.

Every manifest form, whether a perceived object or a contemplative image, appears through consciousness.

The divine form is understood through the same ultimate expressive power.

And within the relevant tantric practice, the image becomes an occasion for recognising that power.

The crucial difference is between a form being **manifested through consciousness** and a form being **fabricated as an independently existing physical entity by a private ego**.

The first belongs to his metaphysics.

The second would be a much stronger and generally unsupported causal claim.

He is not asserting that simply visualising a deity produces an independently verifiable physical person in the room.

He is explaining how the relationship between deity, devotee, and worship is constituted through conscious freedom.

### Now Utpaladeva enters

Abhinavagupta quotes his teacher's teacher, Utpaladeva, from the *Īśvarapratyabhijñā*.

The verse is **1.5.16**.

**स्वातन्त्र्यामुक्तमात्मानं स्वातन्त्र्यादद्वयात्मनः ।**

*svātantryāmuktam ātmānaṃ svātantryād advayātmanaḥ.*

**प्रभुरीशादिसङ्कल्पैर्निर्माय व्यवहारयेत् ॥**

*prabhur īśādisaṃkalpair nirmāya vyavahārayet.*

The nondual Lord, through his freedom, manifests himself in differentiated forms such as Īśa and conducts the affairs of manifestation through them.

This verse is independently attested in the *Īśvarapratyabhijñā* tradition; Abhinavagupta quotes it here in the midst of explaining how the worshipped form is brought forth through divine freedom. ; ; \]

Notice the crucial compound:

**ईशादिसङ्कल्पैः - īśādisaṃkalpaiḥ**

Through determinations or conceptions beginning with Īśa.

**saṃkalpa** - determination, intention, conceptual formation.

**nirmāya** - having fashioned or manifested, absolutive of *nir-* \+ √*mā*, to measure, form, or construct.

**vyavahārayet** - would cause to engage in activity or transaction, a causative formation associated with *vyavahāra*, practical conduct or interaction.

The argument is not merely that the Lord stands outside creation and commands it.

The Lord manifests differentiated subjectivity through his freedom.

Thus the subject capable of worship is itself a manifestation of the divine.

And the worshipped deity is understood within that same expressive freedom.

### The philosophical consequence

Suppose we distinguish:

The person worshipping.

The deity worshipped.

The act of worship.

The offering.

The intention.

The recognition.

Ordinarily, these appear as different elements.

And they are different at the level of their manifested functions.

The offering is not the same empirical object as the worshipper's body.

The action is not the same event as the contemplated divine image.

But the ultimate reality of their manifestation is not divided into separate conscious substances.

All arise within Śiva's freedom.

That is why the worshipped form can be manifested through consciousness without being dismissed as merely illusory.

The ultimate reality is the self-manifesting conscious power.

The differentiated form is a mode of that manifestation.

This is the Pratyabhijñā answer.

And now the commentary returns to self-offering.

### Why offer yourself?

Abhinavagupta asks the question implicitly through a devastating sentence:

**अन्यस्य निवेद्यस्याभावात्**

*anyasya nivedyasyābhāvāt.*

“Because there is nothing else to be offered.”

This is the philosophical climax of the entire ritual block.

The ordinary ritualist offers a substance.

The higher practitioner recognises the supreme reality of the act.

But the ultimate offering cannot be a foreign object existing outside consciousness.

There is nothing outside the supreme Self that could be transported into it.

Thus the highest offering is **ātmanivedana**, the offering or surrender of oneself.

Yet even this must be understood correctly.

The supreme Self is not being handed over to another metaphysical substance.

The finite subject recognises that its apparent separateness is not ultimate.

The offering is the relinquishment of that mistaken independence.

### Nivedayet - निवेदयेत्

The verb is an optative causative formation of *ni-* \+ √*vid*, in the sense of making known, presenting, submitting, or offering.

**ātmanam nivedayet** in the context means “one should offer oneself.”

Abhinavagupta then expands it:

**आत्मानमेव निःशेषेण निरुत्तरपदं वेदयेत्**

*ātmānam eva niḥśeṣeṇa niruttarapadaṃ vedayet.*

“One should bring the whole Self, without remainder, into recognition of the unsurpassable state.”

The exact force of *vedayet* includes making known or causing to apprehend; the philosophical interpretation concerns complete self-offering into the supreme reality.

**niḥśeṣeṇa** means without remainder.

Nothing held back.

No separate little ego preserved as an independent possessor of enlightenment.

No private metaphysical estate reserved outside Śiva's consciousness.

The whole restricted subject is recognised through the supreme ground.

Now the interpretation becomes extremely sharp.

A person may say:

“I am willing to offer my possessions.”

Fine.

But the person still imagines themselves as an independent owner.

They may say:

“I am willing to offer my actions.”

Fine.

But they may still imagine themselves as an independent doer.

They may say:

“I am willing to offer my thoughts.”

Fine.

But they may still imagine themselves as an independent thinker.

The highest offering concerns the very assumption of independent subjecthood.

It is the recognition that the ultimate reality of the subject is not separate from universal consciousness.

That's what *ātmanivedana* means in this context.

Not self-harm.

Not the destruction of the physical body.

Not the suppression of thought or agency.

The surrender of false metaphysical separateness.

The final object being offered is the imagined independent ownership of experience.

**The ego has brought a gift to the supreme Lord and discovered that the Lord already owns the gift, the hand offering it, and the awareness through which the whole event is known.**

An unusually comprehensive property dispute.

And Śiva wins.


## XIII. The Bhagavadgītā Joins the Ritual: Every Being in the Self, the Self in Every Being

### Gnoli pp. 277-278

Before reaching the discussion of devotion, Abhinavagupta has also invoked the *Bhagavadgītā*.

His quotation is deliberately partial:

**सर्वभूतस्थमात्मानम् …**

*sarvabhūtastham ātmānam …*

“The Self situated in all beings…”

He does not quote the entire verse.

But the source is identifiable as **Bhagavadgītā 6.29**.

The complete verse in the Gītā is:

**सर्वभूतस्थमात्मानं सर्वभूतानि चात्मनि ।**

*sarvabhūtastham ātmānaṃ sarvabhūtāni cātmani.*

**ईक्षते योगयुक्तात्मा सर्वत्र समदर्शनः ॥६.२९॥**

*īkṣate yogayuktātmā sarvatra samadarśanaḥ.*

My translation:

“The person whose self is established in yoga and whose vision is equal everywhere sees the Self abiding in all beings and all beings within the Self.”

The complete verse is independently verified; the *Vivaraṇa* quotes only the beginning and indicates continuation.

This is a perfect quotation for the present argument.

Because the ritual instructions distinguish support and supported.

The seat.

The Goddess seated upon it.

The worshipper.

The object of worship.

But the Gītā speaks of the Self in all beings and all beings in the Self.

It provides an apt parallel for Abhinavagupta's interpretation of the underlying unity.

### Sarvabhūtastham

**sarva** - all.

**bhūta** - being, existent thing, creature, manifestation.

**stha** - situated, standing, abiding.

The compound means abiding in all beings.

### Ātmani

Locative singular of *ātman*.

“In the Self.”

The second half of the initial line reverses the relationship.

The Self is in all beings.

All beings are in the Self.

This is not the relation of two ordinary containers.

If taken spatially, it would be impossible for every creature to contain the whole Self while also being contained by that Self, unless we abandon the ordinary notion of spatial inclusion.

The verse concerns a mode of seeing in which the fundamental unity of reality is recognised.

### Does the Gītā prove Trika metaphysics?

No.

It is an independently existing scriptural text with a complex history of interpretation.

Different Vedānta and yoga traditions read its account of the Self differently.

Abhinavagupta appropriates it within the Śaiva nondual framework.

His own *Gītārthasaṃgraha* interprets the verse through the relation between the knowing subject and the known objects, drawing them into a unified apprehension.

That gives us direct evidence of his interpretive orientation.

The passage therefore illustrates how Abhinavagupta integrates wider Sanskrit religious literature into the Trika metaphysics of consciousness.

He is not merely collecting famous quotations to make the commentary look prestigious.

Each quotation supplies a specific philosophical function.

Somānanda illuminates self-worship through divine power.

The *Mālinīvijayottara* illuminates the inner liṅga.

The Gītā illuminates the unity of the Self and beings.

Utpaladeva illuminates the Lord's freedom to manifest the differentiated relation between worshipper and worshipped.

And now the ritual interpretation reaches the final topic.

**Fire.**

The actual fire ceremony.

The one we supposedly didn't need.

And Abhinavagupta delivers the most outrageous reinterpretation yet.


## XIV. The Real Fire Offering: Feed the Entire Universe into the Blaze of Consciousness

### KSTS p. 267 - Gnoli p. 279

The root verse has concluded:

**एवं यजनमाख्यातमग्निकार्येऽप्ययं विधिः**

*evaṃ yajanam ākhyātam agnikārye 'py ayaṃ vidhiḥ.*

“Thus the worship has been explained; this same procedure also applies to the fire rite.”

In ordinary ritual language, **agni-kārya** denotes work involving fire, particularly a sacrificial or offering procedure.

**agni** - fire.

**kārya** - action, work, what is to be done.

But Abhinavagupta says that the ultimate fire rite has a different meaning.

It concerns the inward consumption of the seeds of conditioned experience in the supreme fire of Bhairava-consciousness.

The prose becomes extraordinarily elaborate.

He describes the supreme light arising through the conjunction of Śiva and the supreme Śakti, blazing with an enormous power of consumption.

The objects of experience become fuel.

The seeds of conditioned tendencies become offerings.

The fire is the supreme conscious radiance.

And the ritual of offering becomes the recognition of everything within that radiance.

### The phrase *vāsanābīja*

**वासनाबीज - vāsanābīja**

The seed of a latent tendency, impression, or conditioning.

**vāsanā** - a latent impression or disposition, related to √*vas* in the sense of perfuming, dwelling, or leaving a trace, depending on the historical and philosophical analysis.

**bīja** - seed.

In Indian philosophical psychology, *vāsanā* often refers to the residual tendencies through which experiences condition subsequent cognition and action.

The relevant point in Abhinavagupta's argument is that conditioned tendencies participate in the limitation of the subject.

They help structure the way experience is habitually apprehended.

The supreme fire is the recognition that dissolves their claim to ultimate independence.

### The cosmic fuel

The commentary employs the idea of **sarvapadārthendhana**.

Every object as fuel.

**sarva** - all.

**padārtha** - thing, object, meaningful entity.

**indhana** - fuel.

The universe becomes the fuel of the supreme conscious blaze.

But again, this is not a literal instruction to destroy objects or burn the world.

It is a metaphysical image.

The objects are recognised as manifestations of consciousness.

Their apparent absolute separation is consumed.

The forms need not physically disappear.

The misrecognition does.

### The ghee becomes bliss

The passage describes a vast flame nourished by the ghee of supreme loving conjunction or attachment in its transformed divine sense.

The term **sneha** is especially beautiful.

It can mean oiliness, unctuousness, affection, or love.

In ritual language, oily substances and clarified butter feed the fire.

In the metaphysical interpretation, the intensity of divine self-relish supplies the figurative ghee.

The pun is not arbitrary.

The semantic range of *sneha* permits a connection between physical unctuousness and affective attachment.

Abhinavagupta uses the image to transform the external offering into an expression of consciousness.

Ordinary fire consumes fuel.

The supreme fire of recognition consumes the appearance of absolute separation.

And the bliss of Śiva-Śakti is the power of that transformation.

### Antardāha - अन्तर्दाह

Inner burning.

From:

**antar** - within.

**dāha** - burning, combustion, from √*dah*, to burn.

Again, this does not imply that the practitioner should physically injure themselves or induce dangerous heat.

The relevant burning is the dissolution of the limited self's misrecognition within supreme conscious luminosity.

The inner fire is a contemplative and metaphysical image.

### The decisive conclusion

Abhinavagupta says:

**अयमेव अग्निकार्ये विधिर्दीक्षापर्यन्तोऽपि नान्यः पृथक्कश्चित्**

*ayam eva agnikārye vidhir dīkṣāparyanto 'pi nānyaḥ pṛthak kaścit.*

“This alone is the procedure in the fire rite, extending even to initiation; there is no separate other one.”

This is the culmination.

Now we finally understand why he explained the ritual after declaring that the highest initiation does not require external fire offerings.

Because the ultimate meaning of the ritual is not separate from recognition.

The external rite can be understood as an expression of the same principle.

The limited worshipper offers substances into fire.

The practitioner established in nondual recognition apprehends the entire process as arising within the supreme consciousness.

The divine fire is not somewhere else.

The offering is not an independently existing object outside awareness.

The one who offers is not a second ultimate subject.

The whole event is the self-manifestation of Bhairava.

And this is why Abhinavagupta can say that the supreme Heart encompasses worship, initiation, mantra, and action.

The point is not that external ritual never occurs.

It is that its highest reality is found in the conscious ground, not in the external materials considered as independent entities.

### The metaphysical distinction

We must distinguish three claims.

The first is ritual: a particular ceremony involves offerings into a physical fire.

The second is contemplative: the ceremony can symbolise the dissolution of conditioned experience into awareness.

The third is metaphysical: all manifestation is ultimately non-separate from the supreme consciousness.

Abhinavagupta connects them.

But they are not interchangeable propositions.

The physical fire does not become identical with the absolute in every empirical respect.

The offering remains a physical event.

The ritual has its own structure.

The philosophical transformation concerns the ultimate status of the entire event.

This is how he can preserve both the ritual and nonduality.

The ritual is meaningful.

But the ritual is not a second ultimate reality independent of Śiva.

And now, after all this, Abhinavagupta expresses the entire conclusion in one verse.


## XV. The Great Summary: Knowledge of One's Nature Is the Real Mantra

### KSTS p. 268 - Gnoli p. 279

The concluding verse inside Abhinavagupta's commentary reads in the parallel transcription:

**स्वस्वरूपपरिज्ञानं मन्त्रोऽयं पारमार्थिकः ।**

*svasvarūpaparijñānaṃ mantro 'yaṃ pāramārthikaḥ.*

**दीक्षेयमेष यागश्च क्रियेयमप्यनुत्तरः ॥**

*dīkṣeyam eṣa yāgaś ca kriyeyam apy anuttaraḥ.*

My translation:

“Complete recognition of one's own nature is this ultimate mantra. This is initiation; this is worship; this is action itself, unsurpassable.”

The KSTS electronic text differs in the second half, giving **yogaś ca** in place of **yāgaś ca**, and a different wording involving *kriyāyām*. The parallel transcription's *yāga* reading fits the immediately preceding discussion of worship and fire ritual, but the KSTS reading must be recorded rather than silently erased. Both constructions can satisfy the eight-syllable metrical pattern; metre alone cannot settle the difference. ; \]

This is the intellectual culmination of everything.

### Svāsvarūpa? No. Svasvarūpa.

The compound is:

**स्वस्वरूप - svasvarūpa**

One's own nature.

**sva** - one's own.

**svarūpa** - own form, intrinsic nature.

Then:

**परिज्ञान - parijñāna**

Thorough knowledge or recognition.

The whole compound means complete knowledge of one's own true nature.

It does not mean merely identifying the empirical personality.

Knowing your favourite foods, memories, habits, and preferences is not the supreme *svasvarūpaparijñāna* of this passage.

The nature being recognised is the supreme conscious ground.

The reality of Śiva-consciousness.

### Pāramārthikaḥ

**पारमार्थिक - pāramārthika**

Ultimately real, belonging to the highest truth.

The adjective derives from *paramārtha*, supreme or ultimate meaning, reality, truth.

The masculine nominative singular agrees with **mantraḥ**, understood through the sandhi *mantro 'yam*.

Thus:

“This is the ultimate mantra.”

Not merely another sequence of phonemes.

The true mantra is the complete recognition of one's intrinsic nature.

This is why the entire *Parātrīśikāvivaraṇa* has been so obsessed with the difference between sound and its supreme conscious power.

The mantra's audible or written form is not identical with the full metaphysical reality it expresses.

The ultimate significance is self-recognition.

### Dīkṣā, yāga, kriyā

The concluding verse then equates that recognition with:

**dīkṣā** - initiation.

**yāga** - worship or sacrificial offering.

**kriyā** - action.

These are not simply random religious synonyms.

They are the central functions of the ritual system just explained.

Initiation transforms the disciple's spiritual condition.

Worship establishes relation with the deity.

Action performs the necessary operations.

But in the supreme interpretation all three are grounded in recognition.

The initiation is knowledge of the true Self.

The worship is recognition of the divine reality manifesting as worshipper and worshipped.

The action is the expression of consciousness's freedom.

The three are not separate ultimate realities.

And that is why the text calls the result **anuttara**, unsurpassable.

### Is this Abhinavagupta's own verse?

The verse occurs in the concluding prose of the commentary without an explicit attribution to a separate named scripture at this point.

It functions as Abhinavagupta's summative formulation.

We should not attach a fictitious root-tantra verse number to it.

It is not one of the numbered verses 26-32 of the *Parātriṃśikā*.

Nor should it be automatically attributed to Somānanda merely because Somānanda is mentioned shortly afterward.

The quotation boundary matters.

### What about Somānanda's ritual instructions?

Immediately afterward, Abhinavagupta refers to **Śrī Somānandapāda** and the consecration of the sacrificial ladles, the *sruk* and *sruva*.

These are instruments used in fire offerings.

He invokes Somānanda to show that differentiated ritual distinctions can be understood without disrupting the supreme unity.

The KSTS editor inserts an explanatory bracket describing the two ladles as forms of Śiva and Śakti arranged facing one another:

**$$शिवशक्तिरूपौ हि स्रुक्स्रुवौ ...$$**

That bracket is editorial.

The running prose attributes a treatment of the ritual implements to Somānanda, but the explanatory identification within square brackets must not be promoted into a verbatim quotation from Abhinavagupta.

The distinction is essential because this passage is already crowded with different levels of textual authority.

We have:

The root tantra.

Abhinavagupta's commentary.

Somānanda's independent philosophical work.

The *Mālinīvijayottara*.

The *Bhagavadgītā*.

The *Īśvarapratyabhijñā*.

And the later KSTS editor.

They cannot all be made to speak in the same undifferentiated voice.

### The final philosophical point

Abhinavagupta does not regard the ritual distinctions as absolutely independent realities.

He also does not say that they can never be practised.

He interprets their highest meaning through the undivided Heart.

The ritual can be differentiated without making consciousness itself divided.

That is the great synthesis.

**The supreme reality is not attained by escaping manifestation. It is recognised as the ground through which manifestation, worship, and action are possible.**

And now we are ready for the next genuine transition.

But before that, one last metrical audit.


## XVI. The Metrical Audit of Verses 26-32

All seven KSTS-numbered verses are in the anuṣṭubh family, with four pādas of eight syllables.

Likewise the alternative *sarvāvayavaśobhitām* in verse 30, the *mahābhāgā* reading in verse 31, and the competing conjunctions preserve eight-syllable pādas.

This is why a metrically correct variant is not automatically the earliest or most authoritative one.

Metre can exclude impossible readings.

It cannot always select between two possible ones.

And in this passage several important textual alternatives are metrically compatible.

The surviving textual evidence, not our preferred interpretation, must decide where possible.


## XVII. The Next Authentic Transition: What Actually Happens When Worship Is Performed This Way?

### KSTS p. 269 - Gnoli p. 279

At this point Abhinavagupta makes the transition explicit:

**किमेवमुपासायां भवति? इत्यवतरति ।**

*kim evam upāsāyāṃ bhavati? ity avatarati.*

“What happens through worship in this manner? Thus the next passage is introduced.”

There it is.

A clean transition.

The explanation of verses 26-32 has concluded.

Abhinavagupta has examined the ritual, interpreted it through the Heart, identified supreme recognition with mantra, initiation, worship, and action, and now turns to its result.

The next root line begins:

**कृतपूजाविधिः सम्यक्स्मरन् बीजं प्रसिद्ध्यति ।**

*kṛtapūjāvidhiḥ samyak smaran bījaṃ prasiddhyati.*

“One who has performed the method of worship, properly remembering the seed, attains accomplishment.”

In the independent root edition this is part of verse 33, while the commentary's presentation treats it as the next textual unit. It is followed by the instructions concerning the beginningless and endless seed, the heart-lotus, the lunar portion, repeated practice, desired results, and the ultimate attainment of knowledge. ; \]

And the commentary's next move is especially interesting.

It distinguishes the practitioner who continuously remembers the Heart even during ordinary activity from the practitioner who follows the sequential ritual and gradually acquires its mantric potency.

In other words:

**Spontaneous recognition versus practice-dependent attainment.**

Again.

The very distinction that has organised the entire final movement of the work.

The practitioner established in recognition does not simply obtain an isolated ceremonial result.

Their ordinary activity itself becomes a manifestation of worship.

But the person who has not yet attained such recognition may still engage the successive procedure as a means of cultivating it.

This is the next argument.

And it is an excellent place to stop.


## Closing: The Universe Was Never Waiting for You to Put It on an Altar

Let's return to the beginning of the instalment.

The practitioner stands before a ritual altar.

They place the mantras upon the body.

The head.

The face.

The heart.

The secret region.

The bodily form.

They invoke the five Brahmas.

They bind the *śikhā*.

Twenty-seven repetitions.

They seal the ten directions.

Three claps.

They sprinkle the water.

They prepare the flowers.

They arrange the seat.

They invoke the Goddess.

They worship the creation.

They enclose the creation within the Heart.

They offer fragrant flowers.

Then they offer themselves.

And finally they perform the fire rite.

A complex ritual procedure.

Perfectly intelligible within a tantric religious system.

But Abhinavagupta has explained it from an entirely different standpoint.

The five bodily locations are manifestations of the powers of consciousness.

The twenty-seven repetitions express the differentiated structure of the Śaktis.

The ten directions are understood through the relative manifestation of spatial orientation.

The three claps express the pacification of divisive misrecognition.

The water represents the fluid and unrestricted nature of the Heart.

The flower expresses the unfolding of manifestation.

The liṅga is the sign of the reality in which the moving and unmoving world is absorbed.

The seat becomes the relation between support and supported, grounded in the same supreme freedom.

The fourteenfold repetition invokes the central vowel *au*, the trident-like power of the Heart-mantra.

The creation is enclosed within consciousness.

The Goddess is understood as the totality of the tattvas.

The offering is recognised as a movement within Śakti.

And finally the finite subject offers the very assumption of independent separateness.

This is the higher meaning of worship.

Not a collection of unrelated religious tasks.

A comprehensive recognition of the nature of manifestation.

### Why this is not merely symbolic poetry

The argument is not just a set of pretty comparisons.

It belongs to a rigorous metaphysical system.

If the supreme reality is absolutely nondual consciousness, then no differentiated activity can ultimately occur outside it.

The ritual must therefore be a manifestation of the same consciousness.

The worshipper cannot be an independent substance.

The deity cannot be an ultimate second substance.

The offering cannot be outside the field of manifestation.

The act cannot be foreign to divine freedom.

Therefore the ritual's ultimate reality must be found in the conscious power through which all its components appear.

Abhinavagupta applies that principle systematically.

Not by denying that the ritual has differentiated steps.

But by denying that the steps are ultimately separate from the Heart.

This is why the same text can affirm direct liberation without ritual and still explain ritual with extraordinary care.

The ritual is not the creator of the supreme Self.

But it can be an expression of the recognition of that Self.

### The paradox of devotion

Perhaps the most beautiful part of this passage is the interpretation of *bhakti*.

Because devotion appears to require two.

The devotee.

And the beloved divine reality.

The devotee bows.

The divine receives the offering.

But if the supreme reality is one, what is the meaning of this separation?

Abhinavagupta's answer is not simply to abolish the relationship.

He explains that consciousness freely manifests differentiation.

It can appear as the worshipper.

It can appear as the worshipped.

It can appear as the act of worship.

And its reality is not divided by those appearances.

Thus devotion can remain meaningful.

Not because the supreme Self has become genuinely alien to itself.

But because its freedom includes the manifestation of relationship.

This is where **svātantrya** becomes absolutely central.

The supreme consciousness is not forced into differentiation by something outside itself.

Its freedom is the ground of differentiation.

The same freedom allows recognition of the underlying unity.

And the highest worship is the conscious participation in that recognition.

### The fire that consumes the false division

The external fire consumes an offering.

But the supreme fire of Bhairava-consciousness consumes the assumption that the offering, the one who offers, and the reality to which the offering is made are absolutely separate.

The fire does not need to destroy the world.

It destroys the misrecognition of the world's ultimate nature.

And that is the deepest continuity between Part XXXII and Part XXXIII.

In Part XXXII, we learned that the whole universe is present within the seed of the Heart.

In Part XXXIII, we learned that every action of worship occurs within that same Heart.

The world is not merely something that appears and then must be escaped.

It is the manifestation through which consciousness can recognise its own freedom.

And the ritual is one possible expression of that recognition.

### The final joke

Imagine a person performing this elaborate ritual perfectly.

Every syllable pronounced correctly.

Every placement exact.

Every flower fresh.

Every direction sealed.

Every clap sufficiently audible.

Every repetition counted.

Every gesture correctly executed.

The practitioner reaches the end.

They offer themselves to the supreme Lord.

And Bhairava asks:

“What exactly are you offering?”

“Myself, Lord.”

“Very good. And who are you?”

“A limited person seeking the divine.”

“Are you certain?”

“Well, I've completed the ritual.”

“Yes, excellent. The flowers were lovely. But who was aware of the ritual?”

“I was.”

“And what is the nature of that awareness?”

The practitioner hesitates.

And there, right there, the actual teaching begins.

Because the purpose was never simply to move objects around an altar.

The purpose was to recognise the reality in which the whole ceremony occurred.

The movements.

The sounds.

The intentions.

The sensations.

The image of the Goddess.

The sense of being a worshipper.

The act of offering.

All manifest within awareness.

And Abhinavagupta asks whether that awareness, in its supreme nature, was ever fundamentally separate from Bhairava.

His answer is no.

The worshipper was seeking Śiva through the power of Śiva.

The ritual was an expression of Śakti.

The offering belonged to consciousness.

And the Heart was never absent from the beginning.

**The real offering was the imagined distance between the worshipper and the worshipped.**

And once that distance is recognised as nonultimate, the entire universe becomes intelligible as the self-manifestation of the supreme.

The ceremony is finished.

The fire has consumed its fuel.

The flowers remain on the altar.

The body is still present.

The senses still operate.

Thought may continue.

The world has not disappeared.

But the assumption of absolute separation no longer needs to govern the understanding of experience.

That is the supreme interpretation of ritual.

And the next question is now unavoidable.

**What happens when somebody remembers this even while doing ordinary things?**

Walking.

Eating.

Speaking.

Working.

Thinking.

Breathing.

Does the whole of life become worship?

And if it does, what remains of the distinction between sacred practice and ordinary existence?

That is where Abhinavagupta is taking us next.
